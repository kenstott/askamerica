Pacific and Southwest/Southeast drought & storm belts run the worst crop-insurance loss ratios; concentration is flat, not trending

USDA RMA Summary of Business, indemnity/premium by state, 2016-2025

Summary

Over 2016-2025, the worst federal crop-insurance loss ratios (indemnities paid divided by premium collected) were run by a cluster of drought- and storm-exposed states rather than any single region: Washington (1.45), Florida (1.36), Texas (1.32), Arkansas (1.32), Utah (1.28), California (1.28), New Mexico (1.24), Arizona (1.22), Georgia (1.15), and Louisiana (1.14) all paid out more in indemnities than farmers and taxpayers paid in premium. These fall into two distinct exposure groups: the arid Southwest/Mountain West (UT, NM, AZ, plus CA and parts of TX) driven by chronic and episodic drought, and the Gulf/Southeast/Pacific tier (FL, GA, LA, WA) driven by hurricanes, excess moisture, and orchard-specific perils. By contrast, the Corn Belt (Indiana 0.31, Illinois 0.31, Iowa 0.44, Wisconsin 0.45, Ohio 0.50, Missouri 0.55, Michigan 0.60) ran comfortably profitable for the program the whole decade.

On concentration: state-level indemnity shares show no statistically meaningful trend toward either more or less concentration over the decade. A Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI), computed with the hhi_concentration tool on each year's state shares of national crop-insurance indemnities, swings between 496 (2016) and 969 (2022) with no consistent direction (regression of HHI on year: slope +7.6/year, r²=0.02, correlation r=0.15 - not distinguishable from noise). Every single year falls in the DOJ/FTC 'unconcentrated' band (HHI<1000). The swings track which specific region got hit by a major drought or storm in a given year (e.g., the 2022 spike is the Texas/Kansas/Southern Plains drought concentrating over $4.6B of that year's indemnities into Texas alone, 23.9% of the national total that year), not a structural drift toward fewer or more states dominating losses. 2024-2025 concentration (548, 520) is back near where the decade started (496, 549 in 2016-2017).

Texas and California carry the largest absolute dollar volumes of any loss-making state ($18.8B and $7.2B indemnity respectively over the decade) - so in dollar terms, the 'worst region' story is really a Texas story reinforced by a Southwest-drought and Gulf-hurricane story. By contrast, the Corn Belt states (IN, IL, IA, WI, OH, MO, MI) all ran loss ratios well under 0.6, meaning the program's Midwest grain book has been subsidizing the payouts concentrated in the drought/hurricane-exposed states.

External corroboration: USDA's own reporting on Pasture, Rangeland and Forage (PRF) insurance - a drought-specific crop-insurance line - found the Southwest region received about 40% of national PRF indemnities from 2017-2023, with Arizona alone receiving 12.3% and Nevada 8.7% of all PRF payments nationwide, consistent with the Southwest states' elevated loss ratios found here.

Every year falls in the DOJ/FTC 'unconcentrated' band (HHI<1000) throughout the decade - crop-insurance losses have never been dominated by a handful of states the way, say, a concentrated industry would be. A regression of HHI on year across these 10 points gives a slope of +7.6 HHI points/year with r²=0.024 and correlation r=0.15 - far too weak to call a trend. The apparent 2018-2022 climb and 2023-2025 decline is better explained by which specific disaster hit which state in a given year (2022's spike to 969 is almost entirely the Southern Plains/Texas-Kansas drought, which alone drove Texas to 23.9% of that year's national indemnities) than by any structural shift toward payouts concentrating in fewer places or spreading across more. By 2024-2025 concentration is back down near 2016-2017 levels. The honest answer is: neither - concentration is episodic and disaster-driven, not trending in either direction over the decade.
