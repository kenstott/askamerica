Corpus data gap prevents a direct answer; a proxy analysis puts high-seismic-county housing stock at a median build year of 1974, six years before California's 1980 seismic-code benchmark

census.acs_housing_tenure (works), 2023 ACS 5-Year — disasters.fema_nri_earthquake_by_county and census.acs_housing_year_built_detail (the two tables built for this exact question) returned S3 404s all session

Summary

The two AskAmerica tables built to answer this question directly — FEMA's county earthquake hazard ratings and the Census decade-of-construction brackets — were both unreachable this session (persistent S3 'key does not exist' errors, confirmed after a schema rebuild and logged as a data-quality issue). A precise, corpus-native 'percent of housing units built before the applicable seismic code, in FEMA-rated high-earthquake-risk counties' therefore cannot be computed today.

As a substitute, this report uses the ACS median-year-built figure (which does work) for 14 counties squarely inside the zones USGS's 2023 National Seismic Hazard Model names as the nation's highest earthquake hazard — California's San Andreas corridor, the Cascadia-exposed Pacific Northwest, Utah's Wasatch Front, the New Madrid seismic zone (Memphis), Charleston SC, and Alaska. Their population-weighted median year built is 1974, versus 1977 across all U.S. counties — modestly older, and several of the largest urban cores are far older: San Francisco (1945), Los Angeles (1965), and Alameda County (1969) all predate 1980, the year most commonly cited as when California adopted statewide seismic requirements for wood-frame construction. Published research (California Legislature, FEMA/CRS) corroborates that a large share of this older stock — soft-story apartment buildings, raised-foundation single-family homes, unreinforced masonry — remains seismically under-protected, but no single national percentage of 'housing predating modern codes in high-risk counties' could be verified from a primary source in the time available.

What broke, and what it means

disasters.fema_nri_earthquake_by_county (FEMA National Risk Index earthquake hazard/exposure by county) and census.acs_housing_year_built_detail (ACS table B25034, ten construction-decade brackets by county) are exactly the two tables this question needs — one to define 'high-seismic-risk county,' the other to compute the 'predates modern codes' share directly. Both returned The specified key does not exist. (Service: S3, Status Code: 404) on every query attempted, including a plain SELECT * ... LIMIT 3, after a full schema-catalog rebuild (update_schema) and a fresh reconnect. This is a genuine storage-layer gap, not a SQL error, and was reported via report_issue.

No sibling earthquake-hazard table exists elsewhere in the corpus (confirmed by search_catalog) — 'seismic' is an unmatched catalog term. census.acs_housing_tenure, which carries a single median_year_built figure (not decade brackets) by county, was the only working housing-vintage source at county grain.

Population-weighted (by occupied units) average median year built across these 14 counties: 1973.9, versus a simple average of 1977 across all 3,222 U.S. counties carrying this ACS field. The oldest, densest urban cores in the highest-hazard zones (San Francisco, Los Angeles, Alameda) sit well before 1980; the newer Sun Belt/Interior West suburban counties (Riverside, Charleston, Salt Lake, Pierce) sit at or after it — meaning the age gap is concentrated in specific metro cores, not spread evenly across every high-hazard county.

Caveat on this proxy: median year built tells you the 50th-percentile vintage of a county's stock, not the share below any one threshold — a county could have a 1975 median and still have 30% or 70% of units built before 1980 depending on the shape of its distribution. The ACS bracket table that would answer that directly (acs_housing_year_built_detail) is the one that is down. This is a directional indicator, not the percentage the question asks for.

What published research adds

1980 is the year most consistently cited as the threshold for 'pre-modern seismic code' in California, the state carrying the largest share of the nation's high-seismic housing stock: California adopted statewide seismic building-code requirements for wood-frame construction in 1980, and homes built before that date commonly have unbraced raised (crawlspace) foundations that require retrofit (California Legislature Joint Committee on Emergency Management, 2024 seismic-resilience backgrounder).

That same backgrounder cites the Structural Engineers Association of California and California Seismic Safety Commission estimate of up to 100,000 soft-story apartment buildings statewide, and the U.S. Resiliency Council's estimate that as many as 2.5 million Californians live in older soft-story multifamily housing at elevated collapse risk in an earthquake. Los Angeles identified roughly 8,000 unreinforced-masonry buildings after a 1981 ordinance (nearly all retrofitted or demolished by 2015); San Francisco passed its own unreinforced-masonry ordinance after the 1989 Loma Prieta earthquake (95% retrofitted or demolished by 2014, per the Structural Engineers Association of Northern California).

At the national level, a Congressional Research Service report (FEMA's Building Codes Policies and Considerations for Congress, R47612, Oct. 2023) states that nearly one-third of the U.S. housing stock is considered at high risk of a natural disaster (citing CoreLogic, 2021 — an all-hazard figure, not seismic-specific), and that FEMA gave 35 states its lowest rating for hazard-resistant building-code adoption as of its 2023 tracking review. Neither figure isolates seismic risk or housing vintage specifically, so they corroborate the general pattern (much of the at-risk stock predates current codes, and code adoption/enforcement is uneven) without supplying the precise number this question asks for.

What This Report Does Not Answer

  • Define / identify high-seismic-risk counties: The corpus's only earthquake-hazard table (disasters.fema_nri_earthquake_by_county) was unreachable all session (S3 404s). Substituted a name-based county list drawn from USGS's 2023 National Seismic Hazard Model's own description of where hazard concentrates, rather than an objective in-corpus ranking.
  • Quantify 'predates modern building codes' as a share of housing stock: The corpus's decade-of-construction bracket table (census.acs_housing_year_built_detail), which would give a direct 'share built before 1980' by county, was unreachable all session (S3 404s). Substituted median_year_built (a 50th-percentile statistic, not a share) from census.acs_housing_tenure, which does work, plus qualitative/quantitative figures from published California and federal research. No single verified national percentage answering the question as asked could be produced.
