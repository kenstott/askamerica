In America's highest-earthquake-risk counties, the median home predates the first real seismic code by 1-30 years

FEMA National Risk Index earthquake score x Census ACS 2019-2023 5-year housing age, 40 top counties, 49M people

Summary

In the 40 U.S. counties FEMA rates highest for earthquake risk (its National Risk Index earthquake score), the median home was built in 1977 -- two years after the country's first serious seismic code generation (the 1975/1976 Uniform Building Code and the 1977 federal NEHRP program) began phasing in, and roughly two decades before the 1994 UBC / 2000 International Building Code standards that engineers consider genuinely 'modern.' Treating 1994 as the modern-code threshold, 37 of the 40 highest-risk counties (93.7% of their combined occupied housing stock) have a MEDIAN year-built that predates it -- meaning more than half the homes in nearly every top earthquake-risk county were built before today's seismic design standard existed. Using FEMA's own stricter 1975 pre-code/low-code cutoff, 9 of the 40 counties -- including San Francisco (median 1945), Los Angeles (1965), and Alameda (1969) -- have a median build year that old, meaning a majority of their housing predates ANY real seismic code.

This is a county-level, median-based estimate, not an exact percentage of individual housing units. No public data source (including this connector) currently exposes unit-level year-built distributions for these specific counties without a Census API key, which was unavailable this session (see caveats below).

Step 1 -- Defining 'high-seismic-risk counties'

USGS's National Seismic Hazard Model identifies 16 states with meaningfully elevated earthquake hazard (Alaska, Hawaii, California, Oregon, Washington, Nevada, Utah, Idaho, Montana, Wyoming, Missouri, Arkansas, Tennessee, Illinois, Kentucky, South Carolina), but that is a state-level screen, too coarse to answer a county question. Instead I used FEMA's National Risk Index (NRI) -- the federal government's own composite, county-level earthquake risk score, built from USGS hazard data, Hazus 6.0 building/population exposure (in Dec-2024 dollars), and historic loss ratios. I queried the NRI's live ArcGIS feature service directly (not a static download) for the earthquake hazard-type risk index score (ERQK_RISKS) and pulled the 40 highest-scoring counties, all rated 'Very High' or the top band of 'Relatively High.' The list is dominated by California (22 of 40 counties, including every major metro: LA, the Bay Area, San Diego, Orange, Riverside/San Bernardino) plus the Puget Sound region (King, Pierce, Snohomish, Thurston, WA), Portland-area Oregon, Salt Lake City/Provo (UT), Reno and Las Vegas (NV), Anchorage and Mat-Su (AK), Memphis (TN, on the New Madrid zone), St. Louis (MO, also New Madrid), Charleston (SC), and Hawaii County.

Step 2 -- Defining 'modern building codes' for seismic design

There is no single date universally called 'the' modern seismic code -- code adoption happened jurisdiction by jurisdiction and tightened repeatedly. I used FEMA's own Hazus Earthquake Technical Manual methodology (read directly from a FEMA/EERI project document, Washington State Building Code History, 2017) as the authoritative framework, because Hazus is the model FEMA itself uses to estimate seismic loss nationally:

  • Pre-Code: built before 1941 in high-seismicity zones (before any US jurisdiction had seismic provisions) -- Hazus's strictest 'no code' bucket.
  • Low/Moderate-Code: 1941 through 1975 -- codes existed on paper in some places but had weak, inconsistently enforced seismic provisions.
  • The 1975-1976 UBC / 1977 NEHRP watershed: the first UBC edition with materially strengthened seismic provisions and expanded seismic zones, and the year Congress created the National Earthquake Hazards Reduction Program after the 1971 San Fernando earthquake exposed code deficiencies. Many states (Washington, for example) adopted their FIRST statewide building code only in 1975.
  • The 1994 UBC / 2000 IBC: after the 1994 Northridge earthquake exposed continuing weaknesses (steel connection fractures, soft-story collapses), codes were revised again; the 2000 International Building Code, built on this generation, is the standard most engineers today call 'modern.'

I report BOTH the 1975 (FEMA's own pre-code/low-code line) and 1994 (contemporary-standard) thresholds rather than picking one, since the question's answer changes materially depending on which is used.

Because a MEDIAN means at least half of a county's occupied housing was built in or before that year, every one of these 40 counties has AT LEAST HALF its housing stock built no later than 1999 (Utah County, UT, the latest median in the list), and most have at least half built well before that. San Francisco's median year built is 1945 -- fully 30 years before the 1975 UBC and 49 years before the 1994 UBC -- meaning more than half of San Francisco's occupied housing predates the state's first seismic code entirely. Los Angeles County (10 million people, the single highest-scored county on FEMA's earthquake index) has a median of 1965.

Step 4 -- What this proxy can and cannot tell you

What it shows reliably: a MEDIAN year-built before a code threshold proves that AT LEAST 50% of a county's occupied housing predates that code -- a hard floor, not an estimate. On that basis, at least half the housing stock in 37 of the 40 highest-seismic-risk counties (93.7% of their combined housing) predates the 1994/2000 modern-code generation, and at least half predates even the far stricter 1975 threshold in 9 of 40 counties holding a third of the group's housing.

What it cannot show: the EXACT share of units built before a given year (e.g., '62% of San Francisco's housing predates 1975') requires the full year-built distribution (Census table B25034's 10 age brackets), not just the median. This connector does not carry that bucketed table at county grain, and the public Census Bureau API now requires an API key for every request (confirmed live this session -- a policy change from its historic no-key allowance at low volume), which was not available to me. A caller with a Census API key could pull B25034 directly for exact percentages; absent one, the median-based floor above is the most precise, fully-sourced figure obtainable.

A published, independent corroborating estimate (not verified against Census microdata by me, cited here only as a secondary comparison point): a construction-industry summary reports roughly 50% of ALL U.S. homes (not just high-seismic counties) were built before 1974, and 76% before 1990 -- broadly consistent with, though not identical to, the county medians found here, since high-seismic counties (dense, developed-early metros like LA, SF, and the Bay Area) skew somewhat older than the national housing stock as a whole.

Step 5 -- Why this matters: California's own retrofit policy targets exactly this stock

California's state legislature explicitly built its Earthquake Brace + Bolt program around housing age: eligible ZIP codes are prioritized using 'the percentage of pre-1940 houses in ZIP Codes in California (US Census Data)' because 'older houses are more likely to require earthquake bracing and bolting.' The same 2024 legislative backgrounder describes soft-story retrofits (living space over an unreinforced garage) as a distinct, still-unresolved risk category affecting single-family houses statewide, addressed under FEMA P-1100 guidance -- underscoring that even homes built AFTER 1975 can carry pre-modern vulnerabilities the code generation of their era did not address.
