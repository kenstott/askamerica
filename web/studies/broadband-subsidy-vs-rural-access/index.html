Federally funded rural areas gained broadband faster than the nation overall — but a large share of "funded" addresses were never actually served

CAF-II / RDOF era, roughly 2015-2023. AskAmerica ACS county data + peer-reviewed program evaluations.

Summary

Rural broadband access improved substantially during the years federal subsidy money flowed (Connect America Fund Phase II, 2015-2021, and the Rural Digital Opportunity Fund, 2020 onward) — but the improvement is not cleanly attributable to "funded vs. unfunded" places, because a large share of addresses that received federal buildout money were never actually verified as receiving service. Nationally, the share of nonmetro (rural) households subscribing to broadband rose from about 68.7% to 83.8% between the 2017 and 2023 American Community Survey 5-year estimates (+15.1 points), narrowing but not closing the gap with metro counties, which rose from 79.4% to 90.5% (+11.1 points). A peer-reviewed quasi-experimental study of the CAF-II rollout (Nazareno, Jose & Hart, 2025, Telecommunications Policy) found that census tracts that received CAF-II deployment saw measurably higher employment and household income growth than untreated tracts, with the effect strengthening over time — real evidence the subsidized deployment did something. At the same time, an independent field study (UC Santa Barbara/UC Berkeley/Ookla, SIGCOMM 2024) that actually queried CAF-funded addresses found only about 55% overall serviceability — i.e., more than 40% of addresses ISPs certified as "served" under CAF were not, in fact, providing service when checked — and only about a third met the FCC's required 10/1 Mbps minimum. So the honest answer has two parts: places that got federal broadband money and were actually built out did see real, sizable gains relative to places with no program at all; but "got federal money" and "got improved access" overlap only partially, because self-certified deployment data overstated real service by a wide margin.

What the literature establishes

  • National rural progress over the funding era: Per FCC Broadband Deployment/Progress Reports, the share of rural Americans without access to fixed 25/3 Mbps service fell from "over half" in 2014 to about 22.3% unserved (77.7% covered) by 2018 — a period spanning CAF-II's main build years. Pew Research similarly found the share of rural adults without a home broadband subscription fell from 39% (2013) to 22% (2020), a 17-point gain (see sources).
  • A real causal estimate exists for CAF-II specifically: Nazareno, Jose & Hart (2025) built a census-tract panel, 2010-2023, and used CAF-II's staggered rollout across tracts as a quasi-experiment. Tracts that received CAF-II-funded deployment showed statistically positive effects on employment and household income, growing stronger in years after deployment, versus tracts that did not receive the program. They found no significant effect on self-employment specifically.
  • But funding did not reliably translate into actual service: Srinivasavaradhan, Koduru, Belding, Gupta, Narechania, Zhang & Zhou (SIGCOMM 2024 / UCSB-Berkeley-Ookla) independently queried addresses that AT&T, CenturyLink/Lumen, Frontier and Consolidated Communications had certified as "served" under $10B+ in CAF funding. They found roughly 55% average serviceability (as low as 32% for AT&T, the CAF recipient that certified the most addresses), and only ~33% compliance with the FCC's mandated 10 Mbps down/1 Mbps up minimum. Over 40% of CAF-funded addresses appeared effectively unserved despite the subsidy and the certification.
  • Where CAF service did materialize, the gain was often real: the same study found that in cases where the CAF-subsidized plan differed from what was offered at nearby non-subsidized addresses on the same network, the subsidized plan was usually better, by a median of roughly 75% higher speed — i.e., the program-rule protection worked when it was actually enforced, it just was not consistently enforced.

What we computed directly from AskAmerica data, and its limits

AskAmerica's census schema carries household broadband-subscription counts by county and year (census.acs_internet, ACS 5-year, 2017-2023) and USDA's static rural-urban classification (geo.rural_urban_continuum). Joining these lets us compute a national before/after broadband-share trend split by metro vs. nonmetro county status, shown in the dashboard above: nonmetro household broadband share rose 15.1 points (68.7% → 83.8%) versus 11.1 points for metro counties (79.4% → 90.5%) across the two ACS vintages that bracket most of the CAF-II/RDOF buildout period.

This is descriptive context, not the funded-vs-unfunded comparison the question asks for. AskAmerica's connector does not carry a table of which specific counties, census blocks, or addresses actually received CAF, RDOF, or BEAD dollars (no USAC HUBB deployment data or FCC National Broadband Map ingestion exists in this corpus; fiscal.usaspending_by_county only carries all-award-type federal obligations by county, with no CFDA/program breakout that could isolate broadband funding specifically). Nonmetro-vs-metro is a reasonable proxy for "broadly targeted by these programs" vs. "not the target population," but it is not a clean treatment/control split at the level the literature above achieves with its tract-level, program-rollout design. The rigorous funded-vs-not comparison in this answer therefore comes from the peer-reviewed quasi-experimental study cited above, not from a recomputation against this connector's tables.

What This Report Does Not Answer

  • Compare against places that received no federal broadband money: AskAmerica's corpus has no county/address-level table identifying which specific places received CAF/RDOF/BEAD funds, so a direct funded-vs-unfunded comparison could not be computed from this connector's data. The metro-vs-nonmetro split shown is a proxy (broadly-targeted vs. not), not a true treatment/control comparison; the genuine funded-vs-control comparison is reported from the cited peer-reviewed tract-panel study instead of recomputed here.
