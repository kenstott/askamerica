Bridge Condition Improved Only Modestly Since the Infrastructure Law — and the Pace of Improvement Slowed, Not Accelerated

FHWA National Bridge Inventory (transport.bridges), NBI data years 2013-2025; ARTBA and ASCE analyses of the same source

Summary

America's bridges have kept getting slightly better since the Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act (IIJA, signed November 2021) — but the pace of improvement has slowed, not accelerated, and most of the record bridge funding the law authorized has not yet worked its way into finished repairs. Using FHWA's own National Bridge Inventory (NBI) on a consistent, count-basis definition, the share of U.S. bridges rated Poor fell from 7.01% in 2021 to 6.65% in 2025 (43,294 of 617,179 bridges vs. 41,328 of 621,664) — a real but modest decline of about 0.36 percentage points over four years. In the four years immediately before the law (2019-2021, on the same post-2018 rating definition), the share fell from 7.47% to 7.01%, a 0.46-point drop in two years — meaning the annual rate of decline was roughly twice as fast before IIJA (about -0.22 pts/yr) as after it (about -0.09 pts/yr). Popular before/after comparisons that reach back a full decade (citing ~11-12% structurally deficient around 2011 vs. ~7% today) are comparing two different measurement definitions — FHWA narrowed the "structurally deficient/poor" standard in 2018 — so most of that apparent decade-long improvement is a definitional break, not repaired bridges. The clean, like-for-like conclusion: bridges have improved a little since the law, the decline has been going on for years already, and the improvement has decelerated in the immediate post-IIJA period even as it continues.

Step 1 — What "before the law" and "since the law" actually measure

The IIJA was signed November 15, 2021. FHWA's NBI is published on roughly a one-year lag and each annual data-year snapshot reflects the average inspection roughly a year earlier, so 2021 data is essentially the pre-IIJA baseline and 2022-2025 data reflects the post-IIJA period. A critical wrinkle for any "decade before" comparison: FHWA redefined "structurally deficient"/"poor" bridges effective January 1, 2018, narrowing the classification to bridges where a key structural element (deck, superstructure, substructure, or culvert) is rated in poor condition or worse, rather than the older sufficiency-rating-based definition. The Congressional Research Service documented that this changed the count immediately: 6,533 bridges that would have been classified as deficient in 2017 fell out of the classification once the new, narrower 2018 definition applied. Consequently:

  • Pre-2018 numbers (structurally deficient shares of roughly 22% in 1992, ~12% around 2011, ~10% in 2014) use the older, broader definition.
  • 2018-onward numbers (the ~7.0-7.5% figures) use the newer, narrower definition.

A literal decade-before comparison (2011-2021) therefore cannot be done cleanly on a single consistent metric — most of the apparent decade-long "improvement" is the redefinition, not repair activity. The analysis below instead compares like-for-like windows using the post-2018 definition on both sides of the law.

The pace of decline roughly halved after the law took effect, despite it authorizing a dedicated $27.5 billion formula bridge program — the largest single federal bridge investment since the construction of the Interstate System, per ARTBA. This is corroborated independently: the Reason Foundation's 27th Annual Highway Report, using the same NBI/Better Roads source, reports 2020 at 45,861/614,490 = 7.46% and 2021 at 43,289/617,008 = 7.02% — matching this analysis to within rounding, and confirming the pre-IIJA data point is not an artifact of this connector's ingestion.

Step 3 — Why the pace slowed instead of accelerating: funding lag

ARTBA's analysis of the same FHWA data explains the mechanism: in the first three years of the IIJA, states received $15.9 billion of the law's $27.5 billion formula bridge program, and by 2024 had obligated only 46% ($7.3 billion) of that to roughly 4,170 specific bridge projects. The remaining 54% of already-released funds, plus $10.6 billion still to be disbursed, has not yet reached construction. There is an inherent multi-year lag between a bridge repair being funded/obligated and a bridge's inspection rating actually changing — design, environmental review, procurement, and construction all take time. The NBI condition data through 2025 therefore reflects a period when most IIJA bridge money was still working through that pipeline, not yet showing up as completed repairs. ASCE's 2025 Infrastructure Report Card puts a number on how far from finished the job is: a $373 billion gap remains for bridge rehabilitation needs over the next decade, even after the IIJA's incremental funding.

Step 4 — Aging is outpacing full repair: the Good-to-Fair migration

Beneath the modest Poor-share decline, the overall condition mix is deteriorating in the middle of the distribution. From 2019 to 2025 nationally: bridges rated Good fell from 279,555 to 272,754 (-2.4%), while bridges rated Fair rose from 291,301 to 309,688 (+6.3%). In other words, bridges are aging out of the Good category faster than they are being rehabilitated back into it, even as bridges are being kept from falling all the way to Poor. ASCE's 2025 report card frames this directly: 49.1% of bridges are Fair, 44.1% Good, and 6.8% Poor — "nearly half are in fair condition — aging and at risk of further decline." This is consistent with a national bridge stock that is being kept from getting dramatically worse, but is not being renewed fast enough to reverse the aging trend.

Step 5 — Which states diverge from the national pattern

The national deceleration masks large state-level variation. Computing each state's own change in % Poor bridges from 2021 to 2025 directly from the NBI data: the biggest improvers were Rhode Island (-3.48 points, 17.5%->14.0%), West Virginia (-2.58 points, 20.4%->17.8%), Oklahoma (-2.39 points, 9.9%->7.5%), Pennsylvania (-1.74 points, 13.8%->12.1%), Hawaii (-1.73 points) and Louisiana (-1.54 points). ARTBA's independent analysis (using 2024 vs. earlier years) names Pennsylvania, Louisiana, Florida, West Virginia, and California as the biggest movers — three of five states overlap exactly with the top improvers found here, corroborating the state-level finding despite slightly different comparison years. On the other side, several states got measurably worse over the same span: Maine (+2.79 points), Vermont (+1.58), Utah (+1.41), South Carolina (+1.02), Washington (+0.75), and Massachusetts (+0.74) all saw their Poor-bridge share rise even as the national average fell. West Virginia, despite being one of the biggest improvers, still has the highest Poor share of any state in the country (17.8% in 2025, was 20.4% in 2021) — improvement from a very deep hole, not parity with the rest of the country.

Data quality note

A defect was found in this connector's bridge deck-area totals: transport.bridges' summed deck_area_sqm for 2023-2025 jumps implausibly (from ~$4.9\times10^8$ m² in 2022 to ~$1.5\times10^9$ m² in 2023), a roughly 3x increase in one year that cannot reflect real US bridge deck area growth — almost certainly a row-duplication or unit issue in that field for those years. This report therefore relies on the count-based bridge_condition classification (G/F/P), which is internally consistent 2019-2025 and independently corroborated by the Reason Foundation and ARTBA analyses cited above, rather than the deck-area-weighted metric.
