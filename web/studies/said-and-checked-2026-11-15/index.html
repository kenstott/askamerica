Said & Checked

What officeholders said, and what the data shows

2026-11-15 · 56 statements from 15 speakers

11 checked: 5 supported, 3 unsettled, 2 unsupported, 1 contradicted · 45 not checkable

Fifty-six statements by 15 officeholders appeared in two days of NPR and PBS NewsHour politics coverage. Most were about three things: the Nov. 13 nationwide ground stop and what it will cost to fix air traffic control, the Iran war and talk of ending it, and what Democrats plan to do with the majorities they won on Nov. 3.

Eleven of the 56 were checked. Eight were factual claims. Three were opinions or predictions resting on a factual premise, and for those the premise alone was rated. Five came out supported, including the transportation secretary's statement that air traffic control has seen decades of underinvestment. One was contradicted: the transportation secretary's reported account of what caused the ground stop differs from the FAA's own published account, though the FAA says its investigation is still open. Three were unsettled, meaning evidence was found but does not decide them: the president's statement that much of the air traffic control system is 50 years old, the Florida governor's statement about what a state law requires, and the premise behind the secretary of state's statement about economic pressure on Iran. Two were unsupported, meaning no evidence was found for them; that is not a finding that they are false.

Three statements are linked to a direct counter-statement: two to a statement by another official on this page, and one to the FAA's own published account. A link shows that the two disagree on the same point. It is not a finding about which is right.

The other 45 are opinions, predictions, promises and one personal attack. They are listed because they were said, not because they can be checked, and are folded under each speaker.

How to read this page
Supported
Warehouse or external primary data confirms the statement.
Unsettled
Evidence was found, but it does not decide the claim: it turns on how a law or a loose term is read, or the evidence does not speak to the specific point.
Unsupported
No evidence was found in the AskAmerica warehouse or any external factual source. This does not mean the statement is false.
Contradicted
A primary source, read directly, says something different on the specific point, but some question remains open: an inquiry is unfinished, or the source could be using a different definition or period.
False
A settled official record, read directly, rules the statement out: a published measurement, no ordinary reading of the statement that fits it, and nothing pending that could change it.
Citejacked
The speaker cites a source that does not say what the speaker claims.
Premised prediction
An opinion or prediction that rests on a factual premise. The label rates the premise only, never the opinion or prediction built on it.
Counter-statement
A link to a statement by another speaker, or to an official document, that directly addresses the same point and says otherwise. A link is not a finding about which is right, and a rating shown beside a counter-statement rates that statement only.
Not checkable
An opinion, prediction, promise or personal attack. No data can settle it.

Every statement is listed, whatever its kind. There are no scores and no totals per person. Statements that could not be checked are folded under each speaker. Selection: Every statement by an in-scope officeholder quoted in a story published on Nov. 14 or 15, 2026 in the NPR Politics and PBS NewsHour Politics feeds, as read on Nov. 15. PBS NewsHour carries Associated Press reporting. Two NPR audio segments with no transcript were skipped. Quotes under five words were left out, and two entries that were a commentator's account of what an official said were removed. No original source (transcript, release or post) could be read for any statement, so all wording is as the outlet reported it.

Reported as: He has said the problem began when L3Harris was replacing a section of old copper wire with new fiber wire, somehow causing the system to overload.

The FAA's published account says otherwise. Asked whether upgrades caused the outage, it answers no and attributes the instability to the legacy system. It says data from two sites, a radar site in Amarillo, Texas and a radio site in El Dorado, Arkansas, where L3Harris had deployed a new router, flooded the network. The FAA also says the root cause is still under investigation, and L3Harris has not named a cause. The wording checked is AP's account of what the secretary said.
