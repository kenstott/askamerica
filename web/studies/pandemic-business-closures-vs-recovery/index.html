How many local businesses never came back after the pandemic, and how much does that vary by place and industry?

Published report: http://127.0.0.1:53584/a/0b49b6f3ef9e1e1a6325575bcf1b7194.html (local-machine link, for the human reader; not fetchable from here)

Dashboard image: dashboard.png Supporting chart: chart-leisure-hospitality-by-state.png

Summary

Nationally, the number of private business establishments is UP 18.3% since 2019 (9.98M -> 11.80M, BLS QCEW, private ownership, 2019 vs 2024) — a raw national establishment count says the pandemic left almost no scar. That headline is real but misleading: it is dominated by a surge of new business formation (19M+ new-business applications since 2020, per Census Business Formation Statistics and Decker & Haltiwanger's Richmond Fed/Boston Fed research) that mathematically offsets closures in the same aggregate. A new business opening does not mean the business that existed in 2019 came back.

Looking at employment (not just establishment counts) within specific industries and places tells a very different story:

  • By industry (national, BLS QCEW, private, 2019 vs 2024): Accommodation/hotels (NAICS 721) employment is still down 6.8% (2.07M -> 1.93M jobs) even though establishment counts in that sector rose 8.2%. Nursing/residential care (623) shows the same pattern: -1.4% employment despite +10.9% more establishments. By contrast, food services & bars (722) have recovered and then some (+1.8% employment, +9.2% establishments), and arts/entertainment (711) and professional services (541) both grew employment double-digits. So within "leisure and hospitality," hotels — not restaurants — carry almost all of the non-recovery; a subgroup, not the whole group, is the actual laggard. This matches BLS's own 2021 finding that accommodation and food services had the highest COVID closure-mandate rate of any industry (36%).

  • By place: Using NAICS 72 (Accommodation & Food Services) as the clearest pandemic-exposed sector proxy, computed state-by-state: establishment COUNTS grew in 50 of 51 states + DC (only Massachusetts fell, -3.7%), but EMPLOYMENT is still below 2019 in 25 of 52 tracked jurisdictions — roughly half the country, five years on. Worst 10: Vermont -7.1%, Hawaii -6.7%, West Virginia -5.8%, Louisiana -5.8%, Massachusetts -5.3% (also -3.7% on establishment count), Illinois -4.5%, Maryland -4.5%, Oregon -4.0%, New York -3.6%, Michigan -2.9%. Hawaii/Vermont fit a tourism-dependent story; Massachusetts/Illinois/New York/Maryland point at an urban-core, reduced-office-foot-traffic pattern — consistent with NYC EDC's own finding that Midtown/Midtown East was still 8% below its 2019 small-business count as of 2023. At the 27-metro grain, Chicago is the clearest large-metro laggard on total private establishments AND employment (-1.0% / -2.4%); every other large metro checked grew.

  • Data-quality flag: Seattle metro showed an even steeper apparent establishment decline (-9.7%) in the same table, but investigation traced it almost entirely to one implausible line item (health-care establishments down ~60% with no comparable move in any other Seattle sector) — logged as a likely QCEW area/industry-code discontinuity via report_issue and excluded from headline geographic claims.

  • Recency: BLS QCEW is complete through 2024 (~1-year lag); Census County Business Patterns, the other candidate source, has an interior gap covering 2018-2020 in this warehouse and stops at 2023, so it could not supply a clean 2019 baseline — QCEW was used instead. No source here reaches 2025-2026, so this answer reflects recovery status as of 2024 data, not necessarily today.

Confidence

  • Medium-high on national/industry-level figures — BLS QCEW is an administrative census of nearly all UI-covered employers, not a survey.
  • Medium on the state ranking — n=52 jurisdictions, single-sector (NAICS 72) proxy, no controls for tourism mix, seasonality, or population growth.
  • Low on any single-metro claim beyond Chicago; Seattle's number was found to be a likely data artifact and excluded.
