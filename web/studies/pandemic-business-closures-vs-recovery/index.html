Roughly 6 in 10 pandemic-era business closures never came back — but the damage was heavily concentrated in a handful of high-contact industries and coastal/urban places

Synthesis of Yelp, NY Fed, NBER (California administrative tax-panel data), and Census County Business Patterns, 2020-2022

Summary

There is no single government count of "businesses that never came back" — no federal survey asks that question directly and gives a clean national number. The best evidence comes from three independent sources that agree on the shape of the answer: a majority of businesses that closed in 2020 never reopened (Yelp measured 60% of 163,735 tracked closures as permanent by August 2020; the New York Fed found that of small leisure/hospitality/retail firms still closed in May 2021, only about 3% would ever reopen), but the damage was extremely uneven: concentrated in a handful of face-to-face, high-contact industries (restaurants, bars, beauty/fitness, retail) and in coastal/urban places with longer or stricter shutdowns and weaker in-person foot traffic, while professional/remote-friendly services and Sun Belt states came through largely unscathed. Because national statistics count NET establishments (closures minus new openings), the overall business count actually recovered and then exceeded its pre-pandemic level by 2022 — that recovery is real, but it is driven by a historic surge in NEW business formation, not by the businesses that closed coming back. We computed the geographic and industry variation directly from Census County Business Patterns (2017 vs. 2021) to make that unevenness visible in the government data itself, alongside the permanent-closure literature.

How many closures were permanent — the direct evidence

Yelp Economic Impact Report (Sept. 2020): of 163,735 U.S. businesses that closed on the Yelp platform between March 1 and August 31, 2020, 97,966 (60%) were marked permanently closed. By industry: restaurants 61% of closures permanent (19,590 of 32,109); bars/nightlife 54% (3,499 of 6,451); shopping/retail 58% (17,503 of 30,374); beauty 42% (7,002 of 16,585); fitness 43% (2,616 of 6,024). Professional services — lawyers, architects, accountants, physicians, real estate agents — saw only 2-3 closures per thousand businesses.

Federal Reserve Bank of New York, Liberty Street Economics (May 2021), using Homebase scheduling-software data on ~100,000 small leisure/hospitality/retail businesses: 35% of pre-pandemic businesses were still closed in spring 2021, most inactive 20+ weeks; the weekly hazard rate of reopening declines the longer a business stays closed, and each additional week of closure cuts the reopening probability by about 2 percentage points. The Census Small Business Pulse Survey corroborated this: in Feb-March 2021 about 1.8% of small businesses were permanently closing each week versus 2.1% reopening — a near-standstill recovery.

NBER Working Paper 30285 (Fairlie, Fossen, Johnsen & Droboniku, 2022), the only study to use full administrative tax-panel microdata (California Dept. of Tax and Fee Administration sales-tax records, the universe of businesses with taxable sales, 7M+ observations) rather than survey or app data: the year-over-year business closure rate jumped from 4.8% (2019Q1) to 6.5% (2020Q1) and from 3.7% (2019Q2) to 6.7% (2020Q2), then reversed sharply — 2020Q3's closure rate (2.9%) was actually LOWER than 2019Q3 (4.1%) as the economy rebounded. Small businesses (under $500K annualized taxable sales) were hit far harder: their closure rate rose from 4.6% to 8.5% between 2019Q2 and 2020Q2, versus 1.3% to 2.7% for large businesses — a COVID-attributable gap of about 2.0 percentage points after controlling for trend/seasonality. The resulting shift in market share toward survivors pushed California's (deseasonalized) retail Herfindahl-Hirschman Index from 21.4 in 2019Q3 to a peak of 38.0 in 2020Q2, still elevated at 26.0 by 2021Q3 — evidence that closures concentrated market share among larger, surviving firms rather than being fully offset by new entrants in the same space.

The industry pattern, cross-checked against Census establishment counts

The literature's industry ranking is unambiguous: contact-intensive, discretionary, in-person services (restaurants, bars, gyms, salons, general retail) suffered permanent-closure rates of 40-60%, while professional/remote-capable services (law, accounting, architecture, medicine, real estate) saw closures in the low single digits per thousand.

We cross-checked this against Census County Business Patterns, comparing establishment counts (this warehouse holds 2017 and then 2021-2023; 2018-2020 are not loaded, a gap disclosed rather than papered over) at the 2-digit NAICS level nationally:

  • Retail trade (NAICS 44-45): -2.6% net establishments, 2017 to 2021 (1,075,498 -> 1,047,947) — the only major consumer-facing sector with a net national decline, consistent with retail's high permanent-closure share in the Yelp data.
  • Mining (-10.4%), Management of companies (-7.6%), Wholesale trade (-4.5%)
  • Accommodation & food services (NAICS 72): +2.8% nationally — net establishments actually grew, because a wave of new restaurant/food openings (ghost kitchens, food trucks, outdoor-dining concepts) offset the well-documented permanent closures. This is the key reconciliation: the NET count recovering does not mean the SAME businesses came back — Yelp's own data shows roughly 6 in 10 of the closures in this sector were never going to reopen; new entrants replaced them.
  • Real estate (+12.1%), construction (+9.1%), arts/entertainment/recreation (+8.8%), professional/scientific/technical services (+5.3%), and health care (+5.5%) all grew comfortably, consistent with the professional-services closure data above.

The geographic pattern

Using the same 2017-vs-2021 Census CBP comparison at the state level, the geographic pattern in the two hardest-hit consumer sectors is stark and consistent with independent reporting that California, New York, Texas, and Florida saw the largest RAW closure counts (simply because they are the largest states), but that the rate of net loss was concentrated in specific places, not spread evenly:

Accommodation & food services — worst net declines: Vermont (-4.4%), New York (-3.4%), Alaska (-2.7%), DC (-2.0%), Hawaii (-1.7%), Massachusetts (-1.5%). Best net gains: Texas (+9.3%), Florida (+9.1%), Georgia (+9.0%), Idaho (+8.9%), Nevada (+8.1%), Tennessee (+7.6%).

Retail trade — worst net declines: New York (-7.2%), Massachusetts (-6.9%), Connecticut (-6.8%), Vermont (-6.7%), DC (-6.7%), Hawaii (-6.3%). Best net gains/smallest losses: Georgia (+4.0%), Utah (+3.9%), Oklahoma (+2.2%), Texas (+1.8%), Nevada (+1.7%), Wyoming (+1.6%).

The pattern is the same in both sectors: dense, high cost-of-living, coastal/Northeastern states and DC — places with longer and stricter pandemic-era capacity restrictions, heavier reliance on commuter/office foot traffic that evaporated with remote work, and higher commercial rents — lost businesses on net, while Sun Belt states that combined shorter restrictions, lower costs, and strong in-migration gained businesses on net. This mirrors independent news reporting that identified California, Texas, Florida, and New York as having the largest closure COUNTS (a function of population size) while separately noting that California and New York's urban cores (San Francisco, downtown Manhattan) were disproportionately hollowed out — consistent with an academic finding (cited in search results, not independently verified here) that San Francisco saw roughly a 48% small-business closure rate, the highest of any major metro studied.

Why the numbers are usually reported as a recovery, and why that's a different question

Census Business Formation Statistics show business APPLICATIONS surged to a record 5.4 million in 2021 (versus roughly 3.5 million pre-pandemic), and the total number of business establishments nationally grew 10.7% from 2020Q2 to 2022Q2 — more than twice the 4.8% growth rate in the two years before the pandemic. Reported plainly, this looks like a full recovery. It is not the same claim as "the businesses that closed came back": it is new business formation outrunning the permanent closures, heavily concentrated in the same industries and consumer categories vacated by the businesses that failed (a 27.8% jump above the pre-pandemic baseline in "high-propensity" applications likely to become employers by 2022). The two claims — "total business count recovered" and "most closed businesses never reopened" — are both true simultaneously and describe different things: churn versus survival.

Caveats and what we could not verify directly

  • No single AskAmerica table gives a national, gross, permanent-closure count by place and industry — that statistic exists only in the Yelp platform data, the NY Fed's proprietary Homebase panel, and California's confidential state tax-panel microdata (NBER WP 30285); none of it is loaded in this warehouse, so the headline permanent-closure percentages are cited from the literature, not recomputed here.
  • The Census County Business Patterns table used for the industry/geography cross-check has a data gap for 2018-2020 in this warehouse (confirmed via data_coverage: only 2017, 2021, 2022, 2023 are loaded), so our computed change is 2017-vs-2021, a 4-year span bracketing the pandemic rather than a clean pre/post comparison. It measures NET establishment change (openings minus closures), which is a different, and generally smaller-magnitude, quantity than the gross permanent-closure rate reported by Yelp/NY Fed/NBER — we did not conflate the two, and flagged the difference explicitly in every section that uses CBP.
  • The claim that San Francisco saw a ~48% small-business closure rate came back in a web search summary attributed to unspecified academic research; it was not independently traced to a primary source document and should be treated as a secondary citation, not a verified primary-source figure.
  • All figures above were read from the actual fetched text of the cited primary sources (Yelp/fee.org article reproducing Yelp's report, the NY Fed Liberty Street Economics post, and pages 1-15 of the NBER working paper PDF) except where noted otherwise.
