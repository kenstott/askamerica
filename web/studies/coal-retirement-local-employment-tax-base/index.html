Coal plant shutdowns cut local utility-sector jobs by roughly half within a year and county tax revenue by 10-32%, with only partial, slow recovery

Case evidence from Adams County, Ohio (Killen & J.M. Stuart Stations, closed 2018) plus national coal-shock literature

Summary

In the counties and towns where a coal plant actually sits, the shutdown is not a gentle transition — it is a sharp, largely permanent shock. Using BLS QCEW data for Adams County, Ohio (home to the Killen and J.M. Stuart coal plants, both closed in 2018), utility-sector employment fell from 139 jobs in 2018 to 61 in 2019 (-56%) and has stayed near a third of its pre-closure level ever since (44-82 jobs/year, 2020-2025). The plants' wage bill fell from $11.4M to $4.5M in the same year and never recovered. Published economic-impact research on these same closures (Jolley et al. 2019) put the total employment loss, including indirect and induced jobs in the surrounding region, at over 1,100 (370 direct), and found the county lost $8.5M/year in tax revenue (mostly tangible personal-property tax the plants had paid on their equipment) — a 32% cut to the county general fund and a 10% cut to total county revenue. National research on the broader 2010s coal shock (coal mine, not plant, closures, but the closest large-sample causal evidence available) finds a job-loss multiplier of about 2.0 and persistent, worsening effects on county GDP, wages and employment through at least 10 years after the shock, not a quick rebound.

Step 1 - What the case evidence shows: Adams County, Ohio

Employment in the utility sector never returns to its 2018 level even six years out; it plateaus at roughly a third of its pre-closure size, likely reflecting the residual workforce needed for decommissioning/site maintenance and, in later years, any conversion or redevelopment activity, not a rebound in plant-related jobs. Notably, 2018 utility-sector jobs paid an average of $1,574/week versus $734/week for all jobs county-wide - a roughly 2.1x wage premium consistent with the national coal-sector wage premium (which rose from 53% in 1986 to 102% in 2011 per Nason, Scavette & Stephens 2026), meaning the jobs lost were disproportionately high-value relative to what typically replaces them locally.

Total county employment across ALL industries (not just utilities) did not collapse - it dipped only slightly in 2019-2020 and resumed a slow upward trend by 2022-2025 - which is consistent with the literature's point that plant-level job losses are usually small relative to a county's total workforce, even though they are large relative to the high-wage, well-benefited jobs the plant itself provided, and even though the associated tax-base loss is disproportionate to the modest change in the county employment total.

Step 2 - Tax base impact

The fiscal channel is distinct from and often larger, relative to a local government's own budget, than the direct jobs channel. Jolley, Khalaf, Michaud & Sandler (2019, Regional Science Policy & Practice) modeled the fiscal impact of the Adams County closures directly: because Ohio taxes power-plant equipment as tangible personal property (TPP), and TPP tax revenue cannot legally be replenished by another facility once the plant is decommissioned, Adams County and its local governments/school districts lost a collective $8.5 million per year in tax revenue - a 32% decrease in the county general fund, a 10% decrease in total county revenue, and decreases ranging from 29% to 40% across other county service-area levies (fire districts, libraries, etc. funded by property-tax millage). This mirrors a broader pattern documented for coal-mining (not just plant) counties: an MIT Environmental Solutions Initiative study of Greene County, PA found county-wide mineral tax value down almost 13% from 2010-2019, with one school district (Central Greene) that had drawn over half its funding from coal-severance-style taxes seeing mineral revenues fall 32% even after raising tax rates. NBER Working Paper 27307 (Morris, Kaufman & Doshi, 2020), extrapolating from 27 coal-reliant counties, estimated that a full "demise of coal" could cost these counties roughly 20% of total local-government revenue on average, with coal-related revenue funding a third or more of the budget in some illustrative counties.

Step 3 - How persistent is the effect? National coal-shock evidence

Because single-plant case studies cannot rule out that the county would have declined anyway, the strongest causal evidence on persistence comes from a difference-in-differences study of the broader 2010s coal shock (Nason, Scavette & Stephens, Federal Reserve Bank of Philadelphia Working Paper 26-30, June 2026), which compares 81 Appalachian counties that had a coal mine closure between 2011-2016 against 39 similar counties that did not, using county and year fixed effects (parallel pre-trends confirmed via event-study leads back to 2001 and 2006).

  • By 2016 (end of the shock window): unemployment rate +0.9 percentage point, payroll employment -4%, wages and salaries -8%, county GDP -14%, relative to control counties.
  • By 2021 (5 years later): effects had NOT faded - they worsened - to payroll employment -6%, wages and salaries -10%, GDP -17%. Only the unemployment-rate gap narrowed, and that was because the labor force shrank (people left or stopped looking for work), not because job-finding improved.
  • The estimated job-loss multiplier is about 2.0 for the 10-year window (roughly 1 additional non-coal job lost for every coal job lost), more than 50% higher than the 1.35 multiplier measured for the 1980s coal bust, attributed to the local coal-wage premium roughly doubling between the two eras.
  • Job losses concentrate not just in the affected industry itself but in coal-servicing sectors: administrative/waste-management services (-16%), transportation and warehousing (-13%), finance and insurance (-9%), and retail trade (-6%), consistent with a local demand multiplier.

This is a coal MINE closure study, not a coal PLANT closure study - the two are related but distinct shocks (mine closures hit extraction-sector jobs directly; plant closures hit utility-sector jobs and, uniquely, a large chunk of the local property-tax base). It is cited here as the best available large-sample causal evidence on how long these effects persist, and the Adams County plant case above shows a similar pattern of an abrupt initial drop with no full recovery even 6 years out.

Step 4 - Bottom line

Jobs: In the specific communities hosting a coal plant, direct plant-level jobs typically fall by roughly half within the first year of closure and do not fully recover; counting indirect/induced local jobs (retail, services, contractors that served plant workers), total local job losses run 2-3x the direct plant headcount, and displaced workers who find new local jobs generally take a pay cut, because plant jobs paid roughly double the local average wage.

Tax base: The tax-base hit is often disproportionately larger than the jobs hit, because power-plant property taxes are frequently a concentrated, non-diversifiable revenue source for the host county and its school districts - losses of 10-32% of general-fund/total county revenue in a single year are documented, and this loss is structural (the tax code often prevents replacing it without a new large facility), not cyclical.

Timing: The employment and wage effects are visible within the first year, but they do not resolve within a year or two - the best causal evidence on the broader coal shock shows effects still growing 5 and 10 years out, and the Adams County QCEW series shows utility employment still at roughly a third of its pre-closure level 6 years after the plants closed.
