Coal plant closures show up clearly in a small dependent county's payroll and lost utility tax revenue, but not in a formal cross-county jobs or income-tax test

EIA-860 plant retirements + BLS QCEW county employment (2008-2024, observed) + IRS SOI county income (2011-2022); event-study design, 7 treated / 14 neighboring control counties

Summary

Overall county-level employment and county-level income-tax base show no statistically reliable common effect in a formal event-study across 7 counties that lost a large coal plant between 2015 and 2020 versus 14 neighboring counties that did not (cluster-robust p-values all above 0.25 for employment, above 0.29 for AGI and total income tax). But that null hides real heterogeneity: Muhlenberg County, KY -- small and coal-dependent -- lost 16% of total county employment by the year its Paradise plant retired and was still 8% below its pre-closure peak four years later. Two larger, more diversified counties (Adams, OH and Titus, TX) show no visible dip at all. The specific fiscal channel the literature says matters most -- property/utility tax base, not household income tax -- isn't in this corpus, so the county tax-base result here is a lower bound on the true fiscal hit; published case studies put that loss far higher (see below).

What the literature already established (read before querying)

Facts. Morris, Kaufman & Doshi (NBER Working Paper 27307, 2020) regress local-government revenue on coal employment/production across 27 coal-reliant U.S. counties and extrapolate to a full wind-down: coal-reliant counties could lose about 20% of local government revenue on average, with some counties funding a third or more of their budget from coal. Jolley, Khalaf, Michaud & Sandler (Regional Science Policy & Practice, 2019) study two Dayton Power & Light coal plant closures in Adams County, OH (the same J.M. Stuart/Killen closures in this analysis): they estimate over 1,100 total jobs lost region-wide (direct + supply chain, beyond the county line) and $8.5 million in lost tangible-personal-property tax revenue for local governments, since Ohio plants stop paying TPP tax once decommissioned. Reporting on Emery/Carbon County, UT documents a 700% municipal-services-tax increase after a plant closure to offset lost property-tax and mineral-royalty revenue, since utility plants there carried ~60% of the county's property tax base.

Methodology takeaway. Published studies isolate the fiscal effect through property/TPP tax and assessed valuation -- a narrow, concentrated tax base a coal plant dominates -- and the jobs effect through direct plant + supply-chain payroll, sometimes spanning a multi-county labor market rather than one county. Metrics takeaway: "tax base" in this literature means the property tax roll, not household income; "jobs" often means a regional skillshed, not just the plant's home county.

What this corpus could test, and what it found

AskAmerica has EIA-860 plant-level retirement dates and county-level BLS QCEW employment and IRS SOI county income/tax data, but no property-tax, assessed-valuation, or municipal-budget table -- so this run tests the two channels the corpus can reach (total county payroll employment, and household AGI/income-tax) rather than the property-tax channel the literature identifies as primary. That's a real gap, not a null result on the actual question asked; see Data gaps below.

Design. Identified 7 counties whose single dominant coal plant retired 2015-2020 (Muhlenberg KY/Paradise 2020, Adams OH/J.M.Stuart+Killen 2018, Beaver PA/Bruce Mansfield 2019, Titus TX/Monticello 2018, Rosebud MT/Colstrip units 2020, Washington OH/Muskingum River 2015, Putnam GA/Harllee Branch 2015), plus 14 adjacent, similarly rural never-treated counties as controls (via geo.county_adjacency). Ran an event-study (unit+time fixed effects, cluster-robust SEs, staggered-adoption diagnostics) against: (1) total county employment, BLS QCEW 2008-2024 (a table whose declared coverage window undercounts its actual data back to 2008 -- filed as a data-quality issue this session); (2) county AGI and total income tax, IRS SOI 2011-2022.

Result. Neither outcome shows a lead/lag coefficient distinguishable from zero (employment: all |t|<1.3, p≥0.25; AGI and income tax: all |t|<1.1, p≥0.29). The pre-trend joint test does not reject parallel trends in either case, so the null is not obviously an artifact of already-diverging counties -- but with only 21 clusters, cluster-robust inference is itself under-powered (few-clusters warning), and adoption is staggered across 4 distinct years, which can bias a pooled two-way-FE estimate in either direction. This should be read as "no common effect detectable at this sample size," not as "plant closures don't matter."

The county trajectories tell a sharper story than the pooled test. Muhlenberg County (population ~30,000, the plant a large share of its economic base) lost 16% of total county employment between 2017 and the 2020 closure year and had only partly recovered by 2024. Beaver County, PA declined further post-closure but that window is confounded with the 2020 COVID shock and a broader Pittsburgh-region manufacturing slide -- can't cleanly attribute. Adams County, OH and Titus County, TX -- both larger, more economically diversified -- show essentially no employment dip and grew through 2024, consistent with Jolley et al.'s framing that a much larger regional economy absorbs a single plant's several-hundred jobs more easily than a small county does, even while the same closure produces a real, concentrated hit to that plant's own workforce and the county's TPP tax roll.

Data gaps and how they change what can be said

  • No property/TPP tax or assessed-valuation table in this corpus -- the literature's primary fiscal channel. The AGI/income-tax null result above should not be read as evidence property tax revenue was unaffected; it measures a different, much less concentrated tax base. Treat the NBER ~20% local-revenue and Ohio $8.5M TPP figures above as the best available quantification of that channel until this gap is filled (suggest_external_sources: county assessor/comptroller open-data portals, state Department of Revenue TPP filings).
  • BLS QCEW/SOI are annual, county-wide totals -- they cannot isolate the plant's own several-hundred jobs from a county's tens-of-thousands-strong labor market, which is exactly why Adams and Titus show no dip despite Jolley et al.'s regional 1,100-job estimate: most of that loss is diffused into a wider labor market or offset by unrelated county growth.
  • Declared coverage bug: econ.county_wages/county_qcew describe_table said 2019-2024 only; real data reaches back to 2008, which materially improved this analysis once found and was reported via report_issue this session.
