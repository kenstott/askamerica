Coal plant closures wipe out plant-sector jobs almost overnight; the county-wide tax base often looks fine because the loss is concentrated in property taxes and specific taxing districts, not income taxes

BLS QCEW county employment, IRS SOI county income, EIA-860 plant retirements, and published county-level closure studies

Summary

In the county directly hosting a retired coal plant, the plant's own workforce is largely gone within a year of shutdown — in Adams County, Ohio, NAICS 2211 (power generation) employment fell from 139 jobs and $11.4M in wages in 2018 to zero in 2019, the year the J.M. Stuart plant retired, before a smaller replacement workforce (~55-57 jobs, about 40% of the prior level) reappeared by 2021, likely tied to decommissioning and site redevelopment work. But county-wide total employment barely dipped (6,070 to 5,898, -2.8%) and kept growing afterward, and county-wide IRS adjusted gross income (a proxy for the county's overall income tax base) shows no visible break at all. The published literature explains why those two facts are not in tension: the fiscal damage from a coal plant closure lands overwhelmingly on property tax revenue and on the specific school district / township that hosted the plant, not on county-aggregate income or county-aggregate employment — so a county-level jobs or income statistic will usually look resilient even where the hosting community is fiscally devastated. Published case studies estimate local property-tax-base losses of roughly 10-50% for the specific taxing jurisdiction (higher for the school district than for the county as a whole), roughly 1,000+ regional jobs lost across multi-plant closure waves, and an NBER estimate that coal-reliant county governments could lose about 20% of total revenue as the industry winds down.

What AskAmerica's own data shows (BLS QCEW + IRS SOI)

Method: EIA Form 860 (energy.eia_power_plants) was used to identify coal-fired generators that stopped reporting between 2014 and 2022 — a proxy for retirement, since EIA does not carry a single clean 'retired' flag in this corpus and generators simply drop out of subsequent report years. That plant list was built with a filter county_fips IS NOT NULL, which excludes 20 distinct coal generators (out of several hundred distinct plant_ids in the table) that carry no county location in this EIA feed — mostly small or non-U.S.-territory generators where EIA's own county field is blank. None of those 20 could be geographically matched to any county-level economic series regardless, so the exclusion does not change which counties were available to study; it is reported here because the recipe requires naming any hand-applied filter, not because it altered the result.

Four single-plant, single-industry counties were tested for a clean before/after signal in BLS QCEW county data (econ.county_qcew, NAICS 2211 power generation, private ownership): Adams County OH (J.M. Stuart plant, last reported 2018), Muhlenberg County KY (Paradise plant, last reported 2019), Titus County TX (Monticello plant, last reported 2017), and Coconino County AZ (Navajo plant, last reported 2018).

Three of the four counties show BLS-suppressed (zeroed) employment figures in this specific NAICS code for nearly the entire time series, before and after closure alike — a standard QCEW disclosure-avoidance artifact when a county has very few employers in a 4-digit industry code, not evidence the jobs never existed. Only Adams County, Ohio yielded an unsuppressed series, shown in the dashboard above. There, the signal is unambiguous: sector employment and wages went to exactly zero the year the plant retired, then partially recovered to about 40% of the pre-closure level two years later — plausibly decommissioning crews or a replacement facility, not the original jobs returning.

Meanwhile Adams County's total employment (all industries) never fell by more than about 3% and resumed growing the very next year, and county adjusted gross income (fiscal.soi_income_by_county_year) rose smoothly through the closure with no detectable break. This divergence — a total wipeout in the specific sector against a barely-visible dent in the county aggregate — is itself the finding: at county grain, the loss is diluted by every other employer and household in the county. The literature (below) is explicit that this dilution is even more severe for jobs/income than for property-tax revenue, because the plant's assessed value was disproportionately concentrated in the taxing district that hosted it (often a school district or township smaller than the county), not spread across the county's tax base the way its (small) share of county employment was already spread across the county's job base.

Data-gap note: this corpus does not carry a property-tax-base or local-government-revenue table at the taxing-district or county grain (search_catalog returned no match for 'closures' or a local property tax base measure), so the property-tax and school-district-revenue figures below are drawn from the published literature, not recomputed from AskAmerica data.

What the published literature adds (property tax base, wider job counts)

  • Regional jobs: A peer-reviewed study of Appalachian Ohio coal-plant closures (Dayton Power & Light's units in Adams County, plus an associated training-center closure) found over 1,100 total jobs lost across the region when direct, indirect, and induced effects are counted — far more than the plant's own headcount, because the multiplier effect ripples through contractors, coal suppliers, and local spending (Betz, Partridge & Fahey, The Economic, Fiscal, and Workforce Impacts of Coal-Fired Power Plant Closures in Appalachian Ohio, Energy Research & Social Science / Energy Policy, 2019).
  • Property tax base — the sharpest local hit: the same Ohio study found decommissioned plants stop paying tangible personal property (TPP) tax, producing about $8.5 million in lost annual local-government tax revenue. Colorado's Comanche coal station is reported to generate nearly $31 million/year in property taxes — about 10.5% of all taxes Pueblo County collects — illustrating how large a single plant's share of a county's tax base can be even where county-aggregate income looks unaffected (Colorado Sun, Dec. 2025; NACo coverage of the Centralia, WA / Lewis County closure).
  • School districts specifically are hit harder than counties: reporting on Colorado's Yampa Valley plant closures found school districts facing both lost per-pupil property-tax revenue and fewer local job opportunities for graduates — a taxing-district effect that a county-level statistic will not surface (The Hechinger Report, 2023-2024).
  • Aggregate revenue-at-risk estimate: Morris, Kaufman & Doshi (NBER Working Paper 27307, published in Environmental and Energy Policy and the Economy Vol. 2, 2021) regress on 27 coal-reliant counties and estimate that, extrapolated to the industry's full wind-down, these counties could lose on average about 20% of total local-government revenue — and note this does not even capture the secondary erosion of other tax bases as the dominant industry's collapse depresses property values and spending more broadly.
  • Range across communities: other reporting on Xcel Energy coal-unit retirements describes a 10% to 50% hole in the local tax base depending on how large the plant was relative to the host jurisdiction, alongside hundreds of direct jobs lost per closure.

How to read the two pictures together

The AskAmerica-computed county series and the published literature are not in conflict — they are measuring different grains and different tax instruments, and both are needed. County-level BLS QCEW employment and IRS income data (this corpus's strongest coverage) show that a coal-plant closure devastates its own narrow industry code almost instantly (-100% in the one unsuppressed county tested) while leaving county-aggregate jobs and county-aggregate income tax base nearly untouched, because the plant's payroll was always a small share of a diversified county economy. The property-tax literature shows the opposite pattern at the taxing-district grain: a coal plant's assessed value is often a large, sometimes dominant, share of the specific school district or township tax base that hosts it, so the closure that barely dents county income statistics can still blow a 10-50% hole in that district's revenue and cost the community $8-31 million a year in lost property tax. A reader asking 'does the local economy recover' needs the county jobs data; a reader asking 'does the local government/school district recover' needs the property-tax literature — this corpus answers the first cleanly and can only report the second from outside sources.

WITH coal AS (
  SELECT plant_id, plant_name, county_fips, state_abbr, MAX(report_year) AS last_year, MIN(report_year) AS first_year, SUM(nameplate_capacity_mw) AS cap
  FROM energy.eia_power_plants
  WHERE technology ILIKE '%Coal%' AND county_fips IS NOT NULL
  GROUP BY plant_id, plant_name, county_fips, state_abbr
)
SELECT * FROM coal WHERE last_year < 2023 AND last_year >= 2014 ORDER BY cap DESC LIMIT 30
