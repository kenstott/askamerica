Federal Onshore Oil & Gas Leasing Returned About $379 in Royalty Revenue per Leased Acre in FY2024 — But the Range Runs from Under $1 to Over $1,300

DOI ONRR royalty revenue / BLM leased acreage, FY2004-2024, national and by state

Summary

In FY2024, federal onshore oil & gas royalty payments to the U.S. Treasury totaled $8.42 billion on 22.2 million acres under BLM lease — an average of $379 per leased acre (roughly $679 per producing acre, since only about 12.4 million of those acres, 56%, were actually producing). That average obscures an enormous spread: New Mexico alone (dominated by the Permian Basin) generated $1,340 per leased acre and collected 75.7% of all onshore federal royalty revenue on just 19% of the leased acreage, while most other leasing states — Montana, Alaska, Nevada, Kentucky, West Virginia, Idaho — return under $30/acre, several under $1/acre. The state distribution has a Gini coefficient of 0.92 ("extreme" concentration). Nationally, revenue per acre also swings hard with commodity prices: it roughly doubled from FY2021 ($191/acre) to FY2022 ($410/acre) as oil and gas prices spiked, even as leased acreage kept shrinking.

How this was computed

Numerator — royalty revenue only (excluding bonus bids, rents, and other fees, which are separate revenue streams tied to lease issuance rather than production) from DOI's Office of Natural Resources Revenue, filtered to land_category='Onshore', revenue_type='Royalties', mineral_lease_type='Oil & Gas', summed across commodity (oil, gas, NGLs, CO2, helium) for each fiscal year and state.

Denominator — BLM's own "Total Number of Acres Under Lease as of the Last Day of the Fiscal Year" (Public Land Statistics Table 2), the acreage BLM itself uses as the leasing-program denominator. A second figure divides by BLM's "Total Number of Producing Acres" (Table 6 national total, ~12.4 million in FY2024) — the subset of leased acreage that is actually producing oil or gas in paying quantities. The 20-year national acreage series (FY2004-2020) is BLM's own published total; FY2021-2024 national totals were reconstructed by summing BLM's per-state Table 2 figures for those years (BLM's own workbook leaves those year-totals as unevaluated spreadsheet formulas), and the FY2024 reconstruction ($22,218,215) matches BLM's separately published FY2024 total exactly, which validates the method for the three years in between.

Revenue per acre is a genuinely different question from revenue per barrel or per well — it answers "how much does an acre of federal mineral estate actually pay the Treasury," a land-productivity measure, not a commodity-price or drilling-efficiency measure.

Two forces move this number in opposite directions and both matter: leased acreage has fallen by more than half since the mid-2000s peak (BLM has issued far fewer new leases, and the industry itself has been relinquishing acreage it doesn't plan to drill, consolidating activity onto its best acreage), while revenue per acre nearly tripled from 2021 to 2023 purely on commodity prices (Russia's invasion of Ukraine and the post-COVID recovery pushed oil and gas prices sharply higher) with essentially no acreage growth. A shrinking, more-concentrated lease base earning much more per acre is consistent with industry "highgrading" — holding onto and developing only the most productive acreage (the Permian in New Mexico chief among it) and letting marginal leases lapse.

New Mexico (the Permian Basin's Delaware sub-basin) is the outlier that defines the whole distribution: with 75.7% of national onshore royalty revenue on 19% of leased acreage, it earns roughly 12x the leased-acre average and 40-90x what most other producing states earn. California and North Dakota also post very high per-acre figures, but for different reasons — California's federal leasehold is tiny and legacy (mature, low-decline Kern County fields), so a modest dollar total divides into a very small acreage base; North Dakota's figure reflects the Bakken shale's high per-acre productivity on a comparatively compact federal lease footprint. By contrast, Wyoming — the single largest federal onshore leaseholder by acreage (7.1 million acres, 32% of the national total) — earns only $110.60/acre, reflecting its mix of prolific gas fields (which sell at much lower per-unit value than the oil-heavy Permian) alongside vast, largely undeveloped or marginal acreage. Wyoming's per-acre return is about 8% of New Mexico's despite having 70% more leased acreage. A Gini coefficient of 0.92 across the 27 states with federal oil & gas leases confirms this is not a mild skew but near-total concentration: most of the acreage under lease nationally contributes almost nothing to the royalty total, and one state accounts for the great majority of it.

Producing acres vs. leased acres, and offshore

The $379/acre figure divides royalty revenue by all leased acreage, most of which is not producing at all — leases are routinely held for years while operators explore, permit, or simply wait. BLM's own FY2024 figures show only about 12.4 million of the 22.2 million leased acres (56%) were actually producing; dividing the same $8.42B by that narrower, more relevant base gives roughly $679 per producing acre — a materially different and arguably more meaningful number, since it measures return on land that is actually doing the job rather than land merely held under lease.

This analysis is onshore-only. Federal offshore leases (Gulf of Mexico and Pacific) generated $6.42 billion in royalties in FY2024 — nearly as much as the entire onshore program — from a lease inventory that is orders of magnitude smaller in acreage (BOEM Gulf of Mexico lease sales are typically tens of millions of acres offered, but actual acreage under lease at any time is far smaller and was not available from the sources checked in this session). Offshore per-acre productivity is almost certainly much higher than onshore's, but that figure could not be computed here without a reliable, dated BOEM leased-acreage total, and is flagged as a data gap rather than estimated.

Caveats

  • Royalties only. This excludes bonus bids and rents, which are lease-acquisition costs paid regardless of whether a lease ever produces — including them would inflate the denominator's apparent "return" for states with heavy speculative leasing activity but muddy the production-based question actually asked.
  • Nominal dollars throughout; the FY2022-2024 spike is partly (not entirely) inflation, since headline commodity prices rose faster than CPI over that window.
  • FY2021-2023 national acreage totals were reconstructed by this analysis (BLM's own spreadsheet leaves those cells as unevaluated formulas); the reconstruction was validated by exactly reproducing BLM's separately published FY2024 total.
  • Offshore federal leasing was excluded from all per-acre figures for lack of a leased-acreage denominator; offshore royalty revenue ($6.4B, FY2024) is reported separately above.
  • State grain masks within-state variation. New Mexico's own federal leasehold spans both the highly productive Permian (southeast) and much less productive acreage elsewhere in the state; a county- or basin-level cut would show even more spread than the state figures above.

What This Report Does Not Answer

  • Offshore leasing: Offshore royalty revenue reported ($6.4B FY2024) but no per-acre figure computed — a reliable BOEM offshore leased-acreage denominator was not available in this session; flagged as a data gap rather than estimated or guessed
