Yes — Electric Cars Still Cut Emissions Even on Coal- and Gas-Heavy Grids, but the Margin Shrinks Sharply

EIA state generation mix (2024) cross-checked against UCS/Argonne GREET lifecycle analysis using eGRID2024 data

Summary

Yes. In every U.S. state — including the most coal-dependent ones like West Virginia (84% coal generation in 2024), Kentucky (67%), and Wyoming (60%) — driving the average electric vehicle produces meaningfully lower greenhouse-gas emissions than driving a gasoline car, even after counting the emissions from burning coal and gas to make the electricity. The margin is much smaller than in clean-grid states, but it does not disappear or reverse anywhere in the continental U.S.

The clearest authoritative figure: in West Virginia, the state with the dirtiest grid in the country, the Union of Concerned Scientists (using Argonne National Laboratory's GREET lifecycle model and EPA eGRID data) finds a gasoline car would need to get 62 miles per gallon to match the emissions of the average EV charged there — nearly triple the 25.6 mpg the average new 2024 gasoline car actually achieves. Even the most efficient EV sold, charged on the single dirtiest grid region in the country, still beats the most fuel-efficient gasoline car on the market (a 57-mpg Toyota Prius) everywhere without exception, per UCS's 2026 update.

This analysis's own calculation from EIA 2024 state generation data (a simpler, generation-mix-only proxy, not a full lifecycle model) points the same direction but is more conservative — it estimates roughly 275 g CO2/mile for an EV in West Virginia versus roughly 409 g/mile for the average new gasoline car nationally, i.e., about a one-third reduction, smaller than UCS's more rigorous lifecycle estimate. Both figures agree on the finding: even the very worst U.S. grid does not erase the emissions advantage of driving electric.

Why coal-heavy grids don't flip the answer

Two things work in the EV's favor even where the grid is dirty:

  • EVs are far more energy-efficient per mile. A gasoline engine wastes 70-80% of the fuel's energy as heat; an EV converts roughly 85-90% of grid electricity into motion. ICCT estimates BEVs use only about one-fourth the primary energy of a comparable gasoline car per mile. That efficiency gap is large enough to absorb a lot of grid "dirtiness" before parity is reached.
  • No state's grid is 100% coal. Even West Virginia gets roughly 8% of its generation from non-fossil sources, and most "coal-heavy" states blend in substantial natural gas (roughly half the CO2 per MWh of coal) and some nuclear, hydro, or wind. The gasoline side of the comparison also carries its own significant upstream emissions — extraction, transport, and refining — that are easy to overlook when only counting tailpipe exhaust.

Where the picture genuinely gets tighter: our own generation-mix estimate for West Virginia (275 g/mile) sits above the 184 g/mile mark for the most fuel-efficient gasoline car sold today (a 57-mpg Prius), meaning a WV-charged average EV plausibly does not beat the single most efficient non-plug-in gasoline car available — only the average or typical new gasoline car. UCS's more careful lifecycle accounting (which credits gasoline with none of the upstream-extraction benefit of doubt) still finds even the least-efficient EV beating the most-efficient gas car, so the two methods disagree at the margin on this narrower claim, though not on the headline question.

Methodology and caveats

  • This analysis's EV estimate is a simplified proxy, not a full lifecycle model: it applies flat emission factors (2,200 lb CO2/MWh for coal, 900 lb CO2/MWh for gas — typical U.S. fleet averages) to each state's 2024 coal+gas generation share, assumes 0.30 kWh/mile EV efficiency and a 5% transmission-and-distribution loss, and treats nuclear/hydro/wind/other generation as zero-carbon. It does not include upstream fuel-extraction emissions (coal mining, gas well leakage) or vehicle/battery manufacturing emissions, and it uses each state's average annual generation mix rather than the marginal generation that actually responds to added EV charging load.
  • Marginal vs. average generation matters and is not captured here. If new EV charging demand is met disproportionately by natural-gas peaker plants (common on many grids, especially at night when EVs often charge) rather than by the average generation mix, the real-world emissions benefit could be smaller than either estimate above suggests. Neither this analysis nor the cited UCS study models marginal dispatch.
  • The UCS figures are the more authoritative source for the actual headline claim: they come from a peer-reviewed lifecycle methodology (Reichmuth, Dunn & Anair 2022, and the 2026 eGRID2024-based update) that includes upstream emissions on both the electricity and gasoline sides, which is why they show a bigger EV advantage than this analysis's simplified generation-only proxy.
  • Trend, not snapshot: coal generation has been declining nationally for over a decade, so this margin is set to widen further even in the states named here, as several sources note.
