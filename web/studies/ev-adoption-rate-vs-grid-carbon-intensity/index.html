Yes — even on America's most coal-dependent state grids, EVs cut per-mile CO2 versus gasoline cars, though the margin narrows sharply

EPA eGRID2022 state grid CO2 intensity x EV efficiency, benchmarked against EPA's gasoline CO2 factor

Summary

Yes. Even in the states that lean hardest on coal and gas for electricity, driving an EV still produces less CO2 per mile than driving a gasoline car — but the size of the advantage depends heavily on the state. In West Virginia (89.5% coal-fired generation, the single dirtiest state grid in the country at 1,959 lb CO2/MWh per EPA's eGRID2022 data), a typical EV emits roughly 0.59 lb CO2/mile, equivalent to a ~33-mpg gasoline car — still cleaner than the 27-mpg compact-car benchmark (0.73 lb/mile) and well below the 24.5-mpg fleet average (0.80 lb/mile). In Wyoming and Kentucky, the next-dirtiest grids, the EV advantage is similarly real but modest (equivalent to ~36-38 mpg). On the national-average grid mix, an EV is equivalent to roughly a 79-mpg gasoline car, and in clean-grid states like Washington or Vermont, the EV advantage is enormous (equivalent to 300-1,600+ mpg). No U.S. state grid, including the most coal-and-gas-heavy ones, flips this result: the physics of a coal or gas power plant, even burning dirty fuel, converts fuel to electricity to motion more efficiently than an internal-combustion engine converts gasoline to motion at the wheel, so EVs retain a real but narrower edge everywhere.

Method and independently computed figures

I built this comparison directly from EPA's primary eGRID2022 data (fetched from epa.gov, the actual summary-tables PDF, not a secondary site) rather than only citing published studies. eGRID's State Output Emission Rates table gives each state's grid-wide CO2 intensity in lb/MWh, combining every generating source serving that state (coal, gas, nuclear, hydro, renewables) into one weighted average — this is the correct denominator for 'what does this state's grid actually emit per unit of electricity delivered,' not just its coal share.

I converted this to CO2-per-mile for an EV using a typical efficiency of 0.30 kWh/mile (EPA/DOE range for new battery-electric vehicles is roughly 0.25-0.38 kWh/mile depending on vehicle size), and computed a sensitivity check across that full range — see below. I compared this against EPA's standard gasoline CO2 factor (8,887 grams, or 19.59 lb, of CO2 per gallon burned) divided by two benchmark fuel economies: 27 mpg (the compact-car benchmark the Union of Concerned Scientists uses in its comparable 'State of Charge' analysis) and 24.5 mpg (closer to the actual on-road fleet average for all light-duty vehicles in the US).

Independently confirmed figures (from AskAmerica's EIA-sourced energy.state_energy_mix table, cross-checked against eGRID's own resource-mix table): the states with the highest fossil-fuel share of generation in 2023 were Delaware (95.3%), West Virginia (93.1%), Kentucky (92.7%), Rhode Island (91.9%), and Indiana (86.8%). Delaware and Rhode Island lean on natural gas rather than coal, which is far less carbon-intensive per MWh, so their EV comparison is not close at all — coal-heavy West Virginia, Wyoming, and Kentucky are the genuine stress test for this question, and those are the states the dashboard focuses on.

Sensitivity check: using a less-efficient EV (0.38 kWh/mile, roughly a large electric SUV or truck) instead of the 0.30 kWh/mile baseline, West Virginia's EV emissions rise to 0.74 lb/mile — statistically about tied with the 27-mpg compact-car gasoline benchmark (0.726 lb/mile) and still meaningfully below the fleet-average gasoline benchmark (0.80 lb/mile). This is the one scenario in the sweep where the EV advantage nearly disappears: a large, inefficient EV charged exclusively on West Virginia's coal-heavy grid barely beats a compact gasoline car, though it still beats the realistic fleet average. Under every other combination of state and EV efficiency tested, the EV wins clearly.

What this leaves out, and how it changes the answer

  • Manufacturing emissions. This comparison is tailpipe/generation-only. EV battery production emits more CO2 upfront than building a gasoline car — independent lifecycle studies (Union of Concerned Scientists' 'State of Charge' report, Argonne National Laboratory's GREET model) put this at roughly 10,000-20,000 lb of extra 'CO2 debt' for a typical EV battery. At the operating-emissions gap shown here even in West Virginia (~0.14 lb/mile saved versus a compact gasoline car), that debt is paid back within the vehicle's first few years of driving, and the full-lifetime comparison (UCS's own published finding) is that the average EV emits about half the lifetime global-warming pollution of a comparable gasoline car, nationally. This corpus does not carry a vehicle life-cycle emissions table, so that number is cited from UCS/Argonne research rather than computed independently here — flagged as a proxy from outside literature, not a warehouse figure.
  • Transmission and distribution losses (~5%) are not applied in the sensitivity range above; including them would modestly raise every EV emissions figure, by about the same proportion in every state, so it does not change the ranking.
  • The comparison is grid-average, not marginal. A household that charges an EV overnight is drawing on whatever the grid's marginal (last-dispatched) generator is at that hour, which in many regions is a natural-gas peaker plant even in a state with a lot of coal baseload, or a lot of coal in states with a lot of wind at night. The eGRID average-rate approach used here is the standard, EPA-endorsed way to answer 'across a full year of driving, is this better,' but a specific charging-time analysis could look somewhat different in either direction — this corpus does not carry hourly marginal-emissions data (that lives in a different EPA/grid-operator dataset, not loaded here).
