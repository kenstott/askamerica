Yes — EVs still cut emissions even on coal-and-gas-heavy grids, but the margin shrinks from ~85% down to ~20-40%

EPA eGRID2023 state grid CO2 rates vs. operational EV and gasoline-car emissions

Summary

Yes, but with real caveats. In every state we checked — including the most coal-dependent grids in the country (West Virginia, Wyoming, Kentucky) — an average electric vehicle running on that state's own grid still produces fewer grams of CO2 per mile than the average gasoline car currently on U.S. roads. But the size of that advantage varies enormously: from roughly an 85-98% cut on very clean grids (Vermont, Washington, California) down to only about 20-25% on the dirtiest coal grids (West Virginia, Wyoming). Against a fuel-efficient new gasoline car (~40 mpg) rather than the average car on the road, the EV's edge in West Virginia and Wyoming nearly disappears in this operating-emissions-only comparison. And this is an operating emissions comparison only — it excludes vehicle manufacturing, where EV battery production adds meaningfully more upfront CO2 than building a gasoline car, which independent published lifecycle studies show pushes the coal-state break-even point out to 30,000+ miles of driving before the EV pulls fully ahead.

How coal/gas-heavy the identified states actually are

Using AskAmerica's EIA electricity-generation data (energy.state_energy_mix / energy.eia_electricity_generation, most recently reliable full year loaded = 2017; the table has an interior gap for 2018-2025 and a 2026 partial-year figure that looked internally inconsistent — e.g. Wyoming's fossil share appeared to drop to 54% in the 2026 partial data, which does not match any independent source and was treated as unreliable rather than reported), the most fossil-fuel-reliant state grids by generation share (coal+gas) were: Delaware, West Virginia, Rhode Island, Kentucky, Indiana, Wyoming, Utah, New Mexico, Missouri, Florida, Colorado, Mississippi, Louisiana, Texas, Ohio — all above 83% fossil generation in that year. This ranking was cross-checked against the EPA's own eGRID2023 state resource-mix table (the authoritative, most current federal source, vintage 2023 generation year) fetched directly: West Virginia (85.7% coal), Wyoming (71.4% coal + 4.9% gas), Kentucky (68.6% coal + 23.9% gas), Missouri (60.5% coal), Indiana (45.5% coal + 38.7% gas), and Utah (45.4% coal + 36.1% gas) are the clearest examples of grids still dominated by coal and gas in the most recent published data.

Compared against a gasoline benchmark of 364 g CO2/mile (EPA's 8,887 g CO2/gallon divided by 24.4 mpg, roughly the current U.S. on-road light-duty fleet average fuel economy), the EV wins everywhere: by about 20% in West Virginia and Wyoming, ~29-42% in Kentucky/Indiana/Missouri/Utah, and by 56-98% in Ohio, Texas, the national average, and the cleaner-grid states. Against a much more fuel-efficient new gasoline car (~40 mpg, ~222 g/mile) — a tougher, more conservative test — West Virginia's EV edge shrinks to under 24%, and Wyoming's shrinks to essentially a rough tie (roughly +19% for the EV), because at that point the two options are separated mainly by how dirty that specific state's marginal generation is.

What the published literature adds beyond this operating-only comparison

The ICCT's 2024 lifecycle GHG analysis of U.S. sedans/SUVs finds that model year 2024 battery-electric vehicles have life-cycle emissions 66-74% below comparable gasoline vehicles depending on the carbon intensity of the local grid, and that this holds across the full range of U.S. grids, including coal-heavy ones — a wider margin than our operating-only numbers because it double-counts the gasoline car's much larger fuel-cycle emissions (extraction, refining, distribution) that our simple tailpipe-only benchmark leaves out. A peer-reviewed 2025 study (Reichmuth et al., PMC12461929) and a 2025 trade-press analysis both report that EVs have lower life-cycle GHG emissions than gasoline vehicles in every U.S. county, but that the manufacturing break-even point — the mileage at which the EV's larger upfront battery-manufacturing footprint is paid back by its lower running emissions — stretches from roughly 7,000-8,000 miles in very clean-grid states (Vermont) to more than 30,000 miles in coal-heavy states (Wyoming, West Virginia). That means a driver in a coal state who replaces their gas car with an EV every 3-4 years, or who drives unusually little, could plausibly come out close to a wash or slightly behind on total lifecycle carbon versus keeping (or buying) a highly fuel-efficient gasoline car — a genuine, non-trivial caveat this analysis's operating-only numbers do not by themselves capture, but which the literature is explicit about.

Data-quality note (AskAmerica corpus)

AskAmerica's own EIA electricity-generation table (energy.eia_electricity_generation / energy.state_energy_mix) has a real gap: full years are only loaded for 2013-2017, then nothing until a 2026 partial year, and that 2026 partial-year data looked internally implausible for at least one state (Wyoming's fossil share appeared to have collapsed from ~92% to ~54% year over year, which does not match any independent source on Wyoming's grid). We did not use that 2026 data for anything in this report and instead relied on EPA's own eGRID2023 summary tables, fetched directly from epa.gov, for all current-year figures.
