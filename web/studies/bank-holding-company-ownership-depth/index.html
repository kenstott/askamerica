Foreign-owned banks sit under 2-4 layers of holding companies, and control about 17% of US banking system assets

Federal Reserve IBA structure/share data and GLEIF corporate ownership graph, Q1 2026

Summary

Ownership depth: a foreign-owned US bank typically sits 2 to 4 corporate layers below its ultimate foreign parent -- not one hop, as a simple '% foreign owned' framing implies. Verified examples pulled directly from GLEIF's reported consolidation edges: HSBC Bank USA sits 4 layers below HSBC Holdings plc (HSBC Bank USA → HSBC USA Inc. → HSBC North America Holdings Inc. → HSBC Overseas Holdings (UK) Ltd. → HSBC Holdings plc, London), and Santander Bank, N.A. sits 2 layers below Banco Santander S.A. (Santander Bank N.A. → Santander Holdings USA, Inc. → Banco Santander S.A., Spain). Across GLEIF's full reported corporate ownership graph (not banks alone), chains can run up to 9 hops deep, but the distribution collapses fast: of ~118,000 entities with a reported parent, 76% sit only 1 hop from their immediate parent and 98% resolve within 4 hops -- so a 4-layer bank like HSBC is already deeper than nearly all reported corporate structures, consistent with academic findings that bank holding company hierarchy depth is 'small' and doesn't vary much across firms even as total subsidiary counts do.

Foreign share of the system: as of Q1 2026, foreign banking organizations (FBOs) -- foreign-owned US-chartered bank subsidiaries plus US branches/agencies of foreign banks -- hold 17.0% of total assets ($4.87 trillion of $28.68 trillion) in the Federal Reserve's tracked commercial banking universe: 4.8% in foreign-owned US bank subsidiaries and 12.2% in foreign branches/agencies. That combined share peaked around 21.9% in 2007 and has drifted down since the 2008 financial crisis and subsequent Dodd-Frank/IHC (intermediate holding company) regulation. Foreign banks are disproportionately active in commercial lending: they hold 25.1% of commercial and industrial (C&I) loans, well above their 17.0% asset share, but only 11.9% of deposits, reflecting their reliance on wholesale funding rather than the retail deposit base.

This is the whole corporate universe GLEIF tracks (roughly 89,000 distinct entities with a reported parent), not banks specifically -- GLEIF does not tag entities by industry, so there is no clean way to isolate 'all US banks' depth distribution from this table alone. To ground the question in real bank cases, two large foreign-owned US banks were traced individually:

  • HSBC Bank USA, N.A. → HSBC USA Inc. (depth 1) → HSBC North America Holdings Inc. (depth 2) → HSBC Overseas Holdings (UK) Limited (depth 3) → HSBC Holdings plc, London, the ultimate parent (depth 4).
  • Santander Bank, N.A. → Santander Holdings USA, Inc. (depth 1) → Banco Santander S.A., Spain, the ultimate parent (depth 2).

Two other large OCC-supervised foreign-owned banks named in press/regulatory coverage -- BMO Bank, N.A. and MUFG Union Bank, N.A. -- returned no rows in GLEIF's direct-consolidation table, meaning their parent, BMO Financial Group and Mitsubishi UFJ Financial Group respectively, has not filed a reportable direct-consolidation edge for that US entity in GLEIF's Golden Copy publish; this is a reporting gap in GLEIF, not evidence those banks are unowned, and is disclosed here rather than papered over.

The OFR/NBER research literature on this question (Flood, Kenett, Lumsdaine & Simon, 'The Complexity of Bank Holding Companies: A New Measurement Approach,' OFR Working Paper 17-03, and its companion 'A Topological Approach') finds the same qualitative pattern: maximum ownership-chain depth grows with a bank holding company's size, but 'tends to be small and lacks sufficient cross-firm variation' -- complexity in large BHCs shows up mainly in the sheer NUMBER of subsidiaries (into the thousands for the largest US banks) rather than in how many layers deep the chain runs. That is consistent with what both GLEIF and the two worked bank examples show here: 2-4 layers is typical even for globally systemic banking groups.

Two structural forms of 'foreign' presence are combined here and are worth distinguishing: (1) foreign-owned US bank subsidiaries are separately US-chartered, FDIC-insured banks (like HSBC Bank USA and Santander Bank N.A. above) that happen to sit under a foreign parent through the multi-layer chains described above; (2) US branches and agencies of foreign banks are not separately incorporated US entities at all -- they are direct extensions of the foreign bank's own balance sheet, uninsured by the FDIC, and (per Federal Reserve research, Liberty Street Economics, Jan. 2023) they fund themselves through wholesale markets rather than deposits and are the marginal price-setters for short-term dollar liquidity. Branches/agencies are the larger of the two foreign categories (12.2% of assets vs. 4.8%) and dominate foreign banks' outsized 25.1% share of C&I lending.

Over time, the combined foreign share has moved with the cycle: it climbed from 10.8% of assets in 1980 to a series peak of roughly 21.9% in December 2007, just before the financial crisis, then declined through the 2010s as US regulators (via Dodd-Frank's intermediate holding company requirement for large FBOs, effective 2016) tightened capital and liquidity rules on US operations of foreign banks. It has held in the 17%-18% range since roughly 2020 and stood at 17.0% in Q1 2026.
