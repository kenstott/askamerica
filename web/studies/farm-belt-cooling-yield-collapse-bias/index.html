The premise is false: Corn Belt corn yields have risen ~37% since 1994, not fallen — and growing-season temperatures show no significant drop

USDA NASS QuickStats corn yield, 11-state Corn Belt, 1994-2025; farmdoc daily (Univ. of Illinois) Corn Belt temperature trend analysis, 1895-2023

Summary

The premise is false. Crop yields in the American farm belt have not fallen — they have risen sharply, and growing-season temperatures have not measurably dropped. USDA data show the 11-state Corn Belt's average corn yield rose from 137.3 bushels/acre in 1994 to 187.6 bushels/acre in 2025 — up 36.7% in just the last 32 years, a statistically strong upward trend (+1.97 bu/acre per year, R²=0.74). Zoom out further and the rise is even more dramatic: national corn yields were around 20-30 bu/acre in the 1930s-40s and are near 180 bu/acre today, roughly a six-fold increase, driven mainly by genetics, hybrid seed, fertilizer, and precision agriculture — not by climate. On the temperature side, the best available long-run analysis of Corn Belt growing-season weather (1895-2023 and 1980-2023) finds essentially no statistically significant trend in the months that matter most for corn yield; the one exception found is a significant WARMING in September, not a cooling. A separate, real phenomenon exists — the Midwest 'warming hole,' a documented pocket of summer cooling relative to the global warming trend — but published research on it found this cooling coincided with a 5-10% per year yield BOOST, the opposite of what the question assumes.

What the yield data actually show

Using USDA NASS QuickStats (ag.nass_crop_production), filtered to corn grain yield (bu/acre) for the 11 states most identified with the Corn Belt/American farm belt (Iowa, Illinois, Indiana, Nebraska, Minnesota, Ohio, Wisconsin, South Dakota, Missouri, Kansas, Kentucky):

  • 1994 average yield: 137.3 bu/acre
  • 2025 average yield: 187.6 bu/acre
  • Change: +50.4 bu/acre, +36.7%
  • Linear trend: +1.97 bu/acre per year, R² = 0.74 (n = 32 years)

Year-to-year dips are visible (1995, 2002, 2012-13, 2019) and correspond to well-known drought or excess-moisture years, but there is no multi-decade downward trend anywhere in the series — the opposite is true. This is consistent with USDA's own national corn yield trend line, which NASS and university agricultural economists (e.g., farmdoc's long-running trend-yield model) have tracked rising at roughly 1.8-2.0 bu/acre/year for decades.

What the temperature data actually show

Askamerica's own weather tables (weather.cdo_monthly_summaries, weather.ghcnd_daily) only reach back to 2009-2010 — far too short a window to test a 'last several decades' temperature claim, so that clause is addressed from the primary published literature rather than a warehouse query, and is flagged as such here rather than presented as a corpus-derived figure.

The most rigorous public treatment is Scott Irwin's farmdoc daily analysis (University of Illinois, May 2024), which used NWS/Midwest Regional Climate Center monthly data for the 10 core Corn Belt states over both 1980-2023 and the full 1895-2023 record. Of 12 growing-season temperature and precipitation trend variables tested, only ONE was statistically significant: September temperature rose 3.1°F over 1980-2023. July temperature — the single most important variable for U.S. average corn yield — showed only a slight, not-statistically-significant downward trend in both sample periods. The author's own conclusion: 'there is very little evidence of significant upward or downward trends in the monthly temperature and precipitation variables that are most crucial for determining the U.S. average corn yield' over more than a century of Corn Belt records.

Separately, a real and well-studied phenomenon called the U.S. 'warming hole' does describe genuine summer cooling in the Midwest relative to the global warming trend (attributed to increased rainfall, aerosols, irrigation, and land-use/agricultural intensification effects). Dartmouth-linked research on this phenomenon, reported by Columbia's State of the Planet and phys.org, found it coincided with a 5-10% per year corn yield boost — climate cooling in this specific, narrow sense has been associated with higher yields, not lower ones, further undercutting the question's implied link.

Bottom line

Neither half of the question's premise holds up against the data. Crop yields in the Corn Belt have risen substantially, not fallen, over the last several decades, and the best long-run temperature record shows no significant growing-season cooling trend — with the one real, narrower cooling phenomenon in the literature (the 'warming hole') actually associated with higher yields. The question's implied cause-and-effect (cooling growing seasons -> falling yields) is not supported in either direction by the available evidence.
