The premise is backwards: farm-belt growing-season temperatures have not fallen, and yields have risen sharply, not fallen

USDA NASS corn yields, 1995-2025 (10-state Corn Belt); NOAA/peer-reviewed climate literature on Midwest summer temperature trends

Summary

The question's premise does not hold. Growing-season/summer temperatures across the American farm belt (the Corn Belt states of the upper Midwest) have not broadly fallen over the last several decades — most published analyses find little to no significant multi-decade trend in the monthly temperature variables that drive Corn Belt crop weather, going back over a century. There is one real, narrower phenomenon that gets conflated with 'cooling': peer-reviewed research (Mueller et al. 2015, Nature Climate Change) found that summer daytime temperature extremes (the hottest days) declined modestly in parts of the Corn Belt over the 20th century, attributed to cropland intensification (more irrigation and transpiration from denser, higher-yielding crops) and increased precipitation — not a general cooling of the growing season.

And crop yields have done the opposite of falling: U.S. corn yields hit a record 177.3 bushels/acre in 2023, up from under 40 bu/acre in 1950 and roughly 115-140 bu/acre as recently as the mid-1990s — a rise driven overwhelmingly by genetics, hybrid seed technology, fertilizer, and precision management, which have swamped any weather signal, including the muted-heat-extreme effect, which if anything modestly helped yields by reducing heat stress.

So: growing-season temperatures have not dropped in any broad sense, and yields have risen substantially, not fallen. Below is the evidence for each half of that correction, plus the caveats on the connector data used.

Step 1 — Did growing-season temperatures actually fall in the farm belt?

No broad decades-long cooling is documented. The most relevant lines of evidence:

  • farmdoc daily (Univ. of Illinois, May 2024) examined monthly temperature and precipitation trends for the U.S. Corn Belt back to 1895 and found 'very little evidence of significant upward or downward trends' in the temperature and precipitation variables that drive U.S. average corn yield — a finding the authors say holds over more than a century, not just recent decades.
  • Mueller et al. 2015 (Nature Climate Change), 'Cooling of US Midwest summer temperature extremes from cropland intensification,' found a real, but narrow, effect: summer daytime highs (temperature extremes, not the whole growing-season average) declined in parts of the Corn Belt over the 20th century, statistically linked to cropland intensification (irrigation/transpiration) and rising precipitation. This is a regional, extremes-specific finding — not evidence that overall growing-season temperatures fell.
  • USDA ERS reports that Corn Belt temperatures have in fact been warming and are expected to continue warming into the next decade, and each decade since 1992 has seen increasing growing degree days and extreme degree days.
  • Reconciling these: nighttime lows and winter temperatures have risen; some parts of the Midwest have seen slightly lower summer daytime maximums due to the cropland-intensification/precipitation effect described above; net effect on the growing-season mean has been close to flat to modestly warming, not a multi-decade decline.

Data-quality note on this connector: we attempted to verify this directly from weather.weather_daily_by_county (NOAA GHCND daily observations aggregated to county), but data_coverage showed severe interior gaps — several years in the 2012-2019 span have on the order of 1% of the row count of neighboring years (e.g., 2014 had ~8,200 rows vs. ~1.08 million in 2015) — indicating a partial/broken ingest for those years, not a real absence of weather station reports. We did not compute a trend from that table because a regression across those gaps would silently interpolate across missing data and could manufacture a false signal. The conclusion above rests on the peer-reviewed literature instead.

Step 2 — Have crop yields fallen?

No — the opposite. USDA NASS/ERS's own published national corn yield series (not subject to the connector's state-coverage gaps) shows yields rising from under 40 bu/acre in 1950 to a record 177.3 bu/acre in 2023, with a historical trend rate of about 1.9 bu/acre per year from 1980-2023 (slowing to roughly 1 bu/acre per year in the 2013-2023 sub-period, per farmdoc daily's trend-rate analysis — a deceleration in the RATE of gain, not a decline in yield itself).

We independently pulled a 10-state Corn Belt average (IA, IL, IN, KS, MN, MO, NE, OH, SD, WI) from ag.nass_crop_production in this connector as a cross-check: it shows the same qualitative pattern — yields around 115-140 bu/acre in the mid/late 1990s, rising to a range of roughly 178-196 bu/acre from 2016 onward (with the well-known 2012 drought producing the one sharp dip, at 97.5 bu/acre, consistent with that year's severe Midwest drought, not a temperature trend). See the chart above.

Data-quality note: this connector's ag.nass_crop_production table has patchy year-by-year state coverage for the Corn Belt states specifically (e.g., only 1-2 of the 10 states have rows in several years, versus all 10 in others; total table row counts by year swing from ~11,000 to ~258,000 with no coverage-window explanation), so the state-average figures above should be read as directional corroboration, not a precise annual series. We did not attempt to compute national yield from this connector because it carries no 'US' aggregate row (state/county grain only, per its own description) and a sum-based national reconstruction from the same table produced physically implausible acreages (e.g., 258 million corn acres implied for 2017, versus USDA's actual ~90 million U.S. corn acres that year) — a sign of double-counting across overlapping source_desc/util_practice_desc rows in that year's ingest, which we discarded rather than reported. The record-yield figures cited above (177.3 bu/acre, 2023; the 1950 and long-run trend figures) come directly from USDA NASS/ERS's own published series, not from this reconstruction.

Bottom line

Neither clause of the question's premise holds up: growing-season temperatures in the American farm belt have not broadly fallen over the last several decades (a narrower, well-documented cooling of summer daytime extremes in parts of the Corn Belt is real but is not a decline in overall growing-season temperature, and nighttime/winter warming has been the dominant broader trend), and corn yields have risen sharply over the same period — to record levels — driven by technology and genetics rather than by weather. If anything, the muted summer heat extremes documented by Mueller et al. would be expected to have modestly helped yields (less heat stress), not hurt them.

What This Report Does Not Answer

  • Quantify how much crop yields have fallen: Yields have not fallen — they have risen sharply (record levels). We report the actual magnitude of the RISE instead, since the premised decline does not exist. This is a deliberate premise correction, not an omission.
  • Attribute any yield change to the temperature trend (causal claim implied by 'as ... have dropped'): Not applicable once the premise is corrected: temperatures did not broadly drop, so there is no such causal relationship to quantify. The one documented, narrower temperature effect (muted summer extremes in parts of the Corn Belt) is noted as plausibly yield-helping, not yield-harming.
