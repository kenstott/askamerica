Both premises of the question are false: Corn Belt growing-season temperatures have not dropped, and crop yields have not fallen — yields have risen for decades and hit a record in 2025

USDA NASS QuickStats (via AskAmerica ag.nass_crop_production) and peer-reviewed/university climate analyses of NOAA/NWS Midwest station data

Summary

Both halves of the premise are false. American farm-belt (Corn Belt) crop yields have not fallen over the last several decades — they have risen steadily, at about 1.9 bushels per acre per year since the mid-1950s, and the U.S. set a fresh national record of 186.0 bu/acre in 2025, beating the prior record of 179.3 bu/acre set just one year earlier in 2024. Meanwhile, growing-season temperatures across the 10-state Corn Belt have not dropped over the last several decades either: a University of Illinois analysis of acreage-weighted NOAA/NWS station data for April-September, run over both 1980-2023 and the full 1895-2023 record, found only one statistically significant temperature trend out of sixteen tested — and that one (September) is a warming trend of +3.1°F since 1980, not a cooling one. There is no falling-yield magnitude to report because the direction assumed in the question does not hold in the data.

1. Crop yields: risen, not fallen

Purdue University's historical corn-yield series (from annual USDA-NASS Crop Production Reports, fetched directly) shows yields were flat at roughly 26 bu/acre from 1866 through the mid-1930s (open-pollinated varieties), then rose in two step-changes: to about 0.8 bu/acre/year after hybrid-corn adoption began in the late 1930s, and to about 1.9 bu/acre/year, sustained ever since, after further gains in fertilizer, pesticides, mechanization and genetics beginning in the mid-1950s. That 1.9 bu/acre/year trend has held for roughly 70 years with no significant deviation, including through the era of genetically modified traits since the mid-1990s.

Recent national averages continue that climb: 172.0 bu/acre (2020), 176.7 (2021), 173.4 (2022), 177.3 (2023), 179.3 (2024), and a record 186.0 bu/acre (2025).

This corpus's own USDA NASS QuickStats mirror (ag.nass_crop_production) shows the same rising pattern at the state level for Iowa, the largest corn-producing state, over the years it has actually loaded (2008, 2010-2013, 2015-2017, 2020, 2025): yield rose from 171 bu/acre in 2008 to a state record 210 bu/acre in 2025, with the low point (137 bu/acre) in the 2012 national drought year — a known, temporary weather shock, not a long-run decline.

2. Growing-season temperatures: no cooling trend

A University of Illinois (farmdoc daily) analysis by Scott Irwin, fetched directly from the primary article, built acreage-weighted monthly temperature and precipitation series for the 10 largest corn-producing states (Iowa, Illinois, Indiana, Minnesota, Missouri, Nebraska, North Dakota, Ohio, South Dakota, Wisconsin) using NOAA National Weather Service station data via the Midwest Regional Climate Center, for April through September, over two windows: 1980-2023 and the full available record back to 1895.

Of the sixteen monthly temperature-trend coefficients tested across both windows, only one was statistically significant at the 5% level — September temperature over 1980-2023, which rose 3.1°F (a warming trend). Nine of the sixteen point estimates were negative (nominally cooling) but not statistically distinguishable from no trend at all; July temperature specifically showed a slight, non-significant downward drift since 1980, and a similarly slight, non-significant downward drift over the full 1895-2023 record. The single hottest and driest month in the entire 129-year record remains July 1936, during the Dust Bowl — a point the study uses to illustrate how much year-to-year variability swamps any long-run trend.

USDA's own Economic Research Service, by contrast, reports Corn Belt temperatures trending warmer since the early 1990s and projects continued warming — consistent with the farmdoc finding that the recent decades show mild warming (not cooling) even though the trend is not statistically significant over the noisier full-century window.

Net: there is no credible reading of the U.S. government or university climate record in which Corn Belt growing-season temperatures have dropped over the last several decades.

Data and method notes

What was queried directly in this corpus: ag.nass_crop_production (USDA NASS QuickStats mirror) for Iowa corn grain yield and production, filtered per the corpus's own aggregation-hazard guidance (agg_level_desc='STATE', util_practice_desc='GRAIN', domain_desc='TOTAL', source_desc='SURVEY', prodn_practice_desc='ALL PRODUCTION PRACTICES', statisticcat_desc='YIELD', unit_desc='BU / ACRE'). Even after pinning every qualifier, that filter returned only 10 of the 32 declared years for Iowa — a state that is NASS-surveyed every year without gap in reality — which was logged as a likely ETL/partition data-quality issue rather than presented as a real absence.

What was NOT available in this corpus: weather.weather_daily_by_county (the only county/temperature series in this connector) only covers 2010-2026 and has severe internal coverage holes in 2012/2014/2016/2017 (roughly 1% of a normal year's row count), so it cannot support a 'last several decades' growing-season temperature trend at all. That gap is why the temperature side of this answer relies on two independently fetched primary sources (a peer-reviewed-adjacent university climate analysis and Purdue's historical yield-trend page) rather than an in-corpus query — both were read directly via web_fetch, not taken from a search-result summary.

Caveat: 'Statistically insignificant' is not the same claim as 'zero' — the farmdoc analysis explicitly cautions that its monthly-average framing could mask trends visible in more granular (e.g. daily max/min, or sub-monthly) weather data, and that the absence of a trend to date is no guarantee against one emerging in the future.

What This Report Does Not Answer

  • Quantify 'how much' yields have fallen: Cannot be quantified because the premise is false in the direction it assumes — yields have risen, not fallen, so no falling-yield magnitude exists to report. The rising trend is quantified instead (~1.9 bu/acre/year since the mid-1950s; record 186.0 bu/acre in 2025).
