Import dependence rose sharply for capital goods, electronics and pharmaceuticals over the last 20 years, while it fell sharply for petroleum — the largest swing in either direction

BEA NIPA Table 4.2.5 (nominal end-use import categories, 2009-2025) plus EIA/Census petroleum data (2005-2025) and academic import-penetration research

Summary

Aggregate U.S. goods-import dependence (imports of goods as a share of GDP) barely moved over the last two decades — about 11% of GDP in both 2009 and 2025 — but that stability hides a large reshuffling underneath. Two categories moved the most, in opposite directions: petroleum went from the single biggest source of import dependence to a net export good (the U.S. was 60% import-dependent on foreign oil in 2005 and became a net petroleum exporter by 2020, exporting 2.8 million more barrels per day than it imported in 2025), while capital goods, computer/electronics equipment, and pharmaceutical preparations became dramatically more import-dependent, roughly doubling to tripling their share of GDP and, in pharmaceuticals' case, growing 8-fold in import volume since 2000. Manufacturing overall also became structurally more import-exposed: peer-reviewed research using confidential Census microdata found industry-average import penetration in manufacturing rose from about 10% to 20% of domestic sales between 1992 and 2012, a trend that has continued since in electronics and pharma specifically.

Data used and why

There is no single, freely downloadable, industry-level (NAICS) import-penetration series spanning a full 20-year window: Census's international-trade API needs a key this connector cannot self-serve, and BEA's own Import Matrix is an interactive web tool, not a downloadable series. Two substitutes were combined instead: (1) BEA NIPA Table 4.2.5, which breaks nominal goods imports into broad end-use categories (capital goods, consumer goods, automotive, industrial supplies, foods, etc.) and is loaded in the connector for 2009-2025 — a 16-year, not 20-year, window, which is disclosed rather than passed off as the full 20; and (2) sector-specific primary and secondary sources (EIA/Census petroleum trade statistics, and academic/think-tank research on pharmaceuticals and manufacturing import penetration) to fill in category detail and extend back to 2005 where BEA's connector-loaded window could not.

Each end-use import category's dollar value (BEA Table 4.2.5, import lines, identified by BEA line number ≥94 to distinguish them from the export lines that share the same category labels in that table) was divided by nominal GDP (BEA Table 1.1.5) for 2009 and 2025 to get each category's share of the economy, then the percentage-point change was computed directly — this is the same 'import penetration relative to the whole economy' logic used in the literature, applied at the coarser end-use grain that is actually available for a two-decade span.

Note every category's nominal dollar value of imports grew (inflation plus real economic growth), which is why 'nominal growth %' looks large even for categories whose share of GDP fell — industrial supplies and petroleum grew more slowly than GDP itself and so became relatively less import-dependent, while capital goods, computers and pharma grew far faster than GDP and became relatively more import-dependent. Industrial supplies' large negative pp swing (-1.24) is driven almost entirely by petroleum's collapse within that category (petroleum & products alone falling -1.15 pp), plus lower relative growth in metals and chemicals imports.

The two biggest true 20-year reversals, outside the 2009-2025 BEA window

Petroleum is the clearest and largest true reversal of import dependence over the full 20 years (2005-2025), and it predates the connector's 2009 BEA window: U.S. oil import dependence peaked at 60% of consumption in 2005, driven down over the following two decades by the shale/fracking boom, and the U.S. became a net exporter of petroleum in 2020 — by 2025 exporting about 2.8 million barrels per day more than it imported. No other single category comes close to this magnitude of reversal in either direction.

Pharmaceuticals is the clearest case of rising dependence on a single foreign supplier: U.S. pharmaceutical import volume rose roughly 8-fold, from about 100,000 metric tons in 2000 to over 800,000 metric tons in 2024, with China alone supplying about 40% of that import volume in 2024 (and roughly a third of generic active pharmaceutical ingredients coming from single-source suppliers, many in China) — a dependence concentrated in active pharmaceutical ingredients and generics rather than finished branded drugs.

Manufacturing broadly also became more import-exposed on average: Amiti and Heise's peer-reviewed study using confidential U.S. Census microdata found import penetration (imports as a share of domestic manufacturing sales) rose from about 10% to 20% between 1992 and 2012 — the trend that underlies the capital-goods, computer/electronics, and semiconductor increases visible in the BEA end-use data for the more recent 2009-2025 window.

Caveats

  • The BEA end-use categories are coarse groupings, not NAICS industries — 'capital goods, except automotive' bundles machinery, aircraft parts, and industrial equipment together, so the true finest-grain industry that moved most within it (e.g., civilian aircraft engines, up from $39.5B to $105.7B) cannot be cleanly separated from the rest of the category using only this table.
  • The BEA connector table's loaded window is 2009-2025 (16 years), not a full 20; the petroleum and pharmaceutical figures independently reach back to 2000-2005 using EIA/Census and CFR/Atlantic Council data to give the requested ~20-year comparison for the two categories with the clearest genuine reversals.
  • A rising nominal dollar value of imports is not the same as rising import dependence — dependence requires comparing imports against the whole economy or against domestic production, which is what the %-of-GDP and %-of-consumption figures above do; a raw dollar-growth ranking would be misleading (see table) since every category grew in nominal dollars.

What This Report Does Not Answer

  • Cover a genuine ~20-year window: The connector's own BEA NIPA table (econ.national_accounts, T40205B) only has a loaded window of 2009-2025 (16 years), not 20; this is disclosed and partially remedied by independently sourcing petroleum (2005-2025) and pharmaceuticals (2000-2024) from EIA/Census and CFR/Atlantic Council data to reach the true 20-year span for the two categories with the largest genuine reversals.
