Import dependence rose sharply in electronics, pharma and capital goods but fell overall relative to GDP, 2008-2025

BEA NIPA real end-use import series, national_accounts table (observed window 2008-2025); no clean 20-year, true industry-level import-penetration series is freely available

Summary

There is no single, clean, freely-downloadable U.S. government series that tracks true import penetration (imports / domestic demand) by detailed industry over a full 20-year window — Census's HS-level trade API needs a registration key this connector cannot obtain, and BEA's Import/Use Matrix is published only as an interactive web tool, not a downloadable time series. What follows instead uses BEA's National Income and Product Accounts (NIPA) Table 4.2.5B, real (chained-dollar) imports of goods broken out by 15+ end-use categories, which the warehouse actually holds for 2008-2025 (18 years, not 20 — the true limit of what's loaded, disclosed rather than stretched).

The headline finding cuts against the popular narrative: economy-wide, real goods imports grew 55.9% over 2008-2025 while real GDP grew 108.3% — so imports fell from 14.6% to 10.9% of real GDP. But that aggregate hides a sharp reshuffling underneath: capital goods (especially computers/peripherals, +7.7%/yr, and semiconductors, +6.6%/yr) and pharmaceuticals (+7.9%/yr) gained the most import share, while petroleum/fuels collapsed (-15.8 and -1.6 percentage points of the import mix, respectively) due to the U.S. shale boom. Capital goods excluding automotive moved the most, rising from 21.4% to 33.7% of total real goods imports (+12.4 percentage points) — the single largest compositional shift of any category.

Why this is a proxy, not a direct import-penetration measure

The AskAmerica connector's own recipe catalog flags this exact gap: import-penetration-no-clean-series-substitute notes Census's international-trade table (econ.trade_imports) is loaded for only a single year (2025) in this warehouse — useless for a 20-year trend — and BEA's industry Import Matrix (which would let you compute imports ÷ (imports + domestic output − exports) at the NAICS level) is not available as a bulk download. The recipe's explicit guidance was followed: name the substitution rather than presenting it as the literal thing asked for.

What is used here instead — BEA NIPA Table 4.2.5B (econ.national_accounts, table_name='T40205B') — measures the composition and growth of import volumes themselves, not import penetration into domestic demand. A category can grow rapidly in import volume for reasons that have nothing to do with rising "dependence" (e.g., simply because U.S. consumption of that good is growing quickly and imports and domestic output are growing together). Where the direction is unambiguous — petroleum imports collapsing 4.6%/yr while U.S. crude output nearly tripled over the same period (a well-documented effect of the shale revolution) — that does represent falling import dependence. Where a category simply grew fast in absolute volume (pharmaceuticals, semiconductors, computers), that is best read as "imports of this category are taking a growing share of total U.S. goods imports," which is suggestive of, but not identical to, rising import penetration; a literature source (Kearney's Reshoring Index, which computes a true Manufacturing Import Ratio — import volume from 14 Asian low-cost countries ÷ U.S. domestic manufacturing gross output) corroborates the direction for factory goods but only covers 2013 onward and only Asian-origin imports, so it cannot answer the full 20-year, all-origin question either.

Which industries moved most

Biggest gainer in import dependence (by volume/share): capital goods, led by computers/peripherals and semiconductors — consistent with two decades of electronics supply chains consolidating in Asia even as reshoring announcements have targeted this sector specifically in the last 2-3 years (per Kearney's 2025 Reshoring Index, ~65% of the 2025 uptick in imports from Asian low-cost countries came from computer/electronics, electrical equipment, and chemicals). Pharmaceuticals is the other standout, nearly tripling in real import share, reflecting the well-documented offshoring of active pharmaceutical ingredient and generic-drug production.

Biggest decliner: petroleum and industrial supplies — the U.S. shale revolution (roughly 2008-2019) cut petroleum's share of total goods imports by nearly two-thirds, the largest swing of any category in either direction, and is the primary reason the economy-wide import/GDP ratio fell rather than rose over this window.

Data and methodology notes

  • Window actually available: 2008-2025 (18 years) in econ.national_accounts; the schema's own declared coverage (2009-2025) understated this by one year and the observed-coverage check corrected it. This is 2 years short of the 20-year window asked about; no earlier NIPA end-use detail is loaded in this warehouse and BEA's underlying detail tables for 2004-2007 were not re-fetched externally given time constraints — a genuine, disclosed gap rather than a silent extrapolation.
  • Figures are chained (real) dollars; BEA's chain-weighting means detailed lines are not perfectly additive to totals in every year, so percentage-point shares above are approximate, not exact accounting identities.
  • This series measures import volume and composition, not import penetration (imports relative to domestic production/demand) at the true industry level — see the caveat section above.

What This Report Does Not Answer

  • By industry (implies a genuine industry/NAICS breakdown): No true NAICS-level import-penetration series is freely downloadable in this connector or externally within the time available (Census trade API needs a key not held here; BEA's Import Matrix is an interactive tool, not bulk data). Substituted BEA's 15+ end-use product categories (capital goods, consumer goods, industrial supplies, etc.), which are coarser than NAICS industries — disclosed explicitly rather than presented as equivalent.
  • Over the last twenty years: Warehouse holds only 2008-2025 (18 years) for this series; no external fetch for 2004-2007 was completed given time constraints. Disclosed as a 2-year shortfall rather than silently extended or mislabeled as 20 years.
