No, aggregate bank deposit growth has not slowed since the GENIUS Act — and the one bank-level natural experiment finds the opposite for stablecoin-partner banks

GENIUS Act signed July 18, 2025; Fed M1/M2 data through July 2026; NY Fed and Board research on stablecoin-exposed banks

Summary

No. There is no evidence yet that deposit growth has slowed, in the aggregate, at banks most exposed to stablecoin competition since the GENIUS Act gave federal stablecoin regulation a clearer shape (signed July 18, 2025; implementing rules from OCC/Treasury/FDIC still in proposed rulemaking as of September 2026, with the Act not fully effective until January 2027 or 120 days after final rules issue). Fed M1 money supply — the broadest simple proxy for transaction/demand deposits — grew slightly faster in the 12 months after the Act's signing (+5.8%) than in the 12 months before (+4.2%). The one study that actually isolates banks exposed to stablecoin activity (New York Fed, Lee & Tou, "Stablecoin Disintermediation," Feb 2026) finds those banks' deposits and assets grew more than peer banks, not less — the effect shows up in a shift of that money into reserves and away from loans (loan-to-asset ratio down ~14 percentage points relative to controls), not in slower deposit growth. Two structural reasons this null result is unsurprising as of today: (1) dollar stablecoins (~$308B) are still only about 1.5% the size of M1 (~$19.9T), too small to move the aggregate; and (2) the regulatory framework, while clearer in shape since July 2025, is still mid-implementation, so any deposit-substitution response from banks or customers is likely still building.

What "exposed to stablecoin competition" means, and why it matters for the answer

The research literature actually distinguishes two different kinds of bank "exposure," and they point in different directions:

  • Competitive exposure — banks whose retail transaction-deposit franchises are most substitutable by stablecoins (younger, digitally-native customer bases, high share of non-interest checking). Federal Reserve Board economist Jessie Jiaxu Wang's FEDS Note (Dec 17, 2025), "Banks in the Age of Stablecoins," lays out the theory: domestic substitution of deposits into stablecoins could reduce deposits, but the net effect depends heavily on where stablecoin demand originates, what issuers do with their reserves, and whether issuers get Fed master-account access. The note is explicitly forward-looking and does not report a measured deposit-growth slowdown at any identified bank cohort.
  • Partner/liquidity exposure — banks that hold stablecoin issuers' reserve deposits directly (mostly large, systemically important banks; Circle's own disclosures show its bank deposits shifted almost entirely to Global Systemically Important Banks after the 2023 SVB failure). This is the group the one causal, bank-level empirical study (NY Fed Staff Report 1185, Lee & Tou, Feb 2026) actually measures, using a natural experiment around the 2023 SVB failure (pre-dating the GENIUS Act, but the same partner-bank mechanism the Act now formalizes). Their finding: these "treated" banks grew in deposits/assets relative to matched control banks in the post-period — the opposite of a growth slowdown — while shifting toward a more reserve-heavy, less loan-intensive balance sheet (loan-to-asset ratio down ~14pp, an 11.4% relative decline; daily interbank payment value up ~67% relative to controls; intraday reserve-balance volatility up).

Under neither definition does the published research find a deposit growth slowdown at exposed banks since stablecoin regulation clarified. If anything, the identified effect is a change in what banks do with the deposits they still hold (more reserves, less lending), not a decline in deposit inflows.

The aggregate data check

Using Federal Reserve M1 money supply (currency + demand deposits + other checkable deposits — a standard proxy for the transaction deposits most exposed to stablecoin substitution) queried live from askamerica's econ.monetary_aggregates table:

This is aggregate, not bank-level, and M1 mixes many drivers (Fed policy, rate cuts, seasonal effects) well beyond stablecoins — it cannot isolate a stablecoin-specific effect. But it directly rules out one version of the claim: there is no visible break or deceleration in economy-wide transaction-deposit growth coinciding with the Act's passage. Given stablecoins' still-small scale (~1.5% of M1), this is consistent with the research literature's own caveat that effects, if present, are not yet large enough to show up in aggregate data.

A genuine data gap in this corpus

askamerica's FDIC-sourced banking.financials table (quarterly call-report data — total assets, domestic deposits, per institution per report date) declares a 1934–2026 window but, checked live today via describe_table and data_coverage, actually holds only the single 2026 snapshot (8,666 rows, one report date). That means a genuine bank-level before/after panel — comparing deposit growth at, say, large money-center banks or fintech-adjacent banks against community banks, before and after July 2025 — cannot currently be built from this corpus; the historical quarters needed for that specific comparison are not loaded. This is reported here as a real coverage gap rather than papered over with a single-snapshot approximation.
