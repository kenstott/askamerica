Millennials own homes at age 30 about a third less often than Baby Boomers did — and Gen Z is falling further behind

Same-age comparison across generations, Census CPS/ACS data, 2024 vintage

Summary

Younger Americans own homes at meaningfully lower rates than earlier generations did at the identical age. By age 30, only 33% of Millennials owned a home, versus 48% of Baby Boomers and 55% of the Silent Generation at the same age — a roughly 31% relative shortfall (15 percentage points) versus Boomers. At age 27, today's oldest Gen Zers own homes at 32.6%, versus 40.5% for Boomers at 27 (about a 19% relative gap). By age 35 the gap narrows but does not close: 56% of today's 35-year-olds (mostly Millennials) own homes versus 61.5% of Boomers at 35 (a 9% relative gap). This is not a one-time Millennial anomaly — homeownership-by-age has declined in every successive generation since the Silent Generation, and early Gen Z data show the trend deepening further, not reversing.

The numbers, generation by generation

Two independent research groups, using Census Bureau survey microdata (Current Population Survey via IPUMS-CPS), converge on the same pattern:

  • Apartment List's 2025 Millennial Homeownership Report (Census CPS, individual-level measure): by age 30, homeownership was 55% for the Silent Generation, 48% for Baby Boomers, 42% for Gen X, and 33% for Millennials — three consecutive generational declines.
  • Redfin's 2024 generational homeownership analysis (IPUMS-CPS, 1976-2024, household-head measure): at age 27, 40.5% of Baby Boomers owned homes, versus 38.4% of Gen X and 32.6% of Gen Z at the same age. At age 35, 61.5% of Boomers owned homes versus 59.4% of Gen X and 56.0% of today's 35-year-olds (largely Millennials).

As a sanity check against a third, independent source, we queried Census ACS 5-year data (table B25007, Tenure by Age of Householder) directly: in 2023, the owner-occupied share of households headed by someone aged 25-34 was 40.8% nationally — consistent in magnitude with the CPS-based figures above (ACS uses a household-level definition, which runs higher than Apartment List's individual-level definition, so the two are not directly identical but agree in direction and rough scale).

How much of the gap is age-related vs. a snapshot of today

It's important to compare generations at the same age, not as of today, because generations differ in current age: in 2024 Baby Boomers were 60-78 years old (naturally near-peak homeownership) while Millennials were 28-43 and Gen Z 12-27 (naturally lower, since people buy homes as they age). Unadjusted, today's headline rates are 79.6% (Boomers), 72.9% (Gen X), 54.9% (Millennials), 26.1% (Gen Z) — a much larger-looking gap that is partly just an artifact of current age composition. The age-matched comparisons above are the correct way to isolate the generational effect, and they still show a real, non-trivial gap: 15-19% relative shortfall in the 20s, narrowing to about 9% relative by the mid-30s.

Why the gap exists

Both sources attribute the gap primarily to (1) the 2008 Great Recession suppressing Millennial homebuying just as that cohort reached adulthood, and (2) the 2022-2024 surge in mortgage rates (roughly 3% to 7%) combined with elevated home prices, which has hit Gen Z and younger Millennials hardest since they lack existing home equity to roll into a purchase. Redfin also notes tight housing supply (older owners not selling) and later life-milestones (marriage, first childbirth now averaging 27.5 vs. 24.9 two decades ago) as contributing factors.

Methodology and caveats

Figures are drawn from two independently published generational analyses, each based on Census Bureau survey microdata (CPS/IPUMS), plus a direct cross-check against Census ACS 5-year estimates queried live for this report. The two published sources use slightly different homeownership definitions (individual-level for Apartment List vs. household-head for Redfin), so their exact percentages differ by a few points for the same age/generation, but both show the same ranking and same order-of-magnitude gap. Neither source nor our cross-check found any generation reversing the trend; Gen Z's early trajectory (26.1% overall in 2024, roughly flat since 2022) suggests the shortfall may be widening further rather than closing.
