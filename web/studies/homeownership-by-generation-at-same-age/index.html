Young Americans own homes at about 5-10 points lower rates than the same age bands did in the 1980s-2000s, a gap of roughly 10-18% in relative terms

Census CPS/HVS Table 12 (housing.hvs_homeownership_by_age), 1982-2025, householder-age homeownership rates

Answer

At the same age, younger Americans own homes at lower rates, but the gap is smaller than popular framing suggests and is concentrated in the 30s. Ages 30-34: 57.1% in 1982 vs 46.6% in 2025 (-10.5 points, about 18% less likely). Under-35 overall: 41.2% in 1982, 43.1% peak in 2004, 37.0% in 2025 (-4.2 points vs 1982, -6.1 vs peak). Ages 25-29 are close to the 1982 level (38.6% then, 33.3% now; era average 36.0% in 1982-94 vs 34.6% in 2020-25).

Era averages (same age band): age 30-34 was 53.0% (1982-94) and 54.7% (1995-2007) vs 48.3% (2020-25), about 5-6 points or 9-12% lower. Age 35-39: 63.9/64.6 vs 58.7 (about 5-6 points lower). Age 40-44: 70.5 vs 65.3 (5 points lower).

The drop is not a steady generational slide. It came with the 2008 crisis: the 2008-19 and 2020-25 eras are nearly identical, and rates ticked up in 2020-23 before easing in 2024-25 (30-34: 49.4% in 2023, 46.6% in 2025). The overall rate barely moved (64.8% in 1982, 65.2% in 2025), because the homeowner base is older.

Caveats: these are age-period comparisons from annual survey estimates (sampling error not published; single-year moves of 1-2 points are noise); era averages mix cohorts, so a strict birth-cohort view requires tracking each cohort across ages. Millennials aged 30-34 (2011-2025) sit at 45-49%, versus 51-57% for Boomers at the same age in the 1980s and Gen X in the late 1990s-2000s. I did not control for household composition, marriage or income.
