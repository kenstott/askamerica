Millennials own homes at roughly 2/3 the rate Boomers did at the same age — a 15-point gap at age 30

Apartment List/Census CPS individual-level homeownership by generation at matched ages (2025 report); ACS B25007 2023 cross-section computed in-corpus

Summary

At age 30, only 33% of millennials owned a home, versus 48% of baby boomers and 42% of Gen Xers at the same age — a 15-percentage-point (31% relative) shortfall against boomers, and each generation since the Silent Generation (55% at 30) has bought homes more slowly than the one before it. The gap narrows somewhat with age but persists: by 35, millennial homeownership had risen to 53%, still 7 points behind the 60% that both Gen X and boomers had reached at 35. As of 2024 the overall millennial homeownership rate (all millennial ages, individual-level measure) stood at 47%, versus 74% for boomers and 65% for Gen X.

The generation-at-same-age comparison

Source: Apartment List's 2025 Millennial Homeownership Report, built on Census Bureau Current Population Survey (CPS) microdata, using an individual-level homeownership measure — the share of adults who are either the householder or the householder's spouse in an owner-occupied home. This differs from (and runs lower than) the household-level rate Census usually headlines, but Apartment List argues it tracks generational and life-stage trends more accurately because it is not distorted by shifts in who forms a separate household (e.g., more young adults living with parents).

By age 35, millennials had reached 53%, versus 60% for both Gen X and boomers at 35, and Silent-generation homeownership near 70% by that age. In state-by-state data, millennials aged 35–39 trail Gen X-at-the-same-age homeownership in 48 of 50 states plus DC — only Indiana, Iowa, and DC show millennials at parity or ahead.

Apartment List attributes much of the millennial shortfall to timing: many millennials were entering the workforce or finishing school during the 2008 financial crisis and subsequent housing-market collapse, then faced a post-recession construction shift toward multifamily rental housing over suburban single-family starter homes, followed by a post-2020 run-up in home prices (roughly 53% since 2020) that has outpaced rent growth (about 20% over the same period).

Where this trend stands today, and what this corpus adds

As of 2024 (Apartment List/CPS, individual-level, all ages within each generation, not age-matched): Baby Boomers 74%, Silent Generation 70%, Gen X 65%, Millennials 47%, Gen Z 9% (Gen Z's oldest members were only 27 in the latest data year, so this is not comparable to the age-matched figures above).

This corpus's search_catalog turned up no table carrying a homeownership rate directly (a genuine coverage gap — "homeownership" is an unmatched term against every schema), and no synthetic-cohort table exists that could reconstruct a generation's trajectory by re-aging a historical cross-section. The closest available building block, census.acs_housing_tenure_by_age (ACS 5-year table B25007), was queried directly for 2023 and gives the household-level owner-occupied share by CURRENT age band, summed nationally across all states:

This is a single 2023 snapshot across current ages, not a generation-matched trajectory (it mixes late Gen X and millennials within the 25–44 bands), so it cannot by itself answer "how much less likely is generation Y than generation X was at the same age" — that comparison requires stitching cohorts across decades of vintages, which is exactly the work Apartment List's CPS analysis already did. It is included here as independently-queried corroboration that the age gradient in ownership (rising steeply through the 20s-40s) is the same shape reported in the generational literature, and as household-level numbers to compare against Apartment List's lower individual-level figures for the same current ages: the 25-34 household rate (40.8%) and 35-44 (60.5%) sit noticeably above Apartment List's individual-level millennial rates at comparable ages, consistent with Apartment List's own statement that the individual-level measure runs lower than the household-level one.

Every query behind this report

1 warehouse call ran in this session, in order. Each is reproducible against the same snapshot.

query — 1 rows — 6864 ms
SELECT year,
  SUM(owner_25_to_34) AS own_25_34, SUM(owner_25_to_34+renter_25_to_34) AS tot_25_34,
  SUM(owner_35_to_44) AS own_35_44, SUM(owner_35_to_44+renter_35_to_44) AS tot_35_44,
  SUM(owner_45_to_54) AS own_45_54, SUM(owner_45_to_54+renter_45_to_54) AS tot_45_54,
  SUM(owner_55_to_59) AS own_55_59, SUM(owner_55_to_59+renter_55_to_59) AS tot_55_59,
  SUM(owner_60_to_64) AS own_60_64, SUM(owner_60_to_64+renter_60_to_64) AS tot_60_64,
  SUM(owner_65_to_74) AS own_65_74, SUM(owner_65_to_74+renter_65_to_74) AS tot_65_74
FROM census.acs_housing_tenure_by_age
WHERE geography = 'state' AND year = '2023'
GROUP BY year
