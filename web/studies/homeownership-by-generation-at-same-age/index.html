Younger Americans are 10-40% less likely to own homes than earlier generations were at the same age

Comparison controls for age; magnitude depends on which generations, which age, and which measure of "own"

Summary

Younger Americans today own homes at meaningfully lower rates than earlier generations did at the same age — the size of the gap depends on which generations and which exact age you compare, but every credible age-controlled measure points the same direction. Comparing Millennials to Baby Boomers at age 30 (an individual-level ownership measure from Census Bureau microdata), Millennials are about 31% less likely to own a home (33% vs. 48%); against the Silent Generation the gap is 40% less likely (33% vs. 55%). Comparing Gen Z to Gen X at age 28 using a household-head measure from the Current Population Survey, the gap is smaller but still real: about 10% less likely (38.3% vs. 42.5%). A parallel check in this corpus's own ACS data shows the national homeownership rate for householders aged 25-34 fell from 45.3% in 2009 to a low of 37.6% in 2016 and has only partially recovered to 40.8% by 2023 — still well below the 2009 level, corroborating the trend though it cannot by itself span multiple generations since ACS microdata here only reaches back to 2009.

Why the estimates differ

Two independent research shops measure this differently, and both are legitimate:

  • Apartment List uses an individual-level rate: the share of all adults (not households) who are either the householder or the householder's spouse in an owner-occupied home. This is more sensitive to trends like adult children living with parents, and tends to read lower and show a larger generational gap.
  • Redfin uses a household-head rate from the Current Population Survey Annual Social and Economic Supplement (via IPUMS-CPS, spanning 1976-2025): the share of household heads of a given generation who own vs. rent. People still living with parents or other family are excluded from the calculation entirely for their generation, which tends to narrow the measured gap somewhat.

Neither is "more correct" — they answer slightly different questions (what share of all young adults own homes, vs. what share of young adults who have formed their own household own homes). Reporting a single number without naming which measure it is would overstate the precision of the answer.

The pattern holds across both data sources and both metrics: each generation since the Silent Generation has bought homes more slowly than the one before it at the same age, per Apartment List's analysis, and the gap between the two most recent generations and their parents' generation runs roughly 7% to 40% relative, narrower in the household-head measure and wider in the individual-level measure, and generally wider the younger the age compared (the housing-formation gap has been narrowing somewhat as Millennials age into their late 30s, per Redfin, since the 2008 financial crisis hit Gen X's home equity too around the same calendar years).

Corroborating check in this corpus

This connector's own Census ACS data (table census.acs_housing_tenure_by_age, ACS 5-year estimates, table B25007) does not carry a generation label or reach back before 2009, so it cannot on its own reproduce a Boomer-vs-Millennial comparison. But it does let us check the recent trajectory of the 25-34 age band nationally (summing across all states): the homeownership rate for that age band fell from 45.3% in 2009 to a trough of 37.6% in 2016 (a period spanning the post-2008 recession's effect on young buyers), before partially recovering to 40.8% by 2023 — still 4.5 points below the 2009 level. This is consistent with, but narrower in scope than, the multi-generation comparisons above; it reflects the same age band across 14 years of one persistent (largely Millennial, later Gen Z) cohort turnover, not a same-age comparison of distinct generations decades apart.

Caveats

  • Generational birth-year boundaries (Silent, Boomer, Gen X, Millennial, Gen Z) are conventions, not census categories, and different researchers use slightly different cutoffs.
  • The two external sources use somewhat different generational age brackets and calendar years, so their generational rates are internally consistent within each source but not perfectly interchangeable across sources.
  • Both external figures come from CPS/ACS microdata reprocessed by Apartment List and Redfin respectively; this response did not re-derive the individual-level or CPS-ASEC generational series from raw microdata, since this corpus's own tables do not carry the individual/generation-linked fields needed to reproduce them independently. The in-corpus ACS check above is an independent partial corroboration, not a full replication.

Every query behind this report

1 warehouse call ran in this session, in order. Each is reproducible against the same snapshot.

query — 15 rows — 21169 ms
SELECT "year",
  SUM(owner_25_to_34) AS owners_25_34,
  SUM(owner_25_to_34) + SUM(renter_25_to_34) AS total_25_34,
  ROUND(100.0*SUM(owner_25_to_34)/(SUM(owner_25_to_34)+SUM(renter_25_to_34)),1) AS rate_25_34
FROM census.acs_housing_tenure_by_age
WHERE geography = 'state'
GROUP BY "year"
ORDER BY "year"
