How much of pre-pandemic transit ridership has come back, and how much does it vary between cities?

Bottom line: As of full-year 2024, US public transit ridership had recovered to about 78.6% of its 2019 (pre-pandemic) level, computed directly from FTA National Transit Database (NTD) monthly ridership data: 7.62 billion unlinked passenger trips (UPT) in 2024 vs. 9.70 billion in 2019. APTA's own headline figure, which extrapolates for late/non-reporting agencies, runs a few points higher — roughly 81% for calendar 2024 and about 85% by late 2025.

Recovery varies enormously by city and system. Among the 88 major agencies with more than 10 million 2019 trips, recovery ranges from under 45% up to over 90%, with a standard deviation of about 16.5 percentage points around a mean of 76.6%.

At the extremes: Puerto Rico's transit authority (San Juan) is at just 7% of 2019 ridership (service cuts/infrastructure disruption, not just demand); several smaller/mid-sized systems (Tucson, Albany's CDTA, Kansas City, Spokane) have fully recovered or exceeded 2019 ridership. This matches APTA's finding that cities under 100,000 population had recovered to about 88% of 2019 levels by 2024/25, well ahead of the largest metros (78% for cities over 500,000, 81% for cities over 2 million).

Why the variation is systematic, not random

  • Mode mix drives most of the gap. Nationally, bus UPT is at 82.6% of 2019 and rail UPT is at 74.8% (ferry 82.1%). Rail- and commuter-rail-heavy agencies (BART, MARTA, Chicago's Northeast Illinois Regional Commuter Railroad at 57.0%, SoCal's Metrolink at 40.0%) lag; bus-dominant systems recover faster because bus ridership skews toward essential workers, students, and non-commute trips that were never as disrupted.
  • Downtown office commuting hasn't fully returned, but that's no longer the whole story. Office occupancy has been roughly flat through 2025 even as national ridership climbed toward 81–85% — growth increasingly comes from non-commute trips (errands, leisure, off-peak travel), not the 9-to-5 commute these systems were built around.
  • City-specific catalysts matter. NYC subway ridership got an extra boost in 2025 from congestion pricing (an estimated 90M+ additional subway rides in its first year), pushing NYCT toward ~85% by late 2025; DC's WMATA has been helped by federal return-to-office mandates.

Methodology notes

  • Computed from transport.transit_ridership (FTA NTD Complete Monthly Ridership, with FTA's own adjustments/estimates for late reporters), aggregated to full-year unlinked passenger trips per agency and by mode, 2019 vs. 2024 (the most recent full year with finalized annual totals across nearly all major agencies; 2025 is complete in this table but treated as still-maturing).
  • Cross-checked against APTA's published national and city-size-tier recovery figures and against independent reporting on NYC, BART/Bay Area, Chicago, Boston, LA, Seattle, and Atlanta — all consistent within a few points, validating the NTD-derived numbers.
  • Standard deviation (16.5 pts) and mean/median (76.6% / 77.5%) computed across the 88 agencies with >10M 2019 trips; smaller agencies show even wider variation (7%–120%+).
