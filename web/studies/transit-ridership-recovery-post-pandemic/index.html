US transit ridership has recovered to about 81% of pre-pandemic levels nationally — but that masks a roughly 60-point spread between individual cities' agencies

FTA National Transit Database (unlinked passenger trips), full calendar years 2019 vs. 2025

Summary

Nationally, US public transit ridership in 2025 stood at about 81% of its 2019 (pre-pandemic) level — 7.88 billion unlinked passenger trips versus 9.70 billion in 2019, per the Federal Transit Administration's National Transit Database (NTD). Independent APTA reporting from early-to-mid 2026 puts the figure similarly, around 85%, with bus modes closer to full recovery than rail. But the national number obscures enormous city-to-city variation: among the ~40 largest transit agencies, 2025 ridership ranged from 48% of 2019 levels (BART, San Francisco Bay Area) to over 100% (Sound Transit, Seattle, driven by new light-rail lines), a roughly 60-percentage-point spread. Large legacy heavy-rail/commuter systems oriented around downtown office commuting (BART, MARTA, Denver RTD, Chicago's Metra) have recovered the least; systems with a higher share of bus ridership, non-commute trips, or that have physically expanded service (Sound Transit, San Diego MTS, MTA Bus) have recovered the most, some exceeding 2019.

Step 1 — What the literature says

APTA's ridership tracking (its most recent policy briefs, released in 2026 covering data through late 2025/early 2026) reports national ridership at roughly 85% of 2019 levels, with bus at about 86%, heavy rail at about 71%, and commuter rail at about 70% — meaning rail modes, which are disproportionately used for downtown office commuting, lag the recovery, while bus and demand-response/paratransit service (reported near 93%) have recovered furthest. APTA and outlets covering its research (Mass Transit, Smart Cities Dive) attribute the mode gap to the persistence of hybrid/remote work suppressing peak commuter volumes, even as off-peak, weekend, and non-commute ridership has substantially recovered or grown. Eno Center for Transportation's analysis of agency-level recovery similarly finds wide dispersion by metro area, tied to each region's commuting patterns, downtown employment recovery, and (in cases like San Francisco/BART) office-vacancy rates and central-city population loss. News coverage of individual agencies in 2025-2026 (NYC's MTA reporting record 2025 ridership; press on BART's persistently weak recovery and later-stage service cuts; Chicago's CTA citing 2025 fare increases and route cuts tied to the expiration of federal COVID relief funds) is consistent with the FTA data below: New York recovered far more of its ridership than San Francisco or Atlanta.

The small gap between APTA's ~85% figure and the ~81% computed here from raw NTD data is expected — APTA's briefs sometimes use different agency subsets, seasonally-adjusted or partial-year figures, or slightly different vintage cuts of the same underlying NTD data; both point to the same qualitative conclusion (ridership plateaued in the low-to-mid 80s percent of 2019 by 2025).

Step 2 — Computing recovery directly from FTA National Transit Database data

Recovery was computed as each agency's full-calendar-year 2025 unlinked passenger trips (UPT) divided by its full-calendar-year 2019 UPT, using transport.transit_ridership_by_agency_year, an AskAmerica view built from the FTA's NTD monthly ridership series (source: data.transportation.gov/resource/8bui-9xvu.json). Both 2019 and 2025 were confirmed to be complete 12-month years in the underlying monthly table before comparing them, so the ratio is not distorted by a partial year. The pattern: the lowest-recovering systems (BART, MARTA, Denver RTD, Metra, Pittsburgh) are heavy-rail or commuter-rail systems built primarily around downtown office commuting in metro areas that have seen persistently weak downtown-office return-to-work and, in San Francisco's and Atlanta's cases, notable downtown vacancy and disinvestment. The highest-recovering or growing systems fall into two categories: (1) bus-heavy systems with more diversified, non-commute trip purposes (San Diego MTS, MTA Bus, King County Metro), and (2) systems undergoing physical service expansion — Sound Transit opened new light-rail extensions (Northgate Link, East Link segments) after 2019, so its >100% figure reflects new capacity and coverage as much as demand recovery, not a like-for-like comparison. New York City Transit, despite being far larger than any other system, sits close to the median recovery rate (87%) — it is not an outlier driving the national average up on ratio terms, though its sheer size (3.46B of the nation's 9.70B 2019 trips, or 36%) does pull the national aggregate recovery rate closer to its own than to the median agency's, since the national figure is a trip-weighted average, not a simple average across agencies.

The unweighted median recovery ratio across these ~39 large agencies is about 76%, five points below the national trip-weighted figure of 81% — confirming that the national number is somewhat more optimistic than the typical major city's experience, because it is dominated by New York's large base and above-median recovery.

Caveats

  • Figures are unlinked passenger trips (boardings), the FTA's standard ridership metric; they do not adjust for service levels (an agency running less service could show lower ridership partly because it is offering fewer trips, not purely from weaker demand).
  • Sound Transit's recovery figure is inflated by genuine light-rail network expansion since 2019 and is not a clean recovery comparison; it is flagged rather than excluded, since the question asks about variation across cities as reported.
  • APTA's own 85% national figure differs modestly from the 81.3% computed here from raw NTD annual sums; both come from the same underlying FTA source but likely differ in agency inclusion/vintage, and both support the same conclusion.
  • This analysis is descriptive (differences in recovery across cities), not causal; it does not isolate the effect of any single policy or driver (e.g., remote work share, fare changes, service cuts) on recovery.

Sources

  1. FTA National Transit Database — Monthly Ridership (transport.transit_ridership_by_agency_year)
    Show SQL
    SELECT agency, SUM(CASE WHEN "year"=2019 THEN unlinked_passenger_trips ELSE 0 END) AS upt_2019, SUM(CASE WHEN "year"=2025 THEN unlinked_passenger_trips ELSE 0 END) AS upt_2025 FROM transport.transit_ridership_by_agency_year WHERE "year" IN (2019,2025) GROUP BY agency HAVING SUM(CASE WHEN "year"=2019 THEN unlinked_passenger_trips ELSE 0 END) > 20000000 ORDER BY upt_2019 DESC
  2. FTA NTD national annual totals 2019-2025
    Show SQL
    SELECT "year", SUM(unlinked_passenger_trips) AS total_upt FROM transport.transit_ridership_by_agency_year WHERE "year" BETWEEN 2019 AND 2025 GROUP BY "year" ORDER BY "year"
