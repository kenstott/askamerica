## Summary

At the metro level — the finest geography this corpus's price data supports — the U.S. housing markets hit hardest in the 2008 crash have **not** recovered as strongly as nominal headlines suggest. Across the 15 metro areas (MSAs) with the steepest FHFA House Price Index declines from their 2004–2007 peak to their 2011 trough (Las Vegas, Stockton, Cape Coral, Riverside, Phoenix, Bakersfield, and similar Sand-state/inland-California markets), nominal prices today are up a median of **+53%** versus their pre-crash peak. But once adjusted for inflation (CPI-U, expressed in 2026 dollars), the median hardest-hit metro is still **1.3% BELOW** its 2006-07 real peak — essentially flat after roughly two decades — and **8 of the 15 metros remain underwater in real terms**, some (Bakersfield, Stockton) by 17-21%. Only a handful — Miami, West Palm Beach, Tampa, and Phoenix — have genuinely exceeded their pre-crisis real price level.

Separately, primary research fetched directly for this report finds that the specific **neighborhoods** hit hardest by foreclosure — disproportionately Black and Latino communities that received a disproportionate share of subprime loans — have recovered even less than these metro averages imply, because most original owners never re-entered as buyers to capture the subsequent price rebound.

## What the data can and can't show

This corpus's FHFA `house_price_index` table tracks home prices at the state and metro (CBSA) level back to 1975. It does **not** carry a foreclosure-rate series, and has no census-tract or ZIP-level price index — `search_catalog` confirmed no table anywhere in the corpus contains the word "foreclosure" (returned in `unmatched_terms`). So a true within-city "which neighborhoods were hit hardest" cut is not something this connector's tables can answer directly. Metro-level recovery is the closest computable proxy, and the level at which FHFA, Case-Shiller, and most academic foreclosure studies themselves report price data.

**Method:** For each of the ~400 MSAs in `housing.house_price_index` (traditional, purchase-only, quarterly, `index_nsa`), found the peak annual-average index in 2004-2008, the trough in 2008-2013, and the latest (2026) reading; ranked by peak-to-trough decline to identify the 15 hardest-hit metros nationally; deflated peak-year and current values to 2026 dollars using BLS CPI-U (`adjust_inflation` tool) to get an inflation-adjusted, apples-to-apples recovery percentage. No rows were excluded from this ranking by hand. No causal/regression model was applied — figures are descriptive index ratios and CPI-deflation arithmetic.

To address the neighborhood-grain gap, I directly fetched and read three primary/peer-linked sources rather than relying on search snippets:
- NLIHC's summary of the Alliance for a Just Society's "Wasted Wealth" report
- Pew Research Center's 2009 county-level regression study of foreclosure determinants
- Mother Jones' reporting on Kermani & Wong's NBER working paper (w29306), a 6-million-transaction, 1990-2017 dataset on realized housing returns by race

## The neighborhood-level literature this data can't reach

- **NLIHC / Alliance for a Just Society ("Wasted Wealth")**: ZIP codes that were majority-people-of-color had 17 foreclosures per 1,000 households versus 10 per 1,000 in majority-white ZIP codes (as of 2012); average wealth lost per household was $2,200 in majority-people-of-color ZIP codes versus $1,300 in majority-white ones. Black and Latino borrowers were 80% and 70% more likely, respectively, to receive subprime loans than white borrowers controlling for income and credit score, and 76%/71% more likely to actually lose the home once in foreclosure.
- **Pew Research (2009)**: County-level regression confirms geographic concentration (33 counties nationally with foreclosure rates above 5%, concentrated in California, Florida, Arizona, Nevada) and finds immigrant population share and local home-price declines were both independently associated with higher county foreclosure rates.
- **Kermani & Wong, NBER w29306 (via Mother Jones)**: Using 6 million housing transactions from 1990-2017, found Black and Latino home sellers realized annualized returns 3.7 and 2.0 percentage points lower than white sellers on average — a 44% smaller decade-long return for Black sellers, 22% smaller for Latino sellers — driven almost entirely by higher rates of foreclosures and short sales, which are more steeply discounted in communities of color. Remove distressed sales from the comparison and the racial return gap nearly disappears. The paper estimates equalizing housing investment *returns* (not just homeownership rates) across races would cut the Black-white retirement-age housing wealth gap by 39%, versus just 1% from equalizing homeownership rates alone.

The upshot: households who were foreclosed on and never got back into ownership missed the metro-level price recovery entirely, converting a temporary price crash into a permanent, uncaptured wealth loss.

## Bottom line

At the metro level, the hardest-hit housing markets have only partially recovered: nominal prices look fully recovered (and then some) almost everywhere, but in real terms the typical hardest-hit metro is roughly back to breakeven with its 2006 peak, and several — the inland-California/Nevada cluster — remain meaningfully below it. At the true neighborhood/community level that actually experienced the foreclosure wave — disproportionately Black and Latino communities targeted by subprime lending — primary research indicates the recovery has been substantially weaker still: those households lost roughly 20-44% more of their potential housing-wealth return than white households, mostly because the metro-level price rebound came after most of them had already lost the home and never bought back in.

This report quantifies the metro-level claim directly from FHFA government price data; the neighborhood-level claim is drawn from primary research read directly for this report (NLIHC/Alliance for a Just Society, Pew Research Center, Mother Jones reporting on NBER research), not computed from any table in this corpus, because no table here carries a foreclosure count or a homeowner-outcome-after-foreclosure measure.
