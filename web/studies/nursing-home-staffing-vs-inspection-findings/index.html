Nursing home inspection deficiencies track staffing levels: 1-star-staffed facilities average 12 health deficiencies vs. 5 for 5-star facilities, but staffing alone explains only a modest share of the variation

CMS Nursing Home Compare (data.cms.gov 4pq5-n9py), 14,690 facilities, most recent standard survey cycle

Summary

Yes — thinly staffed nursing homes reliably rack up more inspection deficiencies, and the relationship is consistent across every cut of the current CMS data, but staffing is one contributor among several, not the whole story. Facilities with CMS's lowest (1-star) staffing rating average 11.9 health deficiencies on their most recent survey, versus 5.3 for 5-star-staffed facilities — a 55% gap that falls in a smooth, near-monotonic staircase from 1 star to 5 stars. The raw correlation between nurse hours-per-resident-day and deficiency counts is real but modest (r ≈ -0.15), because deficiency counts are noisy and shaped by many other things (state survey stringency, facility size, occupancy, staff turnover). In a multivariate model, an extra hour of nurse staffing per resident-day is associated with about 0.66 fewer deficiencies, holding bed count, average census, and RN turnover fixed — and nursing staff turnover turns out to be an even stronger independent predictor of deficiencies than raw staffing hours. This matches the published academic and government literature on the subject.

What the published research already found

This question has been studied directly and the current data replicate it. A 2024 peer-reviewed analysis of FY2019 CMS/LTCFocus data covering 13,086 nursing homes (Shin, Harrington, Lee, Ahn, Lee & Ali, Innovation in Aging, 2024) used OLS regression and found that facilities near 4.1 total nurse hours-per-resident-day (HPRD) and 0.75 RN HPRD had markedly fewer deficiency citations than facilities near CMS's 2024 minimum-staffing benchmark (3.48 total HPRD / 0.5 RN HPRD) — higher RN, LPN, and aide hours all independently correlated with fewer deficiencies, while larger facility size, higher non-White resident share, urban location, and higher Medicaid share were all associated with more deficiencies (Shin et al. 2024, PMC).

KFF's July 2026 issue brief on nursing home deficiencies, using the same Nursing Home Compare data this analysis draws on, states directly: "Nursing homes with lower levels of staffing are more likely to receive deficiencies that cause serious harm or immediate jeopardy to residents when compared to nursing homes with higher levels of staffing" (KFF, "A Closer Look at Deficiencies in Nursing Homes," 2026). The HHS Office of Inspector General has separately documented that many facilities misreport staffing levels and that reported staffing shortfalls track poor survey outcomes (HHS-OIG OEI-04-18-00450). CMS's own final rule setting minimum staffing standards (3.48 total HPRD, including 0.55 RN HPRD) was justified explicitly on quality-of-care and deficiency grounds, though it was later rescinded during a change in administration (CMS fact sheet, staffing standards final rule; Center for Medicare Advocacy coverage of the rule and its rescission). A separate Center for Medicare Advocacy report found nursing homes cited for the most serious ("immediate jeopardy") deficiencies during the pandemic disproportionately also had poor health-inspection histories and low staffing (Center for Medicare Advocacy special report). A related NCBI scoping review catalogs the methodological variety across this literature in how both "staffing" and "quality" get operationalized, which is a caution worth carrying into any single-dataset analysis like this one (scoping review, PMC9767033).

What this connector's own data show

Using the CMS Nursing Home Compare provider-information file loaded in this corpus (health.cms_nursing_home, 14,690 facilities), three independent cuts all point the same direction:

  • By staffing star rating (CMS's own 1-5 composite staffing measure), average total health deficiencies fall in a near-perfect staircase: 11.93 (1-star, n=2,642) → 10.38 (2-star) → 9.43 (3-star) → 7.86 (4-star) → 5.31 (5-star, n=1,880) — a 55% drop from worst- to best-staffed. The weighted health-deficiency score (which weights by severity/scope) shows the identical pattern: 99.0 down to 37.6.
  • By raw nurse hours-per-resident-day (HPRD), the bivariate correlation with total deficiencies is r = -0.149 for total nurse HPRD, -0.156 for RN HPRD specifically, and -0.137 for nurse-aide HPRD (n=14,271) — all negative and all highly significant given the sample size, but modest in magnitude, because facility-level deficiency counts carry a lot of noise unrelated to staffing (survey timing, state-level enforcement variation, case mix). A quintile dose-response test shows deficiencies falling from 11.0 (lowest-staffed quintile, ~3.0 HPRD) to 7.2 (highest-staffed quintile, ~5.2 HPRD), though the pattern is not perfectly monotonic (trend p=0.053) — the fourth quintile ticks up slightly before the drop in the fifth.
  • In a multivariate model controlling for facility size (certified beds), average daily census, and RN turnover, one additional hour of total nurse staffing per resident-day is associated with 0.66 fewer deficiencies (p<0.001, n=12,592), holding those other factors fixed. But the model's overall explanatory power is low (R²=0.094) — staffing and these controls together explain under 10% of the variation in deficiency counts, and RN turnover has an even larger, highly significant coefficient (+0.087 deficiencies per percentage point of RN turnover, t=26.0) than staffing hours do. This echoes the literature's point that ownership type, case mix, and facility size all matter alongside raw staffing.

A leave-one-state-out sensitivity check on the bivariate staffing-deficiency relationship confirms it is not an artifact of any single state: the coefficient stays negative and statistically significant (p<0.001) in every one of 53 state/territory refits, ranging from -1.19 to -1.89 depending on which state is excluded (California is the single most influential state, pulling the pooled estimate toward zero when included, likely reflecting California's unusually large, urban SNF population — consistent with the published finding that urban location independently predicts more deficiencies).

How to read the strength of this relationship

Direction: unambiguous and consistent across every staffing measure and every cut (star rating, quintiles, multivariate, leave-one-out) — thinner staffing tracks with more inspection problems. Magnitude: moderate at the facility level. The star-rating comparison (55% fewer deficiencies at 5-star vs. 1-star staffing) looks large because CMS's star bins already sort facilities into a wide staffing spread; the raw correlation (r≈-0.15) is the more honest single-number answer to "how strongly," and it is modest because deficiency counts are driven by many things besides staffing — most importantly nursing staff turnover, which independently out-predicts raw staffing hours in the multivariate model, plus facility size, case mix, ownership type, and which state's inspectors are doing the surveying (a confound this single-dataset analysis does not fully net out, since inspection rigor is known to vary meaningfully by state per KFF and OIG reporting).
