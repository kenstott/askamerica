In 2018-19, USDA trade-war payments covered about 95% of estimated agricultural losses in aggregate — but the coverage was very uneven by commodity

USDA ERS trade-damage estimate vs. USDA Market Facilitation Program and related outlays, July 2018-Dec 2019, plus a note on the still-unresolved 2025-26 round

Summary

For the 2018-2019 US-China trade war, USDA's own economists estimated U.S. agricultural exporters lost about $27 billion in trade damage from retaliatory tariffs (July 2018-December 2019), with China's retaliation responsible for roughly $25.7 billion of that. In response, USDA paid out about $25.6 billion through three trade-relief programs — the Market Facilitation Program (MFP, $23.0B), USDA food purchase/distribution ($2.3B), and the Agricultural Trade Promotion program ($0.3B). In aggregate that is about 95% of the estimated loss — close to full coverage on paper. But that headline number hides a real mismatch: government auditors found the money was not distributed in proportion to where the damage actually fell. Soybean, sorghum, and cotton growers — the commodities hit hardest by China's retaliation — were paid less than their estimated trade damage in the 2019 round, while corn growers were overpaid by roughly $3 billion relative to corn's own estimated damage that year, because 2019's payment formula used county-level, crop-mix-based rates rather than damage estimates tied to the specific commodity harmed. A separate peer-reviewed analysis found that summed over both 2018 and 2019, soybean growers were actually overcompensated by about $5.4 billion, because 2018's payment formula (82% of that year's money went to soybean alone) more than made up for 2019's shortfall — illustrating how unevenly "coverage" landed even for a single crop across the two years. So the honest answer is: yes, roughly-in-aggregate, but not commodity-by-commodity, and a second, still-unresolved trade war (2025-26) has already produced a larger single-crop loss with a compensation program that has not yet paid out.

How much farmers lost

USDA's Economic Research Service, using an economic model of how retaliatory tariffs would reduce U.S. export sales to the retaliating countries, put total agricultural trade damage from July 2018 through December 2019 at approximately $27 billion, of which China's retaliatory tariffs accounted for about $25.7 billion and other countries (the EU, Canada, Mexico, Turkey, India) about $1.5 billion. Soybeans bore the largest single share: China had bought 60% of U.S. soybean export volume before the trade war, and the value of U.S. soybean exports to China fell from about $12.3 billion (2017) to $3.1 billion (2018) after China imposed a 25% retaliatory tariff. U.S. soybean planted acreage fell roughly 15% (about 12 million fewer acres) by 2019 as farmers adjusted to depressed prices.

How much the government paid

USDA ran three trade-relief programs in response: the Market Facilitation Program (two rounds, $8.6B disbursed for 2018 and $14.4B for 2019, totaling $23.0B), a Food Purchase and Distribution Program that bought surplus commodities for food banks and nutrition programs ($2.3B), and the Agricultural Trade Promotion Program to develop new export markets ($0.3B). Combined, that is $25.6 billion, or about 95% of the $27 billion estimated loss — close to full coverage in aggregate.

Separately, in the warehouse's own USDA ERS farm-income series (queried directly: ag.ers_farm_income, US total, category = 'Government payments'), total federal farm program payments of all kinds (not just trade aid — also includes crop insurance-adjacent programs, ARC/PLC, conservation, and disaster aid) ran $11.5B (2017) → $13.7B (2018) → $22.4B (2019) → $45.6B (2020, dominated by COVID-19 CFAP payments, not MFP) → $26.0B (2021). This broader series is consistent with — but not identical to — the MFP-specific figures above; it is included to show that the trade-war payments were layered on top of, not instead of, USDA's regular farm-support programs, and that the much larger 2020 spike was a separate (pandemic) episode.

The coverage was uneven, not proportional

A 2021 GAO review (GAO-22-468) of the 2019 MFP round found that USDA changed its damage-estimation methodology between 2018 and 2019 in ways that broke the link between a commodity's actual trade damage and what its growers were paid. In 2019, corn producers received about $3 billion more in MFP payments than USDA's own estimate of trade damage to corn, while payments to soybean, sorghum, and cotton producers — the commodities that suffered the largest real damage — came in below their estimated trade damage that year. GAO attributed part of this to an unrepresentative import baseline (summing each commodity's single highest historical import year across a decade, rather than a consistent reference period) that inflated "damage" for commodities like wheat far beyond any year China actually imported that much.

Looking across both years together tells a different, still-uneven story: in 2018, roughly 82% of all MFP payments went to soybean producers alone (a peer-reviewed analysis, Baryshpolets et al. 2022, Journal of the Agricultural and Applied Economics Association) — enough that, cumulated over 2018-2019, that same study found soybean growers were net overcompensated by about $5.4 billion relative to their actual losses, even though the 2019 round alone shorted them. In short: the aggregate 95%-of-loss figure is real, but it is an average across a program that overpaid some commodities and years and underpaid others — it is not evidence that any individual farmer's specific loss was covered at a 95% rate.

A second, unresolved round: 2025-26

"The trade war" is not only a 2018-19 event. A new US-China tariff escalation in 2025 produced a fresh round of retaliatory losses: China's retaliation cut U.S. soybean exports to China by roughly 50% versus the prior two marketing years, and reporting citing USDA/industry estimates put 2025 soybean trade losses alone at about $12.6 billion — even after federal assistance, the American Soybean Association estimated growers still lost nearly $75 per harvested acre on the 2025 crop. In December 2025, USDA/the administration announced a new $12 billion farm-aid package (press coverage calls it "Farmer Bridge Assistance"; it is expected to disburse starting around February 2026, covering corn, soybeans, wheat, and barley). As of this warehouse's most recent loaded data (through 2025), that specific payment has not yet been disbursed, so its actual coverage ratio against 2025-26 losses cannot yet be computed — only the announced $12 billion figure against an estimated ~$12.6 billion loss in soybeans alone (before accounting for corn, wheat, and other affected commodities, whose total losses would push the true loss figure higher). Note that a separate 2025 USDA payout captured in the underlying FSA data, the $9.36 billion "Emergency Commodity Assistance Program," is a different, general low-commodity-price relief program (authorized by the American Relief Act, 2025) and is not itself a trade-war-damage payment — it should not be added to the 2025-26 trade-war compensation figure.

Bottom line

In the best-documented episode (2018-19), USDA's trade-relief payments covered roughly 95% of the aggregate estimated agricultural trade loss ($25.6B paid against $27.0B in estimated damage) — essentially close to full coverage nationally. But independent GAO and academic analysis found the money was allocated by a formula that didn't track actual damage closely: corn was overpaid, soybean/sorghum/cotton were underpaid in 2019 even as soybean was overpaid across the full 2018-19 period taken together. So "how much did payments cover" has two honest answers: about 95% in total dollars, and considerably more uneven than that once you ask the question commodity-by-commodity or farmer-by-farmer. The still-unfolding 2025-26 round has already produced a comparably sized single-crop loss (soybean, ~$12.6B) against a newly announced but not-yet-disbursed $12B program, so its own coverage ratio is not yet knowable.
