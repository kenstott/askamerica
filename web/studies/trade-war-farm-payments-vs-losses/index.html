Government trade-war payments covered roughly 85-95% of farmers' aggregate losses — but corn was overpaid while soybeans, cotton and sorghum were underpaid

USDA ERS / GAO / CRS analysis of the 2018-2019 retaliatory-tariff episode. Nominal dollars.

Summary

Nationally, government trade-war payments came close to covering farmers' estimated losses in aggregate, but coverage was very unevenly distributed. USDA's own Economic Research Service put direct U.S. agricultural export losses from foreign retaliatory tariffs at more than $27 billion from mid-2018 through the end of 2019 (95% of it from China's retaliation). In response, USDA distributed about $25.7 billion through three programs — the Market Facilitation Program (MFP, ~$23 billion), the Food Purchase and Distribution Program (~$1.2 billion in food purchased for nutrition assistance), and the Agricultural Trade Promotion program (~$300 million) — putting the aggregate national coverage ratio at roughly 85-95% of estimated losses, depending on which loss estimate is used as the denominator.

That national ratio hides a badly uneven distribution. GAO found that in 2019, corn producers received about $3 billion more than USDA's own estimated trade damages to corn, while payments to soybean, sorghum, and cotton producers were lower than their estimated trade damages — even though commodity crops overall (corn, cotton, soybeans, sorghum, wheat) took 94.5% of all MFP payments, with specialty crops receiving only about 1.5% and dairy/hogs about 4%. Soybeans alone accounted for 71% of the estimated $13.2 billion in annualized commodity losses (about $9.4 billion) per ERS, and took 85% of all 2018 MFP payments ($5.1 billion of $6.0 billion) — but by 2019, USDA changed its baseline methodology in ways that inflated corn's estimated damage roughly six-fold relative to 2018, spreading payments more broadly across commodities that had suffered comparatively little real trade damage.

Geography compounded the mismatch: a UC Davis analysis (Carter & Dong) found MFP payments to California farmers, who grow specialty crops like tree nuts and wine grapes that the formula largely excluded, covered only about half of California's net trade-war losses — MFP receipts equaled about 2% of California net farm income versus a 17% national average. The honest summary: nationally, coverage looked close to complete on paper; for the commodities and states actually hit hardest (soybeans and California specialty agriculture), payments covered well under the loss, while other producers, notably corn and wheat growers in 2019, were compensated beyond their measured trade damage.

The loss estimate: $27 billion in export losses, 2018-2019

USDA ERS (Morgan, Arita, Beckman et al., Economic Research Report 304, Jan 2022; summarized in the Amber Waves article "Retaliatory Tariffs Reduced U.S. States' Exports of Agricultural Commodities"), drawing on Grant et al. (2021), found that retaliatory tariffs imposed by China, the EU, Canada, Mexico, Turkey, and India reduced U.S. agricultural exports to those markets by more than $27 billion between mid-2018 and the end of 2019. China accounted for ~95% of the loss (nearly $26 billion, a 76% reduction in the value of exports to China), with the EU ($0.6B, a 42% reduction) and Mexico ($0.5B) a distant second and third. At the commodity level, annualized losses of $13.2 billion were highly concentrated: soybeans alone made up nearly 71% ($9.4 billion), followed by sorghum (6%, $854M) and pork (5%, $646M), with dairy 3% ($391M) and cotton 3% ($366M). Geographically, losses concentrated in the Midwest — Iowa ($1.46B), Illinois ($1.41B), and Kansas ($955M) accounted for roughly 11%, 11%, and 7% of the total, with soybean losses hitting Illinois ($1.3B) and Iowa ($1.2B) hardest and sorghum losses concentrated in Kansas ($478M) and Texas ($244M).

The government response: about $25.7 billion, mostly via MFP

USDA used Commodity Credit Corporation spending authority (no new congressional appropriation needed) to distribute roughly $25.7 billion across three vehicles (Congressional Research Service, R48548, May 2025): the Market Facilitation Program (direct per-acre/per-unit payments to producers, about $23 billion combined for 2018 and 2019), the Food Purchase and Distribution Program (government purchases of surplus commodities for nutrition programs), and the Agricultural Trade Promotion program (export-market development). USDA's own FOIA-disclosed payment data (analyzed by APM Research Lab and Marketplace) show the program paid nearly $6 billion in its first four months (Sept-Dec 2018) and about $14.4 billion more across 2019 to roughly 644,000 farm operations, with soybean growers receiving 85% of the 2018 total ($5.1B of ~$6.0B). On this framing, the response roughly matched the estimated loss — $25.7B in outlays against ~$27B in estimated losses, or about 95% coverage in aggregate nominal terms.

But coverage was badly uneven across commodities

GAO's review of the $23 billion MFP program (reported by Western Livestock Journal/DTN and Agri-Pulse, Dec 2021, on GAO-22-468/GAO-22-104259) found USDA's payments "significantly overestimated the actual trade damages suffered by producers of certain commodities" because USDA "decoupled" an individual commodity's trade damage from its actual payment rate. In 2019, corn producers received about $3 billion more in payments than USDA's own estimated trade damages to corn, while payments for soybean, sorghum, and cotton producers were lower than their estimated trade damages. USDA changed its formula between years: 2018 payments used 2017 export totals as the loss baseline (a method GAO called "justifiable"), while 2019 payments used each commodity's highest export value over the prior decade — a change that boosted the loss baseline, and therefore payments, for corn and wheat especially. USDA's 2019 county-based payment methodology also produced regional disparities unrelated to actual loss: corn producers in the South received an estimated $69/acre versus $61/acre in the Midwest, $34/acre in the Northeast, and just $29/acre in the West, because Southern counties' crop mix (more cotton, sorghum, and soybeans) drove up the county-level payment rate that then applied to every crop grown there, including corn, regardless of corn's own trade damage. Senate Agriculture Chair Debbie Stabenow, who requested the GAO review, said the findings showed USDA "picked winners and losers... regardless of whether those farmers actually experienced a larger loss."

And badly uneven across states/crops the formula wasn't built for

A UC Davis Agricultural Issues Center study by Colin Carter and Jiayi Dong compared 2018-2019 MFP payments to independent estimates of the net trade-war impact on California agriculture. It found MFP payments covered roughly half of California's estimated net trade-war losses — far below the near-full aggregate national coverage implied by the $25.7B-vs-$27B national comparison. MFP payments amounted to only about 2% of California net farm income, versus a 17% national average, because California's largest export crops — tree nuts, wine, dairy, and processed fruits and vegetables — were largely outside the row crops (soybeans, corn, wheat, cotton, sorghum, dairy, hogs) the MFP formula was built around. This matches the national commodity split reported by GAO: specialty crops received only about 1.5% of all MFP payments even though almonds, pistachios, and walnuts were directly targeted by Chinese, Indian, and Turkish retaliatory tariffs. The APM Research Lab / Marketplace payment microdata (obtained via FOIA) confirms specialty items like almonds ($22.2M in 2018), cherries, walnuts, cranberries, grapes, and pistachios collectively received well under 1% of total payments in most years.

Corroborating context: government payments as a share of farm income

USDA's Farm Income and Wealth Statistics series (schema ag, table ers_farm_income in this corpus, queried directly this session) puts total federal government payments to farmers of all kinds — not MFP alone, also including standing programs like ARC/PLC and CRP — at $11.5 billion in 2017, $13.7 billion in 2018, and $22.4 billion in 2019, consistent with MFP layering on top of baseline farm-program spending in 2019 specifically. This is a descriptive series, not an estimate isolating the trade-war-specific increment from ordinary program growth. The 2020 jump to $45.6 billion in the same series is COVID-era CFAP relief, unrelated to the trade war, shown only for scale. In the same table, the "Net income from government transactions" line — government payments' net contribution to farm income relative to what net farm income would otherwise have been — moved from roughly breakeven in 2018 (+$0.1B) to +$8.3B in 2019, indicating government payments were a large share of what kept aggregate net farm income from falling in 2019, even as the commodity- and state-level evidence above shows that aggregate stability masked which producers actually got made whole. A catalog search (search_catalog) for a dedicated trade-loss or retaliatory-tariff-damage table in this corpus turned up no direct match — the $27B ERS loss figure and the GAO per-commodity comparison had to come from primary published sources rather than a warehouse table built for this exact question.
