Government trade-war aid covered about 86% of farmers' 2018-19 export losses in aggregate — but badly mismatched by crop — and is running well short again in 2025-26

USDA MFP/TMP payments and 2025-26 Farmer Bridge Assistance vs. USDA ERS and farm-group loss estimates

Summary

There have been two U.S.-China trade wars with farm-payment responses. In 2018-19, USDA's Economic Research Service estimated retaliatory tariffs cost U.S. agriculture more than $27 billion in direct export losses (mid-2018 through end of 2019). The government's Market Facilitation Program (MFP) and related Trade Mitigation Program (TMP) payments totaled about $23.2 billion disbursed 2018-2023 — roughly 86% of the national loss figure, in aggregate. But that aggregate number hides a large mismatch: soybean growers alone were paid an estimated $5.4 billion more than their calculated trade damage, while corn growers were paid less than theirs — the program overcompensated the crop hit hardest and undercompensated others.

A second trade war is under way now (2025-26). China's retaliatory tariffs cut roughly $15 billion/year in annualized U.S. agricultural exports to China (and soybean exports to China alone fell from about $12.6B in 2024 to roughly $3B in 2025). The Trump administration's response is a $12 billion aid package, about $11 billion of it the "Farmer Bridge Assistance" program (recorded in USDA's own disbursement data as the "Emergency Commodity Assistance Program," $9.36 billion paid out through the 2025 data available). Farm groups say this is not enough: the American Farm Bureau Federation puts total farmer losses at $34 billion, and the American Soybean Association calculates that even after combining Bridge Assistance, crop insurance, and Price Loss Coverage payments, soybean growers are still ~$75 net loss per harvested acre short in 2025.

2018-19: what USDA's own research found the loss was

USDA ERS report ERR-304 ("The Economic Impacts of Retaliatory Tariffs on U.S. Agriculture," Morgan et al., January 2022) found retaliatory tariffs from China, the EU, Mexico, Canada, Turkey, and India cost U.S. agriculture more than $27 billion in direct export losses from 2018 through the end of 2019 (annualized at $13.2 billion/year). China accounted for ~95% of the loss ($25.7B). Soybeans alone accounted for 71% of the loss ($9.4B annualized); sorghum and pork were next largest.

2018-19: what the government actually paid

Querying USDA FSA's own commodity-payment disbursement data (AskAmerica ag.fsa_commodity_payments) for every Market Facilitation Program and Trade Mitigation Program line item, summed across all disbursement years 2018-2023, gives $23.16 billion — matching GAO's independently reported total of "about $23 billion" for the program's full run almost exactly, which cross-validates the figure. Broken down: $5.19B disbursed against 2018 losses, $14.18B against 2019 (mostly the enlarged 2019 MFP/TMP round), and the remainder in small trailing 2020-2023 payments.

Aggregate coverage: $23.16B / $27B ≈ 86% of USDA's own estimated national export-loss total (or roughly 117% of the annualized $13.2B/yr figure if payments are compared to only about 1.5 years of losses — the two loss figures are stated on different time bases, so both ratios are reported here rather than picking one).

That aggregate ratio, however, was not the story commodity by commodity. GAO and independent analysis (Choices Magazine, citing USDA's own after-the-fact damage re-estimates) found cotton, soybeans, and sorghum were each overcompensated, while corn was undercompensated. The single largest documented overpayment was to soybean growers, estimated at $5.4 billion above their $9.4B calculated loss — meaning soybean farmers, who suffered the biggest loss, also received noticeably more than 100% of it back, while farmers of some other affected commodities recovered less than the full amount.

2025-26: the current, still-unfolding round

As of September 2026 a second U.S.-China trade war is in progress and government aid is still being disbursed, so this cannot yet be scored with the same finality as 2018-19 — but the shape is visible. Farm-press reporting puts the annualized drop in U.S. agricultural exports to China at roughly $15 billion (March 2025-February 2026), with soybean exports to China alone falling from about $12.6B (2024) to roughly $3B (2025). The American Farm Bureau Federation's broader estimate of total farmer losses (not export losses alone, but the full financial squeeze including costs) is $34 billion.

The government's response is a $12 billion aid package announced by the administration, of which about $11 billion is the "Farmer Bridge Assistance" program — which appears in USDA's own FSA disbursement data under the official program name "EMERGENCY COMMODITY ASSISTANCE PROGRAM" (a press-name mismatch worth flagging: searching USDA data for "Farmer Bridge Assistance" by that name returns nothing). $9.36 billion of it had been disbursed as of the most recent data available in this pull.

Against the $15B annualized China export-loss figure, the $12B package covers roughly 80%. Against AFBF's broader $34B total-loss estimate, it covers about 35%. The American Soybean Association's own accounting — which nets out the Bridge Assistance per-acre payment ($30.88/acre for soybeans) together with crop insurance and Price Loss Coverage payments against total 2025 losses — finds soybean growers are still short by about $75 per harvested acre even after all forms of support, the second-largest shortfall of the nine largest U.S. row crops.

Bottom line

In the 2018-19 trade war, government payments covered close to all — roughly 85-90% — of USDA's own estimated national export losses, but the money was distributed unevenly enough that the hardest-hit crop (soybeans) was arguably overpaid while others (corn) were underpaid. In the still-unfolding 2025-26 trade war, the government's pledged aid covers a smaller share of a larger, less-certain loss estimate, and farm groups' own arithmetic says even combining every available support (trade aid, crop insurance, and farm-bill payments) still leaves working soybean farmers with a net per-acre loss.
