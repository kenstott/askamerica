Trade War Payments Covered About 85% of Farmers' Losses Nationally — But the Money Went to the Wrong Crops

USDA ERS trade-damage estimates vs. USDA Market Facilitation Program disbursements, 2018-2019

Summary

Nationally, in aggregate dollar terms, the government's trade-war payments came close to covering farmers' losses: USDA and its Farm Service Agency disbursed about $23.0 billion in Market Facilitation Program (MFP) payments for 2018-2019, against USDA's own Economic Research Service (ERS) estimate of $27.2 billion in cumulative agricultural export losses from retaliatory tariffs over that same mid-2018-through-2019 window — a national coverage ratio of roughly 85%. (Congress authorized up to $28 billion across the two years' packages, which would have been just over 100% of estimated losses if every dollar had gone out the door and been perfectly targeted.)

But that aggregate number hides a serious mismatch in where the money went. Soybean growers absorbed 71% of the total damage ($9.4 billion of $13.2 billion in annualized losses) but MFP's payment formulas were not calibrated to match each crop's actual loss. GAO found that for 2019 alone, corn producers received about $3 billion more than USDA's own estimate of corn's trade damage, while soybean, sorghum, and cotton producers were paid less than their estimated damage. A separate UC Davis analysis found that for California's specialty-crop-heavy agriculture — largely excluded from MFP's covered commodity list — the payments covered only about half of net losses, and the state's growers received MFP payments equal to just 2% of net farm income, versus a 17% national average share.

So the honest answer has two layers: at the national, aggregate level the payments came reasonably close (~85%) to matching total losses. But because MFP's payment formulas were tied to acreage and historical production rather than measured trade damage per crop, many of the farmers who lost the most (soybean, sorghum, cotton, and most fruit/vegetable/tree-nut growers) were undercompensated, while corn growers and Midwestern row-crop operations tied to covered commodities were overcompensated relative to their actual losses.

The numbers behind the 85% figure

  • Total estimated losses: USDA ERS's 2022 report (ERR-304, "The Economic Impacts of Retaliatory Tariffs on U.S. Agriculture") put cumulative U.S. agricultural export losses from six retaliating trading partners (China, Canada, Mexico, the EU, India, Turkey) at more than $27 billion from mid-2018 through the end of 2019 — equivalent to $13.2 billion per year. China alone accounted for roughly $26 billion of that, about 96% of total losses.
  • Total government payments actually made: USDA's Farm Service Agency disbursed $8.6 billion in 2018 MFP payments and $14.4 billion in 2019 MFP payments — $23.0 billion combined — to roughly 600,000-plus farm operations, per GAO's tally (GAO-22-104259).
  • Authorized but not fully needed/disbursed: The 2018 and 2019 trade-aid packages (which also included food-purchase and export-promotion spending beyond direct MFP payments) were authorized up to $12 billion and $16 billion respectively — $28 billion total, which is slightly more than the $27.2 billion in estimated losses.
  • Resulting national coverage ratio: $23.0B paid / $27.2B lost = ~85%.

Why the aggregate ratio overstates how well any individual farmer was made whole

MFP payment rates were set per unit of production (e.g., per bushel or per acre) using USDA's own damage estimates by commodity — and GAO's oversight reviews found those estimates and the resulting payment rates did not track actual measured trade damage well:

  • For 2019, GAO found corn producers received about $3 billion more than USDA's own estimated trade damage to corn, largely because USDA's baseline export-value assumptions (e.g., for wheat and corn) were inflated relative to realistic counterfactual export volumes.
  • Conversely, soybean, sorghum, and cotton producers — the commodities that actually bore the largest share of real damage — were paid less than their estimated trade damage. Soybean growers alone absorbed 71% of the total loss ($9.4B of $13.2B annualized) but did not receive a proportionally larger share of the $23B pot.
  • Coverage of specialty crops (fruits, vegetables, tree nuts) and dairy was thin because most of these were partly or fully excluded from MFP's list of "covered commodities," even though fruit alone accounted for over $600 million in annual losses and tree nuts $219 million.
  • A UC Davis study (published via Agri-Pulse, Jan 2021) found MFP payments covered only about half of California's net trade-war losses, because MFP was structured around Midwestern row-crop commodities; California received MFP payments equal to only 2% of its net farm income versus a 17% national average.

In short: the $23B vs $27.2B comparison is the right aggregate number to answer "how much of the total loss did payments cover," and it says roughly five-sixths. But it is not the right number for "were farmers made whole" — the allocation mechanism meant many of the worst-hit producers recovered well under 85 cents on the dollar, while some others were paid more than their damage.
