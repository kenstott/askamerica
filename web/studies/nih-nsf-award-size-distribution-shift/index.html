Federal research grants are not simply "bigger or more numerous" — real award size has been flat for two decades, dispersion has widened, and 2025-2026 brought an outright contraction in award counts

NCSES Survey of Federal Funds for R&D (2016-2025); NIH RPG award-size analysis 1998-2021 (Lauer, Wang & Roychowdhury 2023, eLife); NSF FY2026 (Grant Witness / USAspending)

Summary

There is no single trend — federal research grants are simultaneously flat in real size, more dispersed (more very-large and very-small awards, fewer mid-size ones), and, as of FY2025-2026, sharply contracting in count at NSF and under pressure at NIH. Total federal research obligations rose 42% in nominal dollars from FY2016 to FY2025 ($67.1B to $95.2B), but only 5.8% after CPI inflation — and real obligations actually peaked in FY2023 at $107.0B and have fallen 11% since. At NIH, the agency's own inflation-adjusted analysis (using its research-specific BRDPI deflator) shows the average Research Project Grant has been essentially flat in real terms since 2003 — around $610k at the end of the 2003 budget doubling, a dip to ~$520k during 2013 sequestration, and back to ~$570k since 2014 — while award counts rose during the doubling, fell gradually through 2015, then rose again with recent budget increases. So historically, grants have not been "getting bigger"; what has changed is the spread — NIH is funding more extreme-large ($5M+ real) and extreme-small solicited projects, thinning the middle. The most dramatic recent shift is not a size trend at all: NSF made 60-70% fewer new awards through May 2026 than in prior years, funding was 30-40% lower, and — tellingly — average award size actually shrank about $100,000 (fewer $30M+ mega-awards) while median award size rose (fewer small awards/fellowships survived cuts), a bifurcation, not a uniform move in either direction.

Step 1 — What askamerica's own tables can and cannot answer directly

The research schema's award-level microdata (research.nih_award_projects) is exactly the table built to answer "is award size/count shifting toward fewer-bigger or more-smaller grants" — but a direct query showed it currently holds only one fiscal year (FY2026, 59,813 rows), not the multi-year run needed for a trend (declared window 2022-2026; only 2026 is actually loaded). A single year cannot show a trend by itself, so this table alone cannot answer the question as asked.

The aggregate table research.nsf_rd_by_field (NCSES Survey of Federal Funds for R&D, all-agency research obligations by field, FY2016-2025) does carry a real multi-year series, but only in total dollars — it has no award-count or per-award-size column. That table was queried directly (SELECT year, SUM(obligations_usd_million) ... GROUP BY year) and is the source of the total-obligations chart above; it answers "how much" but not "how many awards, how big each."

Because askamerica's own microdata could not answer the award-size-distribution question directly, this report follows the coverage-gap protocol: it fetched the primary literature and agency analyses that do carry award-level size and count history, rather than guessing from the one-year table or from memory.

Step 2 — Total federal research funding: nominal growth is mostly inflation, and real dollars have declined since 2023

From research.nsf_rd_by_field (rows where field_level = 0, i.e. the grand "All fields" total, summed across all funding agencies and all performers): nominal federal research obligations rose from $67.1B (FY2016) to $95.2B (FY2025), a 41.9% increase. Deflating with BLS CPI-U (via the connector's adjust_inflation tool, base year 2025) drops that to a 5.8% real increase over the same nine years — and the path is not monotonic: real obligations climbed to a peak of $107.0B in FY2023, then fell to $102.2B (FY2024) and $95.2B (FY2025), an 11% real decline from the 2023 peak. So "grants getting bigger" cannot be read off the nominal total-dollar trend; most of the apparent nominal growth is price inflation, and the real trend has reversed in the most recent two years.

Note: CPI-U is a general consumer-price deflator, not research-specific. NIH's own analysis (Step 3) uses a sector-specific deflator (BRDPI) precisely because research inputs — personnel, lab equipment, clinical trials — inflate at a different rate than a general consumer basket; CPI is used here only because it's what askamerica's adjust_inflation tool carries, and it is a reasonable general-purpose check, not a substitute for BRDPI.

Step 3 — NIH award size over two decades: flat real average, growing dispersion, not a clean "bigger" trend

Because askamerica has no multi-year NIH award microdata, this report fetched NIH's own peer-reviewed analysis directly: Lauer, Wang & Roychowdhury, "National Institutes of Health research project grant inflation 1998 to 2021," eLife 12:e84245 (2023), authored by NIH's own Office of the Director and Office of Extramural Research, using NIH's Biomedical Research and Development Price Index (BRDPI) — a sector-specific deflator built from NIH's own input-cost weights (personnel, supplies, equipment), not general CPI.

Key findings, read directly from the paper (fetched via web_fetch, not a search-result summary):

  • Between FY1998 and FY2021, NIH issued 827,815 Research Project Grant (RPG) awards of at least $25,000/year (BRDPI-indexed). Award counts rose during the 1998-2003 budget doubling, decreased gradually through FY2015, then rose again with recent budget increases.
  • Real average and median RPG cost (FY2021 BRDPI dollars) rose from about $530k to about $610k during the doubling (1998-2003), fell to a nadir of about $520k in FY2013 (budget sequestration), rebounded in FY2014, and has stayed roughly stable around $570k since — essentially flat for the past decade in real terms.
  • The paper's own headline point: "the degree of variation of RPG costs has changed over time, with more marked extremes observed on both higher and lower levels of cost." NIH is funding a growing share of very large (>=$5M real) projects and more very-small solicited projects, with nearly half of RPG money now going to solicited (not investigator-initiated) projects — the distribution is widening/bifurcating even though its center (mean/median) is not moving much.
  • After adjusting for confounders, the authors found no independent association of time with real costs — changes in real average cost are explained by shifts in the composition of the grant portfolio (more R01s vs. R21s, more program grants vs. small grants), not by a uniform inflationary or size-growth trend.

Bottom line for NIH: over the 1998-2021 window, grants were not simply "getting bigger" in real terms — the average and median have been flat-to-cyclical, tracking the NIH budget cycle (doubling, sequestration, recovery) far more than any secular size-growth trend, while the shape of the distribution has spread out at both tails.

Step 4 — The 2026 break: NSF award counts collapsing, with size effects going in opposite directions by statistic

The most consequential recent change is not a size trend at all but a count collapse. Grant Witness (grantwitness.org), an independent tracker built directly on USAspending.gov award data, reported (fetched directly, 2026-06-30 post) that through May 31, 2026 — compared to the same date in prior fiscal years — NSF made 60-70% fewer new awards and obligated 30-40% less total funding. This was shown to Congress in a June 2026 House Science Committee hearing.

On size specifically, the two central-tendency statistics moved in opposite directions, which is itself the finding: average NSF award size is about $100,000 smaller in 2026 than the prior five-year average, driven by the near-absence of very large awards (no $30M+ projects initiated in 2026, versus prior years). But median award size is larger, because NSF cut small awards and fellowships (e.g., I-Corps) disproportionately. Separately, NSF has been "forward-funding" — obligating a larger up-front share of an award's total budget rather than spreading it via multi-year Continuing Grants (the same mechanism NIH has also leaned on via "multiyear funding") — which mechanically raises per-award dollars obligated in a single year without giving researchers any more total money or funding any more science; it just compresses fewer, more front-loaded awards into the count.

So for 2026 specifically: neither "bigger" nor "more numerous" describes what's happening. It is fewer awards, a shrunken mean driven by lost mega-grants, a raised median driven by lost small grants, and a funding mechanism shift (front-loading) that inflates apparent per-award size without any real growth in support.

Answer

Size distribution: Over the long run (NIH, 1998-2021, the only multi-decade award-level analysis located), real average/median grant size has been roughly flat since the 2003 budget doubling ended, cycling with the NIH budget (nadir at 2013 sequestration, recovery since 2014) rather than trending steadily upward — but the spread of award sizes has widened, with a growing share of both very-large (>=$5M real) and very-small solicited awards squeezing the middle of the distribution.

Bigger or more numerous: Historically, it has been more about count cycling with the budget than size growing — real average cost is roughly the same today as in 2003, while counts rose, fell, and rose again with NIH funding levels. As of FY2025-2026, the picture has flipped to outright contraction: total federal research obligations in real (CPI-adjusted) dollars fell 11% from their 2023 peak, and NSF specifically cut new award counts by 60-70% year-to-date in 2026 versus its own recent history — with average award size actually shrinking due to lost mega-grants even as the median nudged up because small awards were cut hardest. In short: not simply bigger, not simply more numerous — fewer overall in the most recent year, more dispersed in size over the longer run, and real (inflation-adjusted) total funding has been declining since 2023.
