Federal research grants are shifting toward fewer, larger awards — a trend visible across NIH and NSF since 2022 and accelerating sharply in 2025-2026

NIH RePORTER award microdata (AskAmerica research.nih_award_projects), FY2022-FY2026, cross-checked against NSF and published reporting for FY2025

Summary

Federal research grants are getting bigger and fewer, not smaller-and-more-numerous. NIH award-level data (research.nih_award_projects, NIH RePORTER) shows the median award rose from $376,949 in FY2022 to $391,597 in FY2024, and the share of awards worth $500,000 or more rose from 33.4% to 37.1% over that same span — while the award count held roughly flat (~79,000-81,000/year). That gradual pre-2025 drift became a sharp break in FY2025-2026: published NIH data show the average competitive-grant size roughly doubled within calendar 2025 (from about $472,000 in the first half to over $830,000 by year end), NIH issued about 5,600 fewer awards in FY2025 than FY2024 (an 8.6% drop), and by partial-year FY2026 the connector's own data shows the $500k-1M bucket had grown from 24.5% of awards (FY2022) to 36.0% of awards, with awards under $500k falling from 66.6% to 55.9% of the total. NSF shows the identical pattern: roughly 11,000 new research grants in FY2024 fell to about 8,800 in FY2025 (-20%), while average award size rose about 15%, from ~$390,000 to ~$450,000. The consensus explanation across NIH and NSF reporting is a policy shift toward "forward-funding" — agencies increasingly pay out a multi-year grant's full commitment in one lump sum up front rather than in annual installments — which mechanically produces fewer, larger individual award records even before any change in total research investment.

Step 1 — What the connector's own award-level data shows (FY2022-FY2024, complete years)

research.nih_award_projects is NIH RePORTER award-level microdata — one row per funded project per fiscal year — the only table in this corpus with individual-award size and count rather than agency/institution totals. Over the three complete fiscal years it holds:

Across these three stable, fully-loaded years, award count barely moved (79,700 → 80,835 → 79,237) while the median grant size rose 3.9% nominally and the large-award share climbed nearly 4 points — a real, if gradual, shift toward bigger grants even before any of the 2025 funding disruption.

Step 2 — A genuine data gap: FY2025 is missing from this table, and it is the pivotal year

A coverage scan (data_coverage) of research.nih_award_projects found FY2025 is an interior gap — present in the table's declared 2022-2026 window but holding zero rows — and FY2026 itself is only partially loaded (51,142 rows vs. ~79,000-81,000 in a complete year, roughly two-thirds of a normal year's volume). This matters because FY2025 is exactly the year every outside source identifies as the inflection point: NIH cut roughly 5,600 awards (-8.6%) between FY2024 and FY2025, and the average competitive-grant size nearly doubled within the year. The connector cannot show that transition directly. To answer the question fully we pulled the FY2025 figures from primary NIH reporting relayed by the Association of American Universities, Science, and FierceBiotech (cited below) rather than leaving the trend unstated for that year.

Step 3 — The 2025-2026 acceleration, from published reporting

  • NIH awarded 7,720 R01-equivalent grants in FY2024 vs. 5,885 in FY2025.
  • NIH issued about 5,564 fewer total grants in FY2025 than FY2024, an 8.6% decline, despite the overall research budget falling only about 1%.
  • The average NIH grant size rose from about $573,000 (2024) to over $622,000 (2025), a 9% year-over-year rise on an annual-average basis — but the within-year trajectory was far sharper: the average competitive grant went from about $472,000 in the first half of 2025 to more than $830,000 by year end.
  • By the first five months of FY2026, NIH had issued 66% fewer grant awards than the FY2021-24 average for the same months, and only 17% of research-grant applicants were funded in FY2025 — the lowest NIH success rate in 30 years, down from roughly 30% for early-stage investigators in FY2023 to 18.5% in FY2025.
  • NSF shows the same shape: ~11,000 new research-project grants in FY2024 fell to ~8,800 in FY2025 (-20%), while average award size rose ~15% (~$390k → ~$450k); total NSF grant dollars stayed roughly flat near $8.17B, so the money simply concentrated into fewer, bigger checks.

Step 4 — Why: the mechanism is a funding-mechanics change, not (only) more research money

Multiple sources attribute this to a 2024-25 White House budget-office policy requiring agencies to pay the FULL multi-year commitment of a grant up front in one lump sum, rather than releasing it in annual installments across the grant's life as had been standard practice. This forces NIH and NSF to commit a much larger share of a fixed annual budget to each award they do make, mechanically shrinking the number of NEW awards a fixed budget can support while inflating the recorded size of each individual award — an artifact of accounting/disbursement timing layered on top of any real change in total research investment. This is an important caveat: part of the "bigger grants" trend is not researchers getting more real annual research money per project, but multiple years of money being registered as a single record instead of several.

Step 5 — Real vs. nominal dollars

Deflating the connector's own median-award series to constant 2024 dollars (BLS CPI-U, via adjust_inflation) shows the pre-2025 drift was smaller in real terms than nominal: real median award fell slightly from $404,041 (2022) to $391,597 (2024, the base year) before recovering to $425,670 in real terms by partial-year FY2026 — still an increase of about 5% over the FY2022 real level and about 9% above FY2024. CPI-U is a general consumer-price deflator, not a research-specific one (e.g., NIH's own Biomedical Research and Development Price Index, BRDPI, is not loaded in this corpus); research inputs such as clinical-trial costs, lab personnel, and specialized equipment can inflate faster than the general CPI, so this real-dollar figure should be read as directionally indicative, not exact.

Bottom line

Grants are getting bigger, not more numerous. The complete-year NIH data (FY2022-24) shows a modest, steady drift toward larger awards; FY2025-26 reporting (not present in this table, sourced externally) shows that drift becoming a sharp break — double-digit percentage cuts in award counts at both NIH and NSF, paired with average award sizes rising 9-15% year over year and briefly nearly doubling intra-year at NIH — concentrated substantially in a policy shift toward up-front, lump-sum multi-year funding rather than (only) a change in total research dollars available.
