Construction Employment Is Turning Down First in California and New York, While Wages Accelerate Nearly Everywhere

BLS CES/QCEW state data (AskAmerica), cross-checked against Cox &amp; East (NBER w35129, 2026) and industry reporting. Prepared 2026-09-15.

Summary

Yes — the effect is visible in the data, and it shows up first and most clearly in California, with New York close behind. Nationally, construction payroll employment growth has decelerated to near-stagnation (BLS CES: roughly +2% over the two years from May 2024 to May 2026, versus much faster growth earlier in the cycle), while construction wages kept rising close to 4% year over year — the signature of a supply-constrained, not demand-constrained, labor market. State-level BLS data (QCEW and CES) show California's construction employment fell outright in 2025 (-2.3%, its first annual decline since the 2020 pandemic year) even as its average weekly wage rose 4.4%; New York's employment also declined slightly (-0.8%) with wages up 4.4%. Both turned negative year-over-year as early as January 2025 and stayed negative through most of the following 18 months. By contrast, Texas and Nevada — also high-immigrant-share construction workforces — kept adding jobs through mid-2026, but paid up sharply to do it: Nevada's construction wage grew 7.0% in 2025, the fastest of the six states examined, and Florida's wage growth (5.3%) also accelerated even as its employment growth stalled to roughly flat. A national NBER study (Cox & East, 2026) independently confirms a causal channel: localities with sudden increases in ICE arrests saw construction employment among likely-undocumented workers fall 7.5%, more than any other tracked industry, alongside a documented "chilling effect" (workers who were never detained staying away from jobsites) that is now several times larger than in earlier enforcement eras.

What the national data show

Using BLS Current Employment Statistics (state industry supersector series, not seasonally adjusted, queried directly from the AskAmerica warehouse), summed across all 50 states + DC by month:

  • National construction employment grew roughly 1.5-1.8% year-over-year in early-to-mid 2025, but that growth rate had decayed to under 1% by Q1 2026 (Jan 2026 vs Jan 2025: +1.8%; by April 2026 vs April 2025: +0.8%).
  • Construction Dive/BLS reporting corroborates this at a headline level: employment grew less than 2% between May 2024 and May 2026, and 92% of contractors report difficulty filling positions even as spending growth has been positive.
  • Construction wage growth nationally has been running close to 4% year-over-year, but decelerated from about 4.3% to 3.1% by Q1 2026 per industry reporting (ConstructConnect) — a sign demand itself is also softening, which complicates a clean read on the labor-supply story at the national level.

California is the standout: it is the earliest state to turn negative, it stays negative for the longest continuous stretch, and its 2025 employment decline (-2.3%) is the first calendar-year drop in California construction employment since the COVID-19 shock in 2020. New York shows the same directional pattern, one to two months behind. New Jersey followed later, only turning persistently negative starting in 2026. Texas, Florida and Nevada — despite comparably high or higher immigrant shares of their construction workforce (NAHB estimates immigrant participation "approaches or surpasses 40%" in California, Texas and Florida) — kept adding construction jobs throughout, but did so while wages accelerated the most (Nevada +7.0%, Florida +5.3%), which is consistent with employers bidding up pay to compete for a shrinking available labor pool even where net hiring did not turn negative.

Reconciling with the causal literature

Cox & East, "Labor Market Impacts of ICE Activity in Trump 2.0" (NBER Working Paper 35129, revised June 2026), is the first causal, national event-study/difference-in-differences analysis of this question. Using local variation in the timing and intensity of ICE arrests, they find: (1) areas with large, sudden increases in arrests see a 1.8-percentage-point decline in the employment rate of likely-undocumented immigrants in high-immigrant-share sectors (construction and manufacturing, defined as sectors where undocumented workers exceed 5% of the workforce), rising to 3.5 points for men in those sectors; (2) construction is the single most-affected industry they track, with a 7.5% drop in employment among likely-undocumented workers in areas with recent ICE raids; (3) employment among U.S.-born men also falls in affected areas, which the authors and a co-author (Elizabeth Cox, interviewed by Fortune) attribute to a combination of "chilling effect" no-shows among documented workers and citizens, and complementarity — fewer laborers means fewer electricians, foremen and managers needed to support them; and (4) the chilling-effect multiplier (workers who leave employment per detention, without being detained themselves) has grown from about 2.3 in an earlier Obama-era study to more than 7 under the current enforcement regime.

That national, causally-identified result is consistent with what the state-level BLS data show: construction is disproportionately hit, and the effect is not limited to direct removals — it shows up as broad wage pressure even in states (TX, NV, FL) where aggregate employment kept growing, and as outright employment declines specifically in the two states (CA, NY) with the earliest and highest-profile enforcement actions and the tightest, most cost-constrained housing markets.

Caveats

  • This is descriptive state-level evidence, not a causal state-level estimate. California's 2025 construction slowdown coincides with several other state-specific factors — elevated mortgage rates, weak housing demand, high insurance and materials costs, and the aftermath of the January 2025 Los Angeles-area wildfires — that a bare before/after comparison cannot separate from an enforcement effect. The causal claim in this report rests on the NBER paper's own identification strategy (local variation in ICE arrest timing/intensity), not on our state comparison, which is offered as corroborating, not causal, evidence.
  • BLS CES (state_industry) data are not seasonally adjusted, so all comparisons above are strictly year-over-year, same-month-to-same-month, to avoid mistaking normal seasonal swings (construction employment always dips in winter) for a trend.
  • 2026 data are provisional and partial (through July 2026 for CES, and 2025 is the most recent complete year for QCEW, released with roughly a one-year lag) and subject to BLS revision.
  • Trade-level exposure matters and is not visible in these state aggregates. Per NAHB/JCHS research cited below, immigrant workforce share varies enormously by trade — 57% for drywall installers and 53% for roofers (concentrated in residential homebuilding) versus 14-16% for heavy equipment operators and structural iron/steel workers (concentrated in nonresidential/infrastructure work). This likely explains part of why Texas — whose current construction boom is skewed toward data-center and other nonresidential/infrastructure projects — has been comparatively insulated, while California and New York's more residential-and-multifamily-heavy markets show the earliest strain.
