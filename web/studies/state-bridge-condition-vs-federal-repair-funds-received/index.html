Pennsylvania, New York, Michigan, Illinois, Massachusetts and Maine got big federal bridge awards since 2022 and still have 9-15% of bridges in poor condition

USAspending FHWA bridge grants (CFDA 20.263/20.264), awards starting 2022-2026; FHWA National Bridge Inventory 2025, record_type=1

Answer

Pennsylvania is the clearest case: the largest recipient of bridge awards since 2022 (about $1.07B) and 12.1% of its bridges rated poor, against 6.7% nationally. Next are New York ($280M, 9.8% poor), Massachusetts ($238M, 9.4%), Illinois ($200M, 9.5%), Maine ($199M, 15.0%, the highest poor share among big per-bridge recipients), Michigan ($180M, 11.0%) and, at smaller dollar totals, Iowa (18.6%, $124M) and West Virginia (17.8%, $86M). Ohio, California, Alabama and New Jersey got large awards but have lower poor shares (3-6%).

Across all 51 units, money and poor share are essentially unrelated (r = +0.13 on total dollars, -0.09 per bridge; n=51). So the overlap is a handful of states, not a pattern.

Method and caveats

  • Money: sum of award_amount in fiscal.usaspending_bridge_awards for awards starting 2022 or later (about $6.8B total). This covers only the Bridge Formula and Bridge Investment grants as recorded in USAspending, keyed to the state DOT's registered address, and is not the full federal highway bridge spend (it excludes regular Federal-aid highway funds spent on bridges). Award timing is by performance start, not apportionment, so totals understate formula apportionments.
  • Condition: share of record_type=1 structures in the 2025 NBI with FHWA bridge_condition = P (41,685 of 624,862 = 6.7%, matching FHWA's published national figure). Counts, not deck area or traffic weighted.
  • Poor condition today partly reflects deterioration that funds have not yet reached; 2022+ awards are mostly recent and many projects are unfinished, so this is not evidence that the money failed.
  • I did not test causal effects; n=51 and funding is formula-driven by bridge need, which would itself link money to poor bridges.
