Corn Belt Yields Have Nearly Doubled Since 1980 — Not Because of, But Despite/Alongside, Modest Growing-Season Warming

USDA NASS yield history, NOAA GHCND temperature data, and the peer-reviewed literature on Corn Belt climate trends

Summary

US corn belt crop yields have risen dramatically since 1980 — national average corn yield rose from 91 to 177 bushels/acre (+95%, +1.92 bu/acre/year, r²=0.83) and soybean yield rose from 26.5 to 50.6 bushels/acre (+91%) between 1980 and 2023. Over that same window, growing-season (April-September) temperatures across the 10-state Corn Belt (IA, IL, IN, NE, MN, OH, SD, WI, MO, KS) have not shown a statistically significant warming trend in the peer-reviewed and applied-economics literature, and the hottest days of the growing season have if anything cooled slightly even as average temperatures nationally rose. So the honest answer is: yields climbed steeply, but the growing-season warming that a simple headline like "as temperatures rose" implies has been much smaller and patchier in the Corn Belt specifically than at the national or global level — and the yield gains are attributable overwhelmingly to genetics, agronomy, and management, not to a temperature-driven boost or despite a temperature-driven drag.

1. Yield growth: the headline number

Using USDA NASS's published national annual corn-yield series (sourced via a Visual Capitalist compilation of NASS data, cross-checked against USDA-NASS's own historical-yield narrative from Purdue University), corn yield grew from 91.0 bu/acre in 1980 to 177.3 bu/acre in 2023 — a 94.8% increase, or +1.92 bu/acre per year on a linear trend (computed directly from the year-by-year series with numpy polyfit; r²=0.83). USDA NASS's own historical-yield note (Purdue Extension, R.L. Nielsen) independently states the trend rate since the mid-1950s has held at "almost 2 bushels per acre per year" with no detectable acceleration from genetically modified traits introduced in the 1990s — corroborating the computed slope. Soybean yield rose from 26.5 to 50.6 bu/acre over the same window (+90.9%, +0.53 bu/acre/year, r²=0.88).

This is the well-established "yield trend" literature: continuous, roughly linear gains driven by hybrid/variety genetics, fertilizer and pesticide technology, planting density, and precision management — not a step change from any single input.

2. Did growing-season temperature actually rise in the Corn Belt?

This is the part of the question that needs the most care, because "American farm belt" temperatures have NOT tracked the national/global warming trend uniformly. A University of Illinois farmdoc daily analysis (Irwin, 2024) built an acreage-weighted growing-season temperature series for the same 10 Corn Belt states across two windows, 1980-2023 and 1895-2023, using NWS/Midwest Regional Climate Center monthly station data. The finding: of 16 monthly temperature-trend coefficients tested (April-September, both windows), only ONE was statistically significant — September temperature, +3.1°F over 1980-2023. Nine of the sixteen temperature trend coefficients were actually negative. July temperature — the single most yield-critical month — showed a slight DOWNWARD trend in both the 1980-2023 and 1895-2023 windows; July 1936 (Dust Bowl) remains the all-time Corn Belt heat record, still warmer than the historic 2012 drought.

This matches a separate, peer-reviewed finding: Butler, Mueller & Huybers (2018, PNAS, "Peculiarly Pleasant Weather for US Maize") found that while average temperatures warmed nationally, the hottest days of the Midwest growing season actually cooled since 1981 — attributed partly to increased evapotranspiration from denser, more productive crop canopies cooling the local microclimate, and partly to earlier planting (shifting ~3 days/decade earlier) that lets crops mature before the hottest part of summer. Their analysis attributes roughly one-quarter of the 1981-onward yield gain to this favorable, not adverse, shift in growing-season weather.

The AskAmerica NOAA-GHCND-derived county weather data corroborates the same DIRECTION but is not a reliable independent trend estimate on its own: aggregating April-September daily max temperature across the same 10 Corn Belt states for connector years with usable station coverage (2010, 2011, 2013, 2015, 2021-2025 — several years, including all of 2012, 2014, 2016-2020, had too few reporting stations to trust) gives a slope of only +0.029°C/year with r=0.36 and n=9 — a small, statistically weak signal over too short and too gap-ridden a window to be treated as a climate trend on its own. It is consistent with, not independent confirmation of, the longer farmdoc/Butler-Huybers finding of muted Corn Belt growing-season warming.

3. Reconciling the two facts

Nationally and globally, growing-season and annual temperatures have risen measurably since 1980 (Midwest region overall: NOAA's National Climate Assessment reports the broader Midwest's average air temperature rose more than 1.5°F from 1900-2010, with warming accelerating after 1980). But the specific combination of factors that matters agronomically for corn and soybeans in the Corn Belt states — daytime growing-season highs, especially in July — has NOT shown a significant warming trend, and extreme-heat days have if anything eased. Farmers also adapted (earlier planting, denser stands, drought-tolerant hybrids), which is itself a partial response to a changing climate even where the raw temperature trend is flat.

The long-run literature on extreme heat (Schlenker & Roberts 2009, PNAS) establishes that corn/soybean yields are non-linearly damaged once temperatures exceed ~29°C (84°F)/30°C (86°F) thresholds — so the RIGHT worry is extreme-heat exposure (degree-days above threshold), not average growing-season temperature. Given the farmdoc/Butler-Huybers finding that Corn Belt extreme-heat days have been trending DOWN, this explains why yields have not shown the heat-driven damage that a simple 'temperatures rose -> yields fell' framing would predict, and why yields instead climbed steadily on the back of technology and agronomy. Projections for MID-CENTURY are different: NCA4/5's Midwest chapter and multiple crop-model studies project growing-season warming becomes the dominant NEGATIVE driver of Corn Belt productivity later this century (estimates of 5%-25%+ yield declines by mid-century absent further adaptation), even though it has not been the dominant driver of the historical 1980-2023 record.

4. Data-quality note on this connector

The AskAmerica warehouse's ag.nass_crop_production table, which should carry USDA NASS's own state-level annual corn/soybean yield series, is severely under-populated for the core Corn Belt states: Iowa (the top corn-producing state) has only 6 years of state-level GRAIN/BU-per-acre corn-yield rows loaded out of a possible 31 (1995-2025); Illinois, Indiana, Nebraska, and Ohio show similar gaps; county-level Iowa corn yield returns zero rows for 2023 entirely. This was flagged via report_issue during this analysis. As a result, the yield figures in this report are drawn from USDA NASS's own published national series (via a secondary compilation cross-checked against a second USDA-NASS-derived university source), not recomputed from the connector's own crop table. The connector's weather.weather_daily_by_county table also has multi-year coverage gaps (2012, 2014, 2016-2020 have well under 1% of the station-days present in complete years), which limited the connector-native temperature trend to a 9-year, gap-filled subset — presented here as a directional corroboration only, not a stand-alone climate-trend estimate.

    WITH by_year AS (SELECT w."year" AS yr, avg(w.tmax_c) AS avg_tmax FROM weather.weather_daily_by_county w JOIN geo.counties c ON c.county_fips = w.county_fips WHERE c.state_fips IN ('19','17','18','31','27','39','46','55','29','20') AND w."year" IN (2010,2011,2013,2015,2021,2022,2023,2024,2025) AND CAST(substr(w.date,6,2) AS INTEGER) BETWEEN 4 AND 9 GROUP BY w."year") SELECT regr_slope(avg_tmax, yr) AS slope_c_per_year, corr(avg_tmax, yr) AS r, count(*) n FROM by_year
