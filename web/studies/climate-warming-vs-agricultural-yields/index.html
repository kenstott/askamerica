Corn Belt yields have roughly doubled since 1980 — but the rise tracks technology, not growing-season warming

USDA NASS crop yields, Purdue/farmdoc climatology, and an AskAmerica-computed Corn Belt temperature series (NOAA GHCND)

Summary

U.S. corn yields in the Corn Belt have risen enormously and steadily since the 1950s — about 1.9 bushels/acre every year, taking the national average from roughly 90 bu/acre in the early 1980s to a record 186.5 bu/acre in 2025 (up from 179.3 in 2024). But the premise that this rise tracks rising growing-season temperature does not hold up well: peer-reviewed and USDA-adjacent climatology of the Corn Belt (Illinois farmdoc daily, 2024; Butler, Mueller & Huybers, PNAS 2018) finds no statistically significant long-run trend in growing-season (April-August) monthly temperature over either 1980-2023 or the full 1895-2023 record for the 10 core Corn Belt states — September is the sole exception, up 3.1°F since 1980. Meanwhile a separate USDA ERS analysis finds heat-stress-relevant extreme degree days (hours above 86°F) and growing degree days have risen steadily since 1992. An AskAmerica-computed check against NOAA station data for 2010-2025 (with four years excluded for a station-coverage gap in this corpus, reported below) shows a modest recent warming of about 0.07°C/year in the May-September mean. Net: yield growth is overwhelmingly a technology and management story (hybrid genetics since the late 1930s, then genetics+inputs since the mid-1950s), not a response to a strong growing-season warming trend — the Corn Belt has been comparatively climate-stable relative to the rest of the warming globe, even as yields kept climbing.

1. How much have yields changed?

USDA-NASS's own long-run trend analysis (reproduced by Purdue University's R.L. Nielsen) breaks U.S. corn yield history into three eras:

  • 1866-1936 (open-pollinated varieties): essentially flat, averaging ~26 bu/acre for 70 years.
  • 1937-1955 (early hybrid adoption): yield growth begins, averaging about +0.8 bu/acre/year.
  • Mid-1950s to present (modern hybrids, nitrogen fertilizer, pesticides, biotech traits, precision agriculture): a steady +1.9 bu/acre/year, a rate that has NOT accelerated with the 1990s adoption of transgenic (Bt/herbicide-tolerant) traits — the trend line's slope is essentially unchanged since the 1950s.

Recent national published estimates: 2020: 171.4 bu/acre; 2021: 177.0; 2022: 173.3; 2023: 177.3 (a record at the time); 2024: 179.3; 2025: 186.5 bu/acre, a new record, +7.2 bu/acre over 2024. For contrast, the historic 2012 drought year (the worst Corn Belt growing-season weather shock of the last two decades) produced only 123 bu/acre — 23% below trend — illustrating how much a single bad weather year can swing output even as the multi-decade trend keeps rising.

2. How much have growing-season temperatures actually risen?

This is the part of the question that needs the most scrutiny. A widely cited farmdoc daily analysis (Scott Irwin, University of Illinois, May 2024) built an acreage-weighted monthly temperature and precipitation series for the same 10 Corn Belt states (Iowa, Illinois, Indiana, Minnesota, Missouri, Nebraska, North Dakota, Ohio, South Dakota, Wisconsin) using National Weather Service data back to 1895, and tested linear trends in every growing-season month (April-September) over two windows: 1980-2023 and the full 1895-2023 record.

Result: of 12 monthly temperature-and-precipitation-trend variables tested, only ONE was statistically significant at the 5% level — September temperature, up 3.1°F over 1980-2023. Nine of the 16 temperature-trend coefficients examined were actually negative. July temperature — the single most important weather variable for corn yield in econometric crop-weather models — showed a slight downward trend since 1980, and a slight downward trend since 1895 as well. The single hottest July on record for the Corn Belt remains 1936 (81.1°F), during the Dust Bowl; even the historic 2012 drought (79°F) did not approach it. This finding is corroborated by Butler, Mueller & Huybers (PNAS, 2018), who described the 1981-present Corn Belt growing season as "peculiarly pleasant" weather, i.e., not trending toward the stress conditions a naive global-warming extrapolation would predict.

A separate USDA Economic Research Service chart ("Warming temperatures in U.S. Corn Belt expected to continue into next decade") reports a different but complementary metric: growing degree days (GDD) and extreme degree days (EDD, hours above 86°F) have both risen each decade since 1992 across the 8-state Corn Belt, and are projected to keep rising through the 2030s. GDD/EDD capture heat accumulation and heat-stress exposure differently than a simple monthly mean, so this is not strictly inconsistent with farmdoc's finding of flat monthly averages — it suggests more hours crossing damaging heat thresholds even without a clear shift in the monthly mean, a distinction the question's framing ("temperatures rose") glosses over.

3. An independent check from AskAmerica's own weather data

To add a computed, not just cited, figure, we built a May-September county-day mean temperature series for the same 10-state footprint directly from NOAA GHCND station data (weather.weather_daily_by_county, joined to geo.counties on state FIPS). This is a genuinely different, more localized data source than the NWS series farmdoc used.

Important data-quality caveat: the table's station coverage collapsed in four years within this corpus — 2012, 2014, 2016, and 2017 return fewer than 3% of the expected county-day rows and were excluded rather than treated as real observations (reported to the connector's issue log). Over the 12 remaining years (2010, 2011, 2013, 2015, 2018-2025), a linear regression of the May-Sept mean against year gives a slope of +0.075°C/year (about +1.35°F/decade), r²=0.57. This is a materially faster warming rate than farmdoc's 1980-2023 finding of no significant trend, but the window here is short (12 usable years), gap-ridden, and overlaps the same years the ERS GDD/EDD chart flags as accelerating — consistent with "warming has picked up more recently" rather than contradicting the longer record. We flag this as suggestive, not definitive, given the small n and coverage gaps.

4. Does the yield rise track the temperature change?

No, not in any simple sense. Econometric "crop weather" models of the U.S. average corn yield (e.g., the farmdoc daily model series cited above) find that year-to-year deviations from the trend line are explained by July temperature and precipitation and by planting-date delays — i.e., weather explains the noise around the line, not the line itself. The multi-decade upward slope of the trend line is instead attributed to a non-weather "technology trend" variable: genetics and management. Because Corn Belt monthly growing-season temperatures show essentially no significant multi-decade trend (aside from September) even as yields rose from ~90 to ~186 bu/acre, the two series are effectively decoupled at the trend level. Where climate change is showing up more clearly is in heat-stress exposure (EDD) and in the shift toward wetter springs and drier late summers noted by other climate-impact research (e.g., Columbia's Lamont-Doherty Earth Observatory), which are risks to the trend going forward rather than the explanation for the trend so far.

5. Data-quality notes and scope

  • ag.nass_crop_production (this connector's own USDA NASS QuickStats table) was tested first for a from-scratch, production-weighted Corn Belt yield series, but state-level CORN PRODUCTION/YIELD rows for the 10 states are badly incomplete — e.g., Illinois has zero state-level corn PRODUCTION rows in 2020, 2021, or 2022, and county coverage per year ranges from 1 to 9 of the 10 states with entire years (2000-2001, 2011) missing. This was reported as a data-quality issue rather than used to compute a headline figure; all yield figures in this report are the published USDA NASS national annual estimates instead.
  • weather.weather_daily_by_county station coverage similarly gaps out in 2012, 2014, 2016, and 2017 for this 10-state footprint; those years were excluded from the regression rather than silently averaged in as zeros.
  • "Farm belt" here is operationalized as the 10 states most commonly used in Corn Belt climate/yield studies (IA, IL, IN, MN, MO, NE, ND, OH, SD, WI), matching the farmdoc daily and ERS analyses cited, so the cited figures and the computed check use the same footprint.

What This Report Does Not Answer

  • Crop yields plural (not just corn): Analysis focuses on corn, the dominant and best-documented Corn Belt crop with the clearest long-run climatology literature available in the time allotted; soybean/wheat trends were not separately quantified
