Neither side wins cleanly: since 2010, state income growth was slower where taxes and school spending started high, but that mostly reflects catch-up, not a verdict on either strategy

ACS 1-year median household income 2010 to 2024 (log growth), 50 states; Census F-33, Census of Governments 2012, BEA personal income

Summary

The data do not crown either strategy. Across 50 states, median household income grew about 49 log points on average from 2010 to 2024 (sd 5.9). States with low state-and-local taxes (share of personal income, 2012) grew faster (low third 51.8, high third 45.2 log points; r = -0.39, p = 0.005). But states that spent more per pupil in 2012 also grew slower (r = -0.54). With baseline spending, highways and starting income held fixed, the tax link disappears (coefficient -0.10, p = 0.81), while higher initial school spending relative to income stays negatively associated (-0.57 per point, p = 0.013). Spending growth was positively associated with income growth (+0.26 per log point, p = 0.003), but that is likely reverse causation: richer states spend more. Highways showed nothing. Regulation was not tested. This is a 50-state descriptive comparison with no identification strategy.

What was computed

  • Outcome: 100*ln(median household income 2024 / 2010), ACS 1-year (census.acs1_income). Nominal; inflation is common to all states so cross-state comparisons are unaffected.
  • Taxes: all T-coded state and local tax revenue in 2012 (fiscal.govt_finance_by_unit) over 2012 personal income (econ.state_personal_income SAINC1). Mean 10.1%, range 7.6 to 22.3% (Alaska, oil taxes, is the high end).
  • Highways: E44+F44+G44 spending over personal income, 2012 (mean 1.4%).
  • Education: enrollment-weighted per-pupil current spending (edu.f33_district_finance) as % of 2010 median income, 2012; plus 2010 to 2022 log growth.
  • Method: least-squares fits with heteroskedasticity-robust errors, computed in Python (numpy/scipy) on pasted query results, not with the engine's regression tools; an attempt to run the engine tool timed out on the object store. Treat the coefficients as exploratory.
  • Spending-growth specification: tax -0.82 (p=0.039), highways +0.85 (p=0.52), spending growth +0.26 (p=0.003), baseline income -11.9 (p=0.027), R2 0.31. The tax sign is unstable across specifications.

Units excluded and what it changed

The filter a.state IS NOT NULL excluded the national United States row from the ACS table (null state), and state_fips IS NOT NULL excluded school-finance rows with a null state code (a few multi-county cooperatives); zero-enrollment rows were also dropped. Puerto Rico (in ACS, absent from the finance tables) and the District of Columbia were left out of the regressions, leaving 50 states. Effect of DC: with DC included, tax -0.03 (p=0.96), spending -0.42 (p=0.095), R2 0.19 versus 0.32 without it, so DC weakens but does not reverse the pattern. Also excluding Alaska: spending -0.61 (p=0.012), tax +0.16 (p=0.84), essentially unchanged.

Interpretation and limits

The negative links for high tax and high spending mostly reflect convergence and geography: high-spending, high-tax states (Northeast, Alaska, Wyoming) started rich and grew more slowly, while Sun Belt, Mountain West and Southeast states caught up. The literature is similarly unsettled: CBPP and Gale, Krupkin and Rueben find no stable tax-growth link, while the Mercatus study finds higher taxes correlate with weaker performance. Caveats: 2012 tax totals come from a sample survey; per-pupil spending is not school quality; medians are survey estimates; migration and housing costs are not controlled; nothing here is causal. Regulation is the untested half of the question.

What This Report Does Not Answer

  • Low regulation: No regulation index in the corpus (Mercatus, Fraser) and I did not fetch one; regulation is untested
