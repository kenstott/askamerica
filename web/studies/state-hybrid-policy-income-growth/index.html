Since 2010, low-tax and high-investment states have produced roughly the same household income growth — but low-investment states gained far more people

AskAmerica state panel (2010-2023) plus the published tax-cut and migration literature

Summary

Since 2010, neither strategy shows a clean win on household income growth: across the 50 states, a state's relative investment in education and infrastructure (public capex/opex, pupil-teacher ratios, hospital capacity, broadband/vehicle access — the AskAmerica Social Infrastructure Investment Index, SIII) has essentially no correlation with real median household income growth from 2010 to 2023 (r = -0.13, p = 0.37, not significant). The published research on the opposite strategy — cutting taxes to spur growth — reaches the same kind of null: multi-state studies by the Institute on Taxation and Economic Policy (ITEP) and the Center on Budget and Policy Priorities (CBPP), including the Kansas 2012-2017 tax-cut episode, find no reliable income or growth dividend from low taxes either.

Where the two strategies diverge sharply is population growth. States with lower investment scores (a rough proxy for a leaner state-and-local government footprint, and empirically overlapping with several low/no-income-tax states such as Florida, Texas, Arizona and Nevada) gained population significantly faster 2010-2023 than high-investment states (r = -0.33, p = 0.018 after excluding DC as an outlier). That matches the Tax Foundation's own migration data: the ten most tax-competitive states gained population and taxable income from domestic migration on net, while the ten least competitive states lost both. So the honest answer is: low-tax/low-regulation states have won the population race, not the income-growth race — and high-investment states have not shown an income-growth payoff either, at least not one visible in a simple 13-year cross-state comparison.

What the published literature says

Tax cuts and growth. ITEP's cross-state analysis found states that raised or kept top income tax rates high performed as well or better economically than states with no income tax, and its review "Do Tax Cuts Fuel Growth?" concludes state and local taxes are too small a share of business costs (roughly 2-4%) to reliably drive growth (ITEP, ITEP). Kansas's 2012 top-rate cut of nearly 30% is the most-cited natural experiment: job growth ran at less than half the national average, the state's credit rating was downgraded, and the tax cuts were reversed in 2017 after failing to pay for themselves (CBPP).

Public investment and growth. The general economics literature (surveyed by the Economic Policy Institute and the Congressional Budget Office) finds that public investment in infrastructure, R&D, and human capital raises long-run productivity, but the effect is diffuse, slow-moving, and hard to isolate at the state level over a single decade (EPI, CBO).

Migration. The Tax Foundation's State Tax Competitiveness Index and IRS migration data show the clearest and most consistent state-policy signal: the ten most tax-competitive states gained an average of ~148,000 residents (2021-2023) and the ten least competitive lost an average of ~213,000; Florida (+760k in 2024) and Texas (+787k) led net in-migration (Tax Foundation STCI, Tax Foundation migration analysis).

What the AskAmerica data shows

Design. Using AskAmerica's own tables — census.income_summary (ACS 5-year median household income by state, 2009-2023), census.acs_population, and fiscal.state_social_infrastructure_index (a composite of per-capita state+local capital and operating spending on education/hospitals/parks, pupil-teacher ratios, library space, and internet/vehicle access, 2012-2024) — real median household income growth 2010-2023 was computed for each state (nominal 2010 dollars converted to 2023 dollars via adjust_inflation, BLS CUUR0000SA0 CPI-U) and correlated against each state's average investment score and its population growth over the same window. DC was excluded from the correlations and charts as an extreme, non-state outlier (both its investment index and its income growth are far outside the state distribution).

Result 1 — income growth is a wash. Pearson r between SIII and real MHI growth = -0.13 (p = 0.37, n = 50) — not distinguishable from zero. The states with the highest investment scores did not out-grow real household incomes relative to the lowest-investment states (mean real income growth: top-10 SIII states +10.0%, bottom-10 SIII states +9.9%, over 2010-2023 — statistically indistinguishable).

Result 2 — migration favors low-investment states. Pearson r between SIII and population growth = -0.33 (p = 0.018, n = 50), a real, if modest, effect: on average each 10-point drop in the investment index corresponds to roughly 1.7 percentage points more population growth over the 13-year window. The bottom-10-SIII states (which include Florida, Arizona, Georgia, North Carolina, California — a mixed group by tax policy, but skewed toward faster-growing, lower-relative-spending states) averaged 13.2% population growth versus 7.5% for the top-10-SIII states.

Result 3 — income growth tracks migration, not investment. Income growth correlates positively with population growth itself (r = 0.48, p < 0.001): states that gained the most people also saw the fastest real income growth. This is consistent with either direction of causality (in-migrants raising labor demand and wages, or already-strong labor markets attracting migrants) and cannot be separated with a 51-state, 2-period cross-section — it is descriptive, not causal.

Interpretation and limits

Put together, the data does not support a simple "investment wins" or "low taxes win" story for household incomes specifically. It supports a more precise claim, which matches the outside literature: tax/regulatory competitiveness has been the more reliable predictor of where people moved since 2010, not of how fast household incomes grew wherever they already lived. Kansas's failed tax-cut experiment and the Tax Foundation's migration data are not actually in conflict — Kansas grew jobs slowly without moving its population needle much, while Florida/Texas/Arizona grew population fast without producing a measurably faster household-income trajectory than higher-investment peers.

Key caveats. (1) The SIII index is a proxy for government investment intensity, not a direct tax-rate measure; a state can score low on SIII while still being a moderate-tax state, so this is not literally a taxes-vs-investment test — it is investment-intensity vs. two named outcomes. (2) We attempted to build a genuine state+local tax-burden series from AskAmerica's fiscal.govt_finance_by_unit table (Census Government Finance, item T01 "Total Taxes") but found the resulting per-capita figures implausible even in full Census-of-Governments years (2012/2017/2022) — e.g. Alabama's total computed 2012 state+local tax revenue came out roughly 25x too low, while several small states computed 20-30% "tax burdens" of personal income, well above the ~9-11% U.S. average reported by the Tax Foundation. This looks like incomplete government-unit coverage in the source table; it was reported as a data-quality issue rather than used. (3) 51-state cross-sectional correlations cannot establish causality and are vulnerable to regional confounds (Sun Belt population dynamics, remote-work shifts post-2020, energy-price cycles in states like Alaska and North Dakota) that were not separately controlled for. (4) All figures are nominal-national-CPI-adjusted for time, not cost-of-living-adjusted across states, so cross-state income LEVEL comparisons (not used here) would need a further regional-price-parity step.
