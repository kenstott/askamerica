Since 2010, neither low taxes nor high public investment predicts which states grew household incomes faster

50 states + DC · real median household income growth 2010-2022 (BLS CPI-U adjusted) vs. state tax burden and a public-investment index

Summary

Neither approach shows a real effect on household income growth. Across all 50 states plus DC, a state's 2022 tax burden (Tax Foundation) has essentially no correlation with its real median household income growth from 2010 to 2022 (r = 0.14, p = 0.31), and this corpus's public-investment index (education, hospitals, infrastructure capital/operating spend per capita) does no better (r = 0.16, p = 0.28, and it flips to slightly negative and stays insignificant once DC is excluded).

What tax level DOES predict, weakly, is where people move. Low-tax states drew somewhat more population growth in this analysis (r = -0.17 for tax burden, not significant alone) and this corpus's investment index shows a real, negative, and robust relationship with population growth (r = -0.29 all states, r = -0.34 excluding DC, p = 0.015-0.04): states that invested less per capita in public infrastructure/education gained population faster over the same period. That direction matches the published literature: the Tax Foundation's own analysis of 2022-2023 IRS migration data finds a moderate negative correlation (r = -0.33, R² = 0.11) between a state's top marginal income tax rate and its net migration, explaining roughly 11% of interstate moves — real but modest, not decisive, and other research (CBPP, ITEP) argues taxes play a minor role in migration relative to housing costs, climate, jobs, and family ties. Nobody in this literature, left or right, claims low taxes or high investment reliably raises EXISTING residents' income growth — ITEP's synthesis explicitly argues public investment (education, infrastructure) is 'at least as good, often better' than tax cuts for growth, while Tax Foundation-aligned research emphasizes migration and tax competitiveness rather than income growth for incumbent residents. Our own data is consistent with that split: the tax-vs-investment choice looks like it shapes migration, not income growth, in the 2010-2022 window this corpus and the Tax Foundation can support.

Method and what each lever actually measures

Income outcome: real median household income, 2010 vs. 2022 (census.income_summary, ACS 5-year), deflated to 2022 dollars using the BLS CPI-U annual average (2010→2022 deflator = 1.342, via askengine's adjust_inflation tool). 2022 is the latest full year in this table; the window is not cost-of-living adjusted across states.

Tax lever: this corpus's own state tax-collection table (fiscal.govt_finance_by_unit) is a Census sampled survey whose documented caveat — 'a raw per-state sum is not guaranteed to equal that state's true total... treat any state-level dollar aggregate as needing cross-validation' — was confirmed live: state totals swung by 5-10x between consecutive years for several states (e.g. one state's total tax figure fell from $115B to $9.5B between 2012 and 2017), which is not a real fiscal event. Rather than publish a defective number, tax burden was sourced from the Tax Foundation's independent State and Local Tax Burden study for calendar year 2022 — state and local taxes paid by a state's residents (accounting for tax exported to non-residents) as a share of that state's net product. This and three other sources (Tax Foundation's IRS migration-data analysis, ITEP's 'Do Tax Cuts Fuel Growth?', and an NBER working paper on public education expenditure) were read in full via direct fetch, not search snippets, to cross-check the corpus's own null against the published literature; two additional sources (CBPP, ScienceDirect) returned HTTP 403 and could not be read directly, so their findings above are relayed from search-result summaries only, not verified against the primary document.

Investment lever: fiscal.state_social_infrastructure_index (SIII), this corpus's own composite: 30% capital spend + 20% operating spend on education/hospitals/parks (per capita, all government levels), 15% hospital beds per capita, 15% pupil-teacher ratio (inverted), 10% library square footage, 10% broadband/vehicle access (inverted) — each expressed as a percentile rank among the 50 states + DC for that year, averaged 2012-2022. This is a relative ranking, not a dollar figure, and its hospital-bed component is a current snapshot broadcast across all years (a documented limitation of the underlying view), so treat SIII as directional, not precise.

'Low regulation' is not testable here — no state regulatory-burden index (e.g. right-to-work status, occupational licensing intensity, land-use restrictiveness) is loaded anywhere in this corpus, so the question's 'and regulation' clause could not be independently measured and is not part of the quantitative result above.

Sample composition: every population query in this analysis explicitly excluded Puerto Rico (predicate state NOT IN ('Puerto Rico')) because it is a territory, not a state, and the question asks about states; Puerto Rico's 2010→2022 nominal median household income actually grew from $18,791 to $24,002 (+28%, though off a much lower base and not inflation- or cost-of-living-adjusted here), so including it would not have changed the direction of the null on the 50-states-plus-DC result, but it is excluded from every figure and chart above. The SIII query's state_fips IS NOT NULL filter is a pure data-hygiene predicate (it removes rows the underlying view already leaves blank for non-state entities) and does not itself exclude any state or DC.

Top income-growth states over the window skew toward the Mountain West/Pacific Northwest (Washington +17.6%, Colorado +15.6%, Oregon +15.9%, Utah +14.9%) which have middling-to-low tax burdens (9.7-12.1%) — and DC (+29.5%, an outlier driven by federal/professional-sector wage growth, not tax policy). Bottom-growth states span both high-tax (Connecticut -0.8%, New Jersey +3.7%) and low-tax (Wyoming +0.4%, Alaska -3.3%, Nevada -4.2%) jurisdictions, which is exactly why the correlation washes out — the tax/investment axis does not sort income winners from losers in this period.
