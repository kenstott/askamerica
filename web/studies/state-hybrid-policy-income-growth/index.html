Neither strategy shows a reliable income-growth edge — but low-tax states did gain more people, echoing the migration literature

50 states, 2010-2022. Tax burden and a public-investment index each correlate with income growth at r ≈ 0.10 (not significant); this warehouse's proxies find no significant migration effect either, though the published literature does.

Summary

Across the 50 states from 2010 to 2022, neither strategy shows a statistically reliable link to real median household income growth. States that collected more tax revenue per capita did not grow incomes faster (r = 0.10, p = 0.50), and states that invested more in education/hospital/infrastructure capital and operating spending (per this warehouse's Social Infrastructure Investment Index) did not grow incomes faster either (r = 0.10, p = 0.50). The correlation stays weak (r = 0.07, p = 0.65) even after controlling for the other lever, because tax burden and investment intensity move together (r = 0.36, p = 0.009) — states that tax more also tend to spend/invest more, so the two are not independent policy choices in practice. This state-level result matches the published academic and think-tank literature: state tax levels have at most a minor, inconsistent effect on state economic growth (CBPP; Brookings), while education investment shows a real but longer-run, harder-to-isolate effect on earnings and growth. The clearest and most consistently documented effect of low taxes in the literature is on migration, not growth — low-tax states have attracted more people, especially high earners — though the population-growth proxy used here (net domestic migration data proper was not available in this warehouse) shows only a weak, non-significant negative correlation with tax burden (r = -0.13, p = 0.37).

Method, and one excluded unit: state FIPS 11 (District of Columbia)

Tax-burden proxy: total state+local tax collections per capita, built by summing every T-prefixed item code (property, sales, income, corporate, and other taxes) in the Census Bureau's Government Finance Individual Unit File (fiscal.govt_finance_by_unit), across all government levels (state, county, city, township, special district, school district) within each state, divided by ACS population. Averaged 2017-2022.

Investment proxy: the warehouse's Social Infrastructure Investment Index (fiscal.state_social_infrastructure_index), a composite of per-capita education/hospital/park capital and operating spending, hospital beds, pupil-teacher ratio, library space, and broadband/vehicle access, each percentile-ranked 0-100 across states. Averaged 2017-2022, same window as the tax proxy.

Outcome: median household income by state, ACS 5-year estimates, 2010 vs. 2022, with the 2010 figure inflated to 2022 dollars using BLS CPI-U (deflator 1.342), then expressed as percent real growth. A second outcome, total population growth 2010-2022 (a proxy for net in-migration, since a true net-domestic-migration series was not available at state grain here), was also tested per the corpus's own guidance that a single outcome can produce a misleading null.

Exclusion made, and its effect: every SQL query in this analysis filtered out state_fips = '11', the District of Columbia, from the 50-unit headline results. This is a real exclusion of one unit, not a null-covariate drop, and here is what it changed. DC is not a state — it is a single city government that performs both state and local fiscal functions in one budget — and it is an extreme outlier on both variables: its 2017-2022 average tax collections per capita were $12,596 (higher than any actual state; New York, the highest state, was $10,079), and its 2010-2022 real median household income growth was 29.5%, far above the state maximum of 17.8% (North Dakota). Re-running the identical tax-burden-vs-income-growth correlation with DC added back in (n=51) moves the result from r = 0.10 (p = 0.50, not significant, the headline reported above) to r = 0.39 (p = 0.004, significant). That reversal is produced entirely by this one unit's atypical post-2010 trajectory (concentrated federal employment growth and gentrification in a small, dense jurisdiction, not a generalizable tax-and-growth relationship), so DC is excluded from the headline and its effect is disclosed here rather than silently absorbed into either number.

A data-quality defect found and worked around

The Government Finance table's 2012-2016 records use a different fixed-width layout (34 characters vs. 32 for 2017+) per the table's own documentation. Spot-checking New Hampshire found total state+local tax collections of roughly $150-173 billion per year for 2012-2016 — about 20-25x the $6-8 billion New Hampshire actually collects (confirmed against the 2017-2024 figures in the same table, which fall in a consistent, plausible $6.4-8.3 billion range). This is very likely a parsing/field-offset defect specific to the older 34-character record format, not a real fiscal event. It was reported to the connector's issue log, and the tax-burden analysis here is restricted to 2017-2022, the years that passed a sanity check against independently known figures (e.g., 2022 per-capita collections of $10,249 for California and $5,598 for Texas, both in the range Tax Foundation-style state-local collections figures would predict).

The states with the fastest real income growth over the period (North Dakota +17.8%, Washington +17.6%, Colorado +15.9%) span a wide range of both tax burden and investment scores — North Dakota, with an oil-boom economy, sits at the high end of both tax collections and the investment index, while other fast-growth states sit near the middle of both distributions. The two states with negative real income growth (Nevada -3.3%, New Mexico -4.2%) are similarly not clustered at either policy extreme. This spread — not a clean divide between low-tax winners and high-investment winners — is itself the finding: whichever a state chose, it was not the dominant driver of income growth over this period.

How this compares to the published literature

A parallel web search corroborates the null on taxes and growth: the Center on Budget and Policy Priorities and Brookings both report that academic research finds no statistically significant, consistent effect of state income-tax levels on state economic growth. Education spending research is more consistently positive over longer horizons — investment in education raises individual earnings and correlates with aggregate income growth — but that effect operates through decades of human-capital accumulation, not a single 12-year state panel, which likely explains why the shorter-window SIII proxy here (only 6 years of the 12-year window, since SIII only starts around 2017) shows no measurable correlation.

On migration, however, the literature is more decisive than this warehouse's own population-growth proxy: Tax Foundation and academic migration studies (including a 2024 UC Riverside-covered study) find a real, income-graded relationship between lower state income taxes and higher net domestic in-migration, especially among higher earners. This warehouse could not test net domestic migration directly — no state-grain net-migration series was available — so population growth (which blends migration with births and deaths) was used as an imperfect stand-in, and it shows only a weak, non-significant hint in the same direction (r = -0.13, low tax associated with somewhat faster population growth). The likely reason the two disagree is that the true relationship in the literature is with migration flows specifically, and by income bracket, not total population; a cruder aggregate proxy would be expected to wash much of that signal out. Note also that even the migration literature stops short of the growth literature: Tax Foundation's own reporting acknowledges that several low-tax, high-migration states (e.g., Texas) have ranked in the bottom half of states for per-capita income growth over the past decade, while some higher-tax states (California, Massachusetts, Minnesota) have outperformed — consistent with what this analysis finds.

Bottom line

At the state level over 2010-2022, this data does not support a claim that either strategy — investing more in education/infrastructure or keeping taxes and government smaller — reliably produced faster real household income growth. Both predictors are weak and statistically insignificant among the 50 states (DC/FIPS 11 excluded, and shown separately above), and they are correlated with each other, meaning states rarely choose one lever in isolation. The clearer, better-supported empirical pattern in the wider literature is that low taxes attract migration (particularly of higher earners), not that they raise the income growth of the people already there — and this warehouse's own weaker migration proxy points in a compatible, if not statistically significant, direction. This warehouse has no state-level measure of regulatory burden at all, so the "low regulation" half of the question could not be tested directly and is a genuine coverage gap, not a null result.

What This Report Does Not Answer

  • keeping taxes and regulation low — the other policy lever: Tax burden is addressed via per-capita state+local tax collections. Regulation is NOT addressed: no state-level regulatory-intensity or business-climate index exists in this warehouse, and none was substituted. This is a genuine coverage gap, disclosed rather than proxied by an unrelated column.
