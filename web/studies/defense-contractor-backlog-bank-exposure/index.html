Bank lending to defense contractors is concentrated at the diversified primes, not the single-program-dependent firms — and that inverts the intuitive risk story

No public source ties bank loan balances directly to defense-contractor program concentration; this report reconstructs the exposure from bank regulatory data, prime-contractor credit filings, and industry-financing research

Summary

No public dataset — including the AskAmerica warehouse — reports bank loan balances broken out by borrower industry at defense-contractor granularity, so the question cannot be answered as a precise dollar figure. What the evidence supports is a more specific and somewhat counter-intuitive finding: the defense contractors most exposed to single-program cancellation risk (small and mid-tier subcontractors) are largely absent from bank balance sheets, because their revenue unpredictability is exactly what conventional underwriting screens out. The bank credit that does flow to the sector is concentrated in ~$21.5 billion of committed revolving credit facilities at six large, publicly traded primes (Lockheed Martin, RTX, General Dynamics, Northrop Grumman, Huntington Ingalls, L3Harris) — firms whose revenue is diversified across dozens of programs. The single most concentrated program among them, Lockheed's F-35, is about 27% of that company's revenue; every other prime's largest named program is under 10%. So the segment carrying most of the identifiable bank credit has comparatively low program-cancellation exposure, while the segment with genuine single-program exposure has comparatively little bank credit to lose — a structural mismatch, not an oversight of this analysis.

What data exists, and what does not

Three searches of the AskAmerica catalog (banking call-report data, SEC XBRL filings, USAspending contract data) returned no table that carries 'defense' as a borrower-industry code alongside loan balances — confirmed by the catalog's own unmatched-terms flag on repeated searches. The Federal Reserve/OCC/FDIC's 2025 Shared National Credit (SNC) Review — the one federal data source that aggregates large syndicated bank loan commitments by industry — was fetched and read directly and does not break out an 'Aerospace & Defense' sector even in its newly expanded 21-subsector breakdown; defense manufacturers are folded into 'Equipment, Machinery, Parts Manufacturing Excluding Auto' ($395.6B in commitments, 4.4% special mention, 4.0% classified as of the 2025 review), a category dominated by non-defense manufacturers and therefore not usable as a defense-specific exposure figure.

The OCC's own Comptroller's Handbook on Concentrations of Credit (fetched and read in full) confirms the structural reason no such breakout exists: bank examiners assess concentration risk at the level of an individual bank's own portfolio (name concentration to a single borrower, or sector concentration within one bank's book), not as a national industry-by-industry public statistic — that kind of disclosure is inherently bank-specific and supervisory, not published in aggregate by borrower industry the way FDIC call-report data is for, say, commercial real estate.

SEC 10-K risk-factor language from defense contractors is the closest thing to a structured concentration measure, but it is narrative, not machine-readable at scale, and (per the SEC filings reviewed) reports customer/contract concentration, not bank-loan-specific exposure. The AskAmerica semantic-search tool over SEC filing text returned an infrastructure error mid-session (missing vectorized-chunk parquet files) and could not be used to scan risk-factor language systematically; this is reported as a data-access gap, not folded into the finding.

The structural finding: banks are largely absent from the truly single-program-dependent tier

A 2026 Business Executives for National Security (BENS) analysis (fetched and read in full), part of its 'Credit as Strategy' campaign, states directly that conventional bank lending to small and mid-tier defense suppliers 'has limits,' and that these firms are effectively invisible to private lenders — not from investor indifference, but because DoD's own contracting structure (long procurement cycles, milestone-based payments, unpredictable cash flow) fails to generate the recurring-revenue signal banks underwrite against. Many of these firms carry no public debt at all. This is precisely the population that would carry the sharpest program-cancellation risk (a firm building one component for one missile program, for example) — and it is the population banks have structurally avoided. Notably, the same piece frames the fix as banks investing through CRA-qualifying private equity/venture funds, not direct lending — itself evidence that direct bank credit is not the current channel reaching this tier.

Corroborating evidence, read directly from DefenseScoop's reporting on the program: the Department of Defense stood up its own Office of Strategic Capital (OSC), formally authorized by the FY2024 NDAA, specifically because private credit — including banks — was not reaching these firms. OSC's FY2025 credit program opened applications for up to $984 million in direct loans ($10M-$150M per loan) for 31 covered critical-technology categories, explicitly targeting "potentially overlooked segments of the market" where "private capital alone may require higher interest rates or comparably rapid repayment." A federal agency building its own lending arm to fill a gap is itself evidence the gap exists; it would not be necessary if banks already carried material single-program-contractor risk on their books. U.S. Bank's own aerospace/defense banking page (fetched directly) confirms the commercial-bank product set for this sector is weighted toward working capital, payments, and capital-markets/trust services for established primes and mid-size firms — not concentrated program-specific term lending to small single-program subcontractors.

Summed, these six facilities total roughly $21.5 billion in committed bank capacity, essentially all of it investment-grade, syndicated across many banks (no single lender holds a concentrated position), and drawn close to zero in normal conditions — these are liquidity backstops, not program financing. Per the OCC Comptroller's Handbook on Concentrations of Credit, this is exactly the profile examiners treat as low-risk: diversified, high-grade, syndicated exposure rather than concentrated name or sector risk. Industry practice separately treats a single program under 30% of revenue as a 'clean' concentration profile for valuation/credit purposes; Lockheed's F-35 exposure, the single largest publicly disclosed figure among the primes, sits just under that threshold. No other prime's disclosed single-program share approaches it.

What this means for cancellation/delay exposure

Given the structure above, a Pentagon program cancellation or major delay would transmit into bank credit risk through two channels, neither of which resembles a concentrated single-borrower loss: (1) a large prime's revolver utilization rising if a program cancellation stressed its liquidity broadly — but because these firms run diversified program portfolios, one cancellation is a revenue-mix shock, not typically a going-concern event, and the revolvers exist precisely as a shock absorber; and (2) knock-on stress to the unbanked or thinly-banked small-supplier tier, which would show up as business failures and supply-chain disruption rather than bank loan losses, because banks were not carrying that risk on their books in the first place. The genuinely concentrated risk in the system — a subcontractor whose whole business depends on one missile or one shipbuilding program — is real, but it is a risk to that firm's survival and to DoD's supply chain, not, on the evidence gathered here, a risk concentrated on bank balance sheets.

What This Report Does Not Answer

  • Quantify total bank lending concentrated in program-dependent defense contractors: No public dataset (including AskAmerica) reports bank loan balances by defense-contractor identity or program concentration -- confirmed both by catalog search and by the OCC Comptroller's Handbook, which explains concentration-of-credit data is bank-specific/supervisory, not a published national industry statistic. The report substitutes the best available proxy (disclosed committed revolving credit facilities at major primes, ~$21.5B) and explains why the true single-program-dependent tier is largely unbanked rather than estimating a figure that cannot be sourced.
