No public dataset quantifies bank lending concentrated in single-program-dependent defense contractors — the exposure is real but structurally invisible

AskAmerica corpus + SEC filings + industry/policy research, checked 2026-09-26

Summary

This cannot be answered with a number, and that itself is the finding. No public dataset — not the AskAmerica corpus, not FDIC call reports, not SEC filings, not GAO or CRS research — reports how much bank lending is concentrated in defense contractors whose revenue depends on a small number of Pentagon programs, or how exposed that lending is to a program's cancellation. The two halves of the question (bank loan books, and borrower program-revenue concentration) are disclosed by different actors at different grains that cannot be joined: banks disclose loan concentration by broad category (commercial real estate, commercial & industrial) but never by borrower identity or industry; defense contractors disclose program-revenue concentration in their own 10-Ks but never who lends to them or how much. What follows is the structural reasoning for where this exposure actually sits, using the handful of concrete disclosures that exist.

Why the large primes are not where bank credit risk concentrates

The top five Pentagon contractors — Lockheed Martin, RTX (formerly Raytheon), Boeing, General Dynamics, and Northrop Grumman — received $771 billion of the $2.4 trillion the Pentagon obligated to contracts from 2020-2024, per the Quincy Institute's July 2025 analysis, and revenue concentration in a small number of major weapons programs is real and disclosed (the F-35, for example, has represented roughly a quarter of Lockheed's total sales in recent years). But these firms are investment-grade and finance themselves mainly through public bonds and commercial paper, not bank term loans. Their bank facilities are large, multi-year revolving credit lines syndicated across dozens of banks and left undrawn — Lockheed Martin's is $3.0 billion, with $0 drawn as of its last reported balance sheet date. A syndicated, undrawn facility spreads any single bank's exposure to the prime across the whole syndicate and only activates in a liquidity crisis, not from an individual program's cancellation. U.S. Bank's own description of its aerospace-and-defense practice (financing, capital markets, corporate trust, payments) confirms the relationship with primes and subcontractors runs mostly through capital-markets and treasury services rather than concentrated balance-sheet lending. So even where single-program revenue concentration is largest and best documented, it does not translate into a quantifiable single-bank credit exposure — the prime's own balance sheet and the bond market absorb that risk first.

Where bank lending concentration to program-dependent contractors actually shows up

The disclosed concentration exists one tier down, among the small and mid-size subcontractors and government-services firms that make up the bulk of the defense industrial base, and it is carried mostly by niche community banks, not the large national banks that serve the primes. FVCBankcorp (Reston, VA) is the clearest example found in this research: as of December 31, 2025 it reported $350.4 million in commitments to government-contractor borrowers — 45% of its commercial and industrial loan segment — specifically defined as borrowers headquartered in the D.C. metro area that recognize over 50% of total revenue from federal government agencies. That bank's own 10-K names "shutdowns of the U.S. government and potential reductions in spending" as a risk factor but does not quantify the dollar exposure at risk or attribute it to any specific program. This pattern — a regional bank building a specialized government-contractor lending book, concentrated geographically around Washington D.C., Huntsville, or similar defense-cluster metros — is the closest real-world analog to the concentration the question asks about. It is disclosed by individual banks voluntarily and inconsistently, so there is no aggregated, sector-wide figure, and "government contractor" as these banks define it is broader than defense specifically (it includes IT and professional-services firms serving any federal agency, not only Pentagon-program-dependent ones).

Why program-cancellation exposure is structurally under-measured

Research from Business Executives for National Security (BENS) on defense-industrial-base financing found that traditional bank underwriting is built around demonstrated cash flow and firm contract backlog, not around a company's strategic importance or program affiliation. A contract structured with a large notional value but only a small exercised "base" period — a common Pentagon contracting pattern — is treated by lenders as if only the base period's value exists, and companies with negative EBITDA (common among newer, technology-focused defense suppliers) fall outside the "bankable" universe entirely, per BENS. The practical implication: much of the defense supply chain most exposed to single-program cancellation or delay risk is not conventionally bank-financed at all — it relies instead on private credit, venture debt, or equity, which carry even less public disclosure than bank lending does. Separately, the historic pattern of concentrated, often sole-source Pentagon contracting documented by the Center for Public Integrity (top 10 contractors won the large majority of their dollars outside full-and-open competition; the top 50 contractors alone collected more than half of all Defense Department procurement dollars) shows the contract-award side of this concentration is well studied; the lender-exposure side is not. GAO's 2024 review of DOD contract financing and the Congressional Research Service's defense-industrial-base report both describe financing fragility in the subcontractor tier but neither publishes a dollar figure for bank exposure or program-cancellation risk.

What would be needed to actually answer this

Answering the question as asked would require either (1) bank regulatory data disclosing loan concentration by borrower industry and counterparty identity — which does not exist publicly at any granularity finer than broad loan-category totals — or (2) a proprietary or supervisory-only dataset joining individual banks' loan books to individual defense contractors' program-revenue mix. Absent either, any number offered for "how much bank lending is concentrated" or "how exposed" would be invented, not sourced, and none is offered here. What can be said with confidence is directional: exposure concentrated in single-program-dependent revenue sits mainly at large, bond-financed primes whose bank exposure is diluted by syndication, while genuinely concentrated single-lender exposure sits at smaller subcontractors and government-services firms served by niche regional banks — exactly the tier where contract-cancellation risk would bite hardest and where public disclosure is thinnest.

What This Report Does Not Answer

  • Dollar amount or share of total bank lending concentrated in defense contractors dependent on a small number of Pentagon programs: No source — public or in this corpus — reports bank lending broken out by borrower industry AND by the borrower's own program-revenue concentration. FDIC call reports (banking.financials, banking.summary) report loan concentration only by broad category (CRE, C&I, consumer); they carry no counterparty/borrower-identity field, so a bank's loan book cannot be joined to which of its C&I borrowers are defense contractors, let alone to those contractors' program mix. search_catalog confirmed 'defense' and 'exposure' as concepts are unmatched anywhere in the corpus. Web research (Business Executives for National Security; GAO/CRS defense-industrial-base literature) confirms no third party publishes this figure either — this is a genuine transparency gap, not a shortfall in search effort.
  • Degree of exposure of that lending to a program's cancellation or delay: No bank publicly attributes loan-loss reserves or credit risk to a specific Pentagon program's cancellation risk; bank 10-Ks disclose only generic risk language ('government shutdowns,' 'reductions in federal spending') without naming programs or dollar amounts. What is offered instead is the structural reasoning for where such exposure would concentrate if it existed, using the one concrete disclosed example found (FVCBankcorp) plus industry/policy research on defense-sector bank underwriting (BENS) and the concentration of Pentagon prime-contract dollars itself (Quincy Institute, Center for Public Integrity/POGO).
