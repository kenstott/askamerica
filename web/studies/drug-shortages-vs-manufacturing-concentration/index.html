Q68 — Drug shortages vs. manufacturing concentration (hospital supply-chain risk)

Published report (self-contained, dashboard inlined): http://127.0.0.1:59215/a/4f52147102eaf368d3e45c91b2f557f2.html (local-only link — not fetchable outside this run; dashboard image also saved alongside this file as dashboard.png)

Summary

Active US drug shortages hit an all-time high of 323 in Q1 2024 — the highest since ASHP and the University of Utah began tracking in 2001 — up from a then-record 295 at end-2022. Shortages have since eased to 223 but are rising again for a second straight quarter. Separately, the FDA's own count of new shortages fell sharply (49 in 2022, 55 in 2023, 15 in 2024), meaning the country has gotten better at preventing new shortages but worse at resolving the ones already underway (average duration rose from about 2 years in 2020 to over 3 years in 2023).

On manufacturing concentration: of 5,766 distinct generic drug names in the FDA NDC directory, 50.7% (2,923) have exactly one listed manufacturer, and 72.7% have 3 or fewer — the FDA/analyst threshold for "inadequate competition." Injectable dosage forms, which account for 53.7% of new shortage events per HHS/ASPE (vs 38.2% oral), average significantly fewer manufacturers (2.09) than non-injectable forms (3.48; Welch t-test p=5.0×10⁻⁴⁶, n=8,373). A 2024 NEJM study of 125 sterile injectables that hit the FDA shortage list in 2021–2024 found 81 (65%) had 3 or fewer US manufacturers. Concentration is a real, quantifiable risk factor for injectables specifically, not just a narrative claim.

1. Shortage trend

Two independent trackers, both national-grain, disagree slightly in scale but agree in direction:

  • ASHP / University of Utah Drug Information Service (tracking since 2001): active shortages rose from a 5-year record of 295 (end of 2022, +30% YoY) to an all-time high of 323 in Q1 2024, fell to 277 by Q2 2024, continued down, then began rising again to 223 most recently — trending up for the second quarter in a row.
  • FDA's own Report to Congress counts NEW shortages started per year: 49 in 2022, 55 in 2023, only 15 in 2024 — FDA attributes the drop to 283 shortages it says it prevented in 2024 through early engagement with manufacturers.
  • ASPE data brief (2018–2023): 710 unique shortage events began in that window, covering 258 unique active ingredients / 1,961 NDC-level products; average 2.9 drugs per event. Average shortage duration rose from ~2 years (2020) to over 3 years (2023) — shortages are lasting longer even as new starts slowed.

Net read: the shortage stock (how many active at once) is near its historic worst and duration is worsening, even though new starts have slowed — two different senses of "worse," both reported so the reader can weight them.

2. Manufacturing concentration — computed from AskAmerica

Query: distinct FDA-listed manufacturers (labeler_name) per generic drug name, from health.fda_ndc_products, restricted to finished, human prescription drug products (n=56,176 NDC listings → 5,766 distinct generic names).

1 (sole source) 2,923 50.7%
2–3 1,270 22.0%

6+ 1,084 18.8%

72.7% of generic drug names have 3 or fewer listed manufacturers. This is broadly consistent with, though higher than, FDA's own 2020 finding that ~40% of generic drug markets have a single manufacturer — expected, since this is a per-NDC-labeler count (can include repackagers/distributors of the same underlying product), a looser proxy than a true manufacturing-site/API-source count.

Isolating injectable dosage forms sharpens the picture: 85.7% of injectable drug×form combinations have ≤3 manufacturers vs. 74.8% for non-injectable, and average manufacturer count is 2.09 for injectables vs. 3.48 for non-injectable (Welch t-test, t=14.33, p=5.0×10⁻⁴⁶, n=8,373 combinations).

3. Relating shortage and concentration

Cross-check of the current FDA shortage list (fetched 2026-08-25) against corpus manufacturer counts:

Bendamustine HCl injection 1–3 (across listing variants)
Fulvestrant injection 2–10

Ritonavir (oral) 7

Injectable-form shortage drugs cluster at the low end; the two oral shortage drugs sampled do not — consistent with the statistically significant injectable-vs-non-injectable gap, and with ASPE's finding that injectables are 53.7% of new shortage events despite being a minority of the supply. The relationship is real but form-specific: fewer manufacturers is a strong risk marker within sterile injectables, not a universal predictor across the whole drug supply.

External corroboration: NEJM (2024) found 81/125 (65%) sterile injectables on the 2021–2024 shortage list had ≤3 US manufacturers, and for two-thirds, a version was approved in a peer market (Australia/Canada/France/Germany/NZ/UK) but not the US — evidence of a US-specific market-structure problem, not a global shortfall.

Confidence

Medium. Manufacturer-concentration figures (Section 2) are computed directly from FDA NDC data, n=5,766 — high confidence. Shortage-trend figures (Section 1) are relayed from three trackers that disagree on exact scale (295/309/320/323 depending on source/quarter) — medium confidence on precise levels, high on direction. Section 3's direct join uses a small hand-picked sample (n=5 shortage drugs) because this corpus has no FDA Drug Shortages Database table (confirmed via search_catalog/list_tables(health); gap logged via report_issue). The injectable-vs-non-injectable test (n=8,373) is the systematic substitute and is statistically strong.
