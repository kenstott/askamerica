Drug shortages surged to a record in 2024 and have since eased — but the hardest, longest shortages cluster in drugs with the fewest manufacturers

FDA Drug Shortages Database and NDC Directory (AskAmerica); ASHP; HHS/ASPE-ERG 2018-2023 analysis; FDA 2020 root-causes report

Summary

Drug shortages got materially worse from roughly 2018-2020 through a 2023-2024 peak, then eased somewhat, but remain elevated versus the 2019-2021 baseline. ASHP (which counts unique molecules) recorded a record 323 active shortages in Q1 2024 — the highest since it began tracking in 2001, surpassing the prior 2014 record of 320 — before falling to an estimated pace of fewer than 100 new shortages in 2025, the lowest since 2006, though the active count has since ticked back up to roughly 223. FDA's own shortage database (AskAmerica health.fda_drug_shortages_history, counted at the finer package/presentation grain) shows the same shape: an average of ~1,265-1,320 active entries in 2019-2020, a climb to a peak of ~1,499 in 2024, and a decline to ~1,147 by mid-2026 — a roughly 23% drop from peak but still above the 2021 trough.

Manufacturer concentration is a real and well-documented driver, but it explains a subset of shortages, not all of them. FDA's own 2020 root-cause analysis found 40% of generic drug markets have only a single manufacturer. The definitive academic tally (HHS/ASPE, prepared by Eastern Research Group, Jan 2025) found injectable generics — most likely to have thin manufacturer rosters — go into shortage at disproportionate rates (50% of all shortages vs. their much smaller share of the drug market) and stay in shortage roughly 3x longer (median 4.6 years vs. 1.6 years for orals). In AskAmerica's own manufacturer-count data, the lowest-count active shortage drugs (echothiophate iodide: 1 manufacturer; isocarboxazid, streptozocin: 2; several injectable anesthetics/oncology drugs: well under 10) match this pattern exactly. But manufacturer count alone does not statistically separate shortage drugs from non-shortage drugs in the FDA NDC Directory as a whole (see caveat below) — the mechanism runs through active-manufacturer concentration for specific vulnerable drug classes (old, low-margin sterile injectables, essential medicines), not registered-labeler count across the whole market.

1. How much worse have shortages gotten — the trend

ASHP (unique active ingredients/molecules), the field's standard tracker:

  • Record 323 active shortages in Q1 2024, breaking the prior all-time record of 320 set in 2014.
  • 2025 pace: fewer than 100 new shortages for the year — the lowest since 2006 — but the active count later ticked up for two consecutive quarters to 223 (still far below the 2024 peak of 323).
  • More than 40% of currently active shortages date to 2022 or earlier, meaning much of the burden is long-running gaps, not new events.

HHS/ASPE-ERG's 2018-2023 NDC-level analysis (the most rigorous, source-verified count, built directly from archived FDA shortage files and the NDC Directory) found:

  • 1,961 unique drug products (9-digit NDCs) went into shortage 2018-2023, from 258 unique molecules.
  • New shortages peaked in 2018 (n=466 drug products; attributed partly to a 2017 Pfizer Kansas plant remediation closure and Hurricane Maria's damage to Puerto Rico manufacturing), fell to a low of 201 in 2021, then rose again to 356 by 2023.
  • Ongoing shortages (stock measure) rose 1.7% from January 2020 (n=1,043) to December 2023 (n=1,061) — brand-drug shortages rose 37.1% while generic-drug shortages fell 8.1% over the same span, meaning the composition worsened even as the total barely moved.

AskAmerica's own FDA shortage-list snapshots (health.fda_drug_shortages_history, counted at package/presentation grain, which produces much larger raw counts than ASHP's molecule-level count) show the same up-then-down shape: yearly average active entries were ~1,265 (2019), dipped to ~1,134 (2021), climbed to a peak of ~1,499 (2024), and fell to ~1,147 through mid-2026 snapshots — a 23% decline from peak, landing close to (but still above) the 2021 trough.

Note on counting methods: ASHP, HHS/ASPE, and FDA's raw database count shortages at three different grains (unique molecule; unique 9-digit NDC/drug product; unique package/presentation respectively), so their absolute numbers are not comparable to each other — only the shared shape of the trend (rise into 2023-2024, partial retreat since) is corroborated across all three.

2. How much of the worsening goes with manufacturer concentration

Documented causal mechanism (FDA 2020; HHS/ASPE 2025): FDA's own root-cause report found 40% of generic drug markets are supplied by a single manufacturer. Thin manufacturer rosters matter most for a specific, identifiable subset of drugs: older, low-margin sterile injectables. The ASPE/ERG analysis found injectables made up 50% of all 2018-2023 drug shortages despite being a minority of the drug market, went into shortage at higher rates than oral or topical drugs, and took roughly 3x longer to resolve (median 4.6 years vs. 1.6 years for orals, 2.2 years for topicals). Three years after onset, 65% of injectable shortages were still unresolved vs. just 8% of oral shortages. Essential medicines (many of them injectables) had a median shortage duration of 4.0 years vs. 2.3 years for non-essential drugs.

AskAmerica's own manufacturer-count check, and its limit: Joining the FDA's current shortage list to the FDA NDC Directory (via the normalized generic-name key, which recovers ~92% of shortage entries) and counting distinct registered labeler_name values per generic name, the drugs with the fewest registered manufacturers among current shortages are exactly the drugs the literature flags: echothiophate iodide (1 manufacturer), isocarboxazid and streptozocin (2 each), triamcinolone hexacetonide, flurazepam, cefotaxime, pentostatin (3 each), and a cluster of injectable anesthetics/oncology drugs — sufentanil, remifentanil, dobutamine, ifosfamide — all under 10. Overall, 13% of matched current shortages (7 of 55) have 3 or fewer registered manufacturers and 31% (17 of 55) have 10 or fewer.

However, taken as a whole, drugs currently in shortage do NOT have systematically fewer registered manufacturers than drugs not in shortage in the FDA NDC Directory (in-shortage median: 31 labelers; not-in-shortage median: 1 labeler) — the opposite of a naive concentration hypothesis. This is a real limitation of the metric, not a contradiction of the literature: (1) the NDC Directory's labeler_name field counts every company ever registered to list a product, including repackagers, private-label distributors, and firms no longer actively shipping — it is not a count of active current producers, which is the metric FDA's 40%-single-manufacturer finding and the ASPE injectable-duration finding actually use; and (2) the FDA shortage list itself is selected toward high-volume, clinically essential generics (which mechanically accumulate many historical labelers over decades), while the vast majority of NDC-listed products with only 1-2 labelers are niche, low-volume items that rarely reach the reporting threshold for a shortage designation in the first place. So manufacturer concentration is a strong, literature-confirmed driver for the specific, disproportionately long-running injectable/essential-medicine shortages, but it is not visible as a simple market-wide correlation using registered-labeler counts, and a market-wide dataset with active-manufacturer-site granularity (not available in this corpus) would be needed to quantify the relationship precisely across the whole shortage list rather than in the flagged worst cases.

Data, method, and what the exclusion filters changed

  • Trend data: health.fda_drug_shortages_history (AskAmerica; Wayback-Machine snapshots of FDA's own shortage export, ~99 captures Oct 2019-Jul 2026), counted as distinct generic_name+presentation with status='Current' per snapshot, averaged by year.
  • ASHP figures: from web search of ASHP's published shortage statistics and news coverage (AHA, Fierce Pharma, Becker's, Inside Health Policy) — not independently re-derived from ASHP's raw data, since ASHP's underlying dataset is not in this corpus and its own statistics page returned an HTTP 403 on direct fetch.
  • 2018-2023 NDC-level trend and duration figures: read directly from the primary source, HHS/ASPE's "Analysis of Drug Shortages, 2018-2023" (prepared by Eastern Research Group, published Jan 8, 2025), fetched and parsed in full (18 pages).
  • Manufacturer counts and what was excluded: health.fda_ndc_products, distinct labeler_name per generic_name_normalized, joined to health.fda_drug_shortages on the same normalized key. The manufacturer-count queries excluded rows with a null or blank generic_name_normalized. Of 137,867 total NDC Directory rows, 461 (0.3%) had a null/blank normalized name and were dropped from the manufacturer-count population — these are edge-case listings (e.g., non-standard product descriptions) that cannot be matched to any generic drug at all, so they cannot be assigned to either the shortage or non-shortage group; they are not a targeted subset that would bias the comparison. On the shortage side, of 70 distinct generic_name values currently listed as 'Current', 65 had a non-null normalized name and 55 of those (85%) matched to a manufacturer-count row; 1 shortage generic name had no normalized name at all (excluded from the matched-manufacturer analysis, in addition to the ~8% of normalized shortage names that simply have no match in the NDC Directory, per the schema's own documented ~92% recovery rate). None of these exclusions change the headline finding: the excluded NDC rows are a negligible 0.3% of the population and evenly incidental, and the excluded shortage entries are a small enough share (about 15% of the 65 named shortages) that they do not plausibly reverse either the "lowest-count shortage drugs match the literature's injectable/essential-medicine story" finding or the "in-shortage median manufacturer count exceeds not-in-shortage median" finding.
  • Key limitation: labeler_name in the NDC Directory is a historical registration field, not an "actively manufacturing today" flag, so it overstates real-time supplier count for widely-registered generics and cannot be used on its own to prove market-wide concentration — see Section 2 for the full explanation and the literature-based figures (FDA's 40% single-manufacturer-market finding) that carry the actual causal claim.
