Drug shortages are near record levels again, and the persistence problem tracks straight back to how few manufacturers make each drug

Analysis of ASHP/University of Utah tracking data, FDA's shortage database, and HHS/ASPE's 2018-2023 shortage-duration study

Summary

US drug shortages are worse than a decade ago and, after a brief improvement, are getting worse again: active shortages hit an all-time record of 323 in Q1 2024 (versus a prior record of 320 in 2014), fell to 216 by year-end 2025, and have now risen for three straight quarters to 227 as of Q2 2026. The persistence of shortages — not just how many start — is closely tied to manufacturer concentration: about 40% of generic drug markets have only a single manufacturer (FDA, 2020), and sole-source products account for roughly 48% of new 2026 shortages. The clearest quantified link is by dosage form: injectable drugs, the segment FDA and HHS repeatedly identify as having the fewest manufacturers and least spare capacity, take a median of 4.6 years to resolve versus 1.6 years for oral drugs — a gap of roughly 3x — and 64.6% of injectable shortages are still unresolved after three years versus 8.1% of oral shortages, an 8x gap. In short: fewer suppliers do not mainly change how often a shortage starts, but they change dramatically how long it lasts once it does.

How much worse have shortages gotten

The US has tracked drug shortages continuously since 2001 through the University of Utah Drug Information Service, whose data ASHP publishes. The historical arc: shortages more than tripled between 2005 and 2010, hit a first record of 320 active shortages in 2014, and after a period of decline (new shortages fell to a low of 49 for calendar year 2022, per FDA's Report to Congress), surged again — culminating in an all-time high of 323 active shortages in Q1 2024, driven partly by IV-fluid disruptions from Hurricane Helene and continuing sterile-injectable and CNS-drug shortages. That peak eased to 271 by December 2024 and 216 by December 2025 (with only 89 new shortages reported in 2025 — the fewest since 2006). But the improvement did not hold: active shortages rose to 223 in Q1 2026 and 227 in Q2 2026, the third consecutive quarterly increase. Just under half of 2026's active shortages (48%) began in 2022 or earlier, underscoring that the current total is less a story of new failures than of a backlog that resolves very slowly.

An HHS/ASPE-commissioned analysis of FDA's shortage archives (2018-2023) confirms the backlog dynamic directly: the total number of drug products with an ongoing shortage barely moved from 2020 to 2023 (+1.7%) even though generic-drug shortages actually declined over that period (-8.1%), because brand-drug shortages rose sharply (+37.1%) and because shortages, once started, resolve slowly — the median time to resolution across all drugs was 2.55 years.

How much of that goes with manufacturer concentration

FDA's own root-cause analyses point squarely at concentration. Its 2019/2020 Drug Shortages Task Force report found that 40% of generic drug markets have only a single manufacturer supplying them, and that markets with few suppliers are far more exposed: any one facility's disruption — a quality failure, a natural disaster, a plant closure — can eliminate most or all of the domestic supply with no immediate substitute. That mechanism shows up directly in 2026 data: 48% of all new shortages this year involve sole-source products.

The sharpest, most directly quantified version of this relationship is by dosage form. Sterile injectable drugs — repeatedly identified by FDA and HHS as the segment with the fewest manufacturers, the oldest facilities, the thinnest margins, and essentially no spare production capacity (US finished-dose facilities typically run above 80% capacity) — behave completely differently from orals once they enter shortage:

  • Injectables make up 50% of all drug shortages despite being a minority of drugs on the market.
  • Median time to resolve: 4.6 years for injectables vs. 1.6 years for oral drugs and 2.2 years for topicals — injectables take about 2.9x as long as orals.
  • Three years after a shortage starts, 64.6% of injectable shortages are still active, versus 8.1% of oral shortages and 26.1% of topicals — roughly an 8x gap between injectables and orals.
  • Nearly half of injectable drugs that went into shortage from 2018-2023 were designated essential medicines, and essential medicines overall take about 4.0 years to resolve versus 2.3 years for non-essential drugs — concentration and clinical necessity compound each other.

The mechanism argued by FDA, GAO, and HHS is consistent across sources: generic-drug price competition erodes margins to the point that manufacturers exit low-profit, hard-to-make products (especially older sterile injectables); fewer suppliers remain; and when one of those remaining suppliers has a quality failure or capacity problem, there is no fast substitute, because entering a generic-injectable market or requalifying an alternate manufacturing line takes years of regulatory review. This is a story about resolution speed, not primarily about new-shortage onset — the correlation is between number-of-suppliers and how long a shortage lasts, not how often one starts.

Data-quality note on this connector

AskAmerica's health.fda_drug_shortages table was checked directly. It has no true year-coverage window (it stores MM/DD/YYYY strings, not a structural year column) and, more importantly, its year-by-year row counts are extremely uneven (e.g., 355 episodes tagged 2023 but only 11 tagged 2024, then 299 in 2026) — a pattern consistent with FDA's live shortage feed being snapshotted at irregular intervals rather than an archived full history, which matches HHS/ASPE's own methodological note that FDA removes resolved shortages from its public feed six months after resolution and that reconstructing a full history requires pulling Internet Archive snapshots (which is exactly what the ASPE-commissioned study did, and what this report relies on instead). A join attempt from fda_drug_shortages.generic_name to fda_ndc_products.generic_name, intended to estimate manufacturer counts directly from the connector, matched only 9 of 245 distinct shortage-drug names (3.7%) — the two tables' name strings are not directly comparable at scale — so that manufacturer-count computation was abandoned in favor of the published FDA/HHS figures cited above, which were built by the same kind of NDC-level linkage but done carefully by the primary-source authors.

  11. AskAmerica health.fda_drug_shortages x health.fda_ndc_products name-join test (data-quality check)
    Show SQL
    WITH shortage_drugs AS (SELECT DISTINCT LOWER(TRIM(generic_name)) AS generic_name FROM health.fda_drug_shortages WHERE generic_name IS NOT NULL), mfg_counts AS (SELECT LOWER(TRIM(generic_name)) AS generic_name, COUNT(DISTINCT labeler_name) AS n_labelers FROM health.fda_ndc_products WHERE generic_name IS NOT NULL GROUP BY LOWER(TRIM(generic_name))) SELECT CASE WHEN n_labelers <= 1 THEN '1 (sole source)' WHEN n_labelers = 2 THEN '2' WHEN n_labelers BETWEEN 3 AND 5 THEN '3-5' ELSE '6+' END AS labeler_bucket, COUNT(*) AS n_shortage_drugs FROM shortage_drugs sd JOIN mfg_counts mc ON sd.generic_name = mc.generic_name GROUP BY 1 ORDER BY 1

All duration/resolution-rate figures are Kaplan-Meier estimates from FDA shortages initiated 2018-2023 (HHS/ASPE-ERG); active-shortage counts are point-in-time snapshots from ASHP/University of Utah and FDA releases, not a continuous monthly series. The AskAmerica connector's own FDA shortage table could not support a reliable manufacturer-count computation (see data-quality note) and was used only to test that limitation, not to produce the headline figures.
