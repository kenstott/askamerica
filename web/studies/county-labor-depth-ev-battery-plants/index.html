Existing skilled manufacturing workforce is concentrated in about two dozen mid-size Midwest/Southeast counties — separate from the low-cost-land counties that win most site-selection contests

BLS QCEW 2023 county employment (NAICS 335 electrical equipment, 3363 motor-vehicle parts, 333 machinery) as a battery-relevant manufacturing proxy, location-quotient method

Summary

Using BLS QCEW 2023 county employment in the three NAICS industries most transferable to battery-cell manufacturing — electrical equipment/component manufacturing (335, which includes storage-battery manufacturing 33591), motor-vehicle-parts manufacturing (3363), and industrial machinery (333) — a location-quotient (LQ) analysis identifies roughly two dozen mid-size counties, concentrated in Michigan, Ohio, Wisconsin, Kentucky, Indiana and the Carolinas/Georgia auto corridor, where this workforce is 6-13x more concentrated than the national average. These are places with decades of automotive-supplier and electrical-equipment employment, not places chosen because land was cheap. By contrast, several real, publicly announced battery megasites — most starkly Ford's BlueOval City in Haywood County, TN — sit in counties with a battery-relevant LQ of roughly 0.3 (well below the national average) and cheap land (median home value ~$120K), meaning the workforce is being built from scratch rather than drawn from an existing base.

Method, exclusions, and what the proxy does and does not capture

No table in this corpus directly measures 'skilled manufacturing workforce for battery production' — there is no occupational series for battery technicians, cell assemblers, or EV-supply-chain electricians at county grain (search_catalog returned no match for 'skills', 'electricians', 'machinists' as county-level occupation counts). The proxy used here is BLS QCEW county employment (2023, private ownership) in three NAICS codes whose labor pools are the ones battery plants actually hire from: 335 (electrical equipment/appliance/component mfg, which includes battery mfg itself, NAICS 33591), 3363 (motor-vehicle parts, the auto-supplier trades — machine operators, industrial electricians, quality techs — that GM/Ford/LG/Panasonic/SK plants recruit from directly), and 333 (industrial/general machinery, the tool-and-die/mechatronics trades). A location quotient (LQ) of 5 means that industry group is 5x as concentrated in that county's private workforce as the U.S. average.

Exclusion disclosed — predicates 'mfg_emp IS NOT NULL' and 'total_emp IS NOT NULL': BLS QCEW suppresses (disclosure_code='N') employment figures for any county-industry cell dominated by one or two employers, replacing the true count with NULL, and a NULL cannot be ranked or divided. Applying those two filters removes 395 of 3,274 county-level QCEW area codes (12.1%) from the ranking. Named by state, the excluded units are concentrated in exactly the sparsely populated rural counties this question is about: 55 in Texas (e.g. Loving, King, Terrell, Kent, Stonewall, McMullen, Culberson), 29 in Nebraska (Arthur, Loup, Blaine, Grant, McPherson), 25 in Georgia (Glascock, Quitman, Echols, Talbot, Baker), 25 in Kansas (Greeley, Wallace, Stanton, Kearny), 23 in South Dakota (Oglala Lakota, Jones, Harding, Ziebach), 20 in North Dakota (Slope, Golden Valley, Billings), 18 in Montana, 18 in Kentucky (Owsley, Leslie, Wolfe, Elliott — rural Appalachian counties), 15 in Colorado, and 11 in Alaska (Denali, Yakutat, Kusilvak). With the filter applied, 2,879 counties remain, representing 131,286,713 total private jobs and 12,840,453 manufacturing jobs — effectively the entire national manufacturing workforce, since suppression removes individual small cells, not whole regional economies; without the filter the true national total is at most negligibly higher, because these are overwhelmingly the lowest-population counties in the country. But the composition, not the total, is what matters for this question: because suppression is triggered by having so few employers that one dominant plant would be individually identifiable, a genuinely rural 'cheap land, one hidden factory' county is exactly the profile most likely to be excluded here. Its true battery-relevant LQ is unknown from this table, not confirmed low, and that is a real limitation of using QCEW to answer this specific question.

The proxy is also imperfect on its own terms: a county can have a high LQ from legacy auto-parts plants whose workers may or may not transfer cleanly to lithium-cell chemistry work, and a county's true workforce also depends on nearby community-college/apprenticeship pipelines (e.g. Georgia's technical college system, cited by Manufacturing Dive as a deliberate part of the Southeast's battery-workforce buildout) that QCEW employment counts do not capture at all.

Two of these are directly validated against real battery investments: Ottawa County, MI is the actual county for LG Energy Solution's Holland, MI battery-cell plant, and Troup County, GA (West Point) sits in the Kia Georgia / West Point EV-supplier corridor that also supports SK Battery America nearby. Large metro counties (Los Angeles, Cook/Chicago, Harris/Houston, Wayne/Detroit) have far larger absolute manufacturing headcounts but much lower LQs (roughly 7-14 for Wayne/Macomb/Oakland MI, but near 1 for LA/Houston/Chicago once diluted across a much bigger, more diversified economy) — a reminder that raw employment counts alone overstate 'workforce readiness' in a giant metro and understate it in a specialized mid-size county.

A real 'cheap land, not workforce' case: Haywood County, TN

Ford's BlueOval City (battery cells plus EV assembly, Stanton, TN) sits in Haywood County — total private employment of only ~4,870 in 2023, battery-relevant LQ of 0.26 (roughly a quarter of the national concentration), and a median home value of $119,600, among the cheapest in this comparison set. This is the clearest quantitative case in the data for a site chosen primarily for land availability, highway/rail access, and state incentives rather than an existing skilled manufacturing base — consistent with public reporting that Tennessee and Ford have had to build workforce housing and training programs around the site rather than draw on a local pipeline.

Caveats

  • NAICS 335/3363/333 is a proxy, not a battery-specific occupational measure; this corpus has no county-level skilled-trades certification or battery-specific workforce data, and the search for one returned no matching table.
  • 395 of 3,274 county-level QCEW area codes (12.1%) — named by state above — are excluded from the ranking because BLS suppresses their employment figures (disclosure_code='N') to protect a dominant single employer's identity. These are disproportionately the sparsest rural counties in Texas, Nebraska, Georgia, Kansas, the Dakotas, Montana, Kentucky, Colorado and Alaska, so a genuine rural workforce concentration in one of those counties could be invisible here rather than confirmed absent.
  • LQ favors small/mid-size counties with a narrow, specialized economic base; very large diversified metro counties will always show lower LQs even with substantial absolute manufacturing headcounts, so LQ and raw headcount answer slightly different questions and both are reported.
  • Home value is used as a land-cost proxy, not literal industrial land price, which this corpus does not carry at county grain.

What This Report Does Not Answer

  • Direct county-level battery-specific occupational/skills data (electricians, machinists, cell technicians): No table in this corpus carries battery-specific or trade-specific (electrician/machinist) occupational counts at county grain; NAICS 335/3363/333 QCEW employment is used as the nearest available proxy and is disclosed as such throughout.
