Auto insurance loss costs rose roughly 14% annualized nationally 2020-2024; repair/parts inflation is a documented but partial driver, with state-level variation dominated by litigation, fraud, and accident/injury-claim patterns

NAIC/Triple-I average expenditure data, ISO Fast Track state cost-driver data, BLS CPI, CCC claims data — all external sources; no state-level loss-cost or repair-CPI table exists in the AskAmerica government-data connector

Summary

Nationally, personal auto insurance losses grew about 14% annualized from 2020 to 2024, outrunning the 9% annualized growth in premiums over the same span (Insurance Research Council, based on S&P Global data) — which is why the personal-auto combined ratio (losses+expenses/premiums) blew out to 104.9% in 2023 before falling back to 95.3% in 2024 as rate increases caught up (Fitch Ratings, via MoneyGeek). The average NAIC-reported expenditure per insured vehicle rose from $1,075 (2019) to $1,282 (2023), roughly +19% cumulatively, and BLS's CPI for motor vehicle insurance shows the increase continuing into 2024 (+17.8%) before slowing to roughly +6% in 2025, for a cumulative +82.7% rise from 2016 to 2025.

Repair and parts cost inflation is a real, well-documented contributor — but it is only one of several named cost drivers, not the dominant one, and it explains almost none of the very large state-to-state spread in loss-cost levels. The Insurance Research Council's own state-by-state cost-driver breakdown (built on ISO's Fast Track Monitoring System, 2023 data) names eight distinct drivers of affordability by state: accident frequency, repair costs, tendency to file injury claims, injury-claim severity, insurer expense/litigation-cost ratio, uninsured-motorist rates, underinsured-motorist rates, and claim litigation rates. Repair cost is explicitly one line in that table, not the explanation for the others. Comparing the BLS motor-vehicle-insurance CPI (+82.7%, 2016-2025) to the BLS motor-vehicle-body-work (repair) CPI (+50.5% over the same span) suggests repair/parts inflation accounts for roughly 60% of the magnitude of the cumulative premium/CPI increase as a directional order of magnitude — not a source-published, precise attribution, since no primary source publishes one. The residual is attributed by IRC, Triple-I, and CCC Intelligent Solutions to bodily-injury/medical-cost severity (rising 3-4x faster than general inflation per CCC's Q4 2025 data), "social inflation"/litigation, record total-loss frequency (23.1% of claims in 2024), and a shrinking insured pool (uninsured-driver rate reached 15.4% in 2023, feeding a vicious cycle IRC calls "deteriorating affordability").

Scope note on this connector: the AskAmerica government-data connector (askengine) has no table carrying state-level private auto insurance loss costs or premiums, and no loaded BLS CPI subindex specifically for motor vehicle maintenance/repair or motor vehicle parts and equipment — confirmed via two separate catalog searches, both of which returned "repair" and "equipment" as unmatched terms with no genuine table match (the connector's own new-vehicle CPI table was surfaced by the search but was never queried for this report, since it does not cover repair/parts pricing). This is a confirmed sourcing gap; every figure in this report is drawn from external primary/authoritative sources (NAIC, ISO Fast Track via IRC, BLS, CCC Intelligent Solutions, Fitch Ratings) rather than the connector's own warehouse.

IRC's brief (based on S&P Global data with 2024 projections) frames the same period as underlying losses (as distinct from premiums, which lag losses) growing 14% annualized 2020-2024 versus 9% annualized premium growth — the gap being exactly why the industry ran an underwriting loss for three straight years before 2024 (personal auto combined ratio 104.9% in 2023, per Fitch Ratings). ValuePenguin/S&P RateWatch rate-filing data show average filed increases of +12.0% (2023) and +16.5% (2024), slowing to an expected +7.5% (2025); BLS's CPI reading (+17.4% 2023, +17.8% 2024, +6.0% 2025) tells the same story with slightly different methodology (CPI measures what consumers actually pay across quote/renewal cycles, filings measure approved rate changes).

How much tracks repair and parts cost inflation specifically

  • BLS CPI, Motor vehicle body work (a repair-labor/parts proxy): rose from index 287.6 (2016) to 432.8 (2025), +50.5% cumulative, with the sharpest single-year jump in 2022 (+12.5%) amid pandemic-era supply-chain disruption.
  • CCC Intelligent Solutions (claims-systems data covering most U.S. collision repairs): average total cost of repair exceeded $4,730 in 2024 (+3.8% y/y), reaching $4,768 through September 2025. Average parts costs, flat in 2022-2023, rose >6% year over year in both Q2 and Q3 2025 after 25% tariffs on imported auto parts took effect May 3, 2025 (roughly 60% of replacement parts are imported); a survey of 500 repair shops found 38.6% already reporting tariff impact.
  • IRC's own cost-driver table explicitly lists "repair costs" (median property-damage-liability + collision claim payment, from ISO Fast Track 2023 data) as one of eight named state-level cost drivers, alongside accident frequency, injury-claim tendency and severity, insurer expense/litigation ratio, uninsured- and underinsured-motorist rates, and claim litigation — a structural statement, from the industry's own research body, that repair cost is A driver, not THE driver.
  • Comparing the BLS motor-vehicle-insurance CPI (+82.7% cumulative, 2016-2025) to the motor-vehicle-body-work CPI (+50.5% cumulative) over the identical window gives a rough order-of-magnitude estimate that repair/parts inflation accounts for on the order of 60% of the cumulative CPI increase in the premium series. This is a directional proxy built from two published index series, not a source-published attribution — no primary source (NAIC, IRC, CCC, Triple-I) publishes a precise percentage decomposition of loss-cost growth into repair/parts vs. other causes, and MoneyGeek's own analysis states this gap explicitly.
  • Named non-repair drivers of the residual: bodily-injury/medical severity (average third-party BI paid outcome reached $27,600 through mid-2025, +35% since 2020, rising 3-4x faster than general inflation per CCC Q4 2025); a documented academic finding (Grace, Leverty & Powell, NAIC Journal of Insurance Regulation, 2019) that auto insurance costs correlate with medical-cost inflation at 0.986; record total-loss frequency (23.1% of claims in 2024, itself partly a repair-cost feedback effect since high repair bills push more vehicles past economic-repair thresholds); and a shrinking insured pool (uninsured-driver rate 15.4% in 2023, rising as premiums outpace what marginal drivers can afford, per IRC).

State-level variation: repair cost is one factor among several, not the primary explanation

2023 NAIC average expenditure per vehicle ranged from $1,494.70 in Louisiana and $1,436 in New York down to $691.77 in North Dakota and $703.81 in Maine — more than a 2x spread. IRC's affordability index (expenditure as a share of median household income, 2022 data) shows an even wider structural range: 0.93% in North Dakota to 2.67% in Louisiana, with Florida, Mississippi, New York, and Georgia rounding out the five least-affordable states.

Repair and parts prices are set in largely national/regional supply and labor markets and do not plausibly vary 2x state to state. IRC's own cost-driver table (built directly from ISO Fast Track state-level 2023 data) puts "repair costs" alongside seven other factors and shows the least-affordable states (Louisiana, Florida, Mississippi, New York, Georgia, South Carolina, Michigan, West Virginia, Nevada) consistently rating unfavorably on litigation, injury-claim tendency/severity, and uninsured/underinsured-motorist exposure — not uniquely on repair cost. Specific documented mechanisms: Louisiana's injury claims run roughly 200% above the national average on only slightly elevated accident rates, a litigation-driven pattern; Florida's high rates are tied to mandatory no-fault PIP coverage (a target for staged-accident fraud), one of the highest uninsured-motorist rates in the country, hurricane-season comprehensive losses, and (historically) one-way attorney-fee-shifting rules since reformed in 2022-2023; Nevada's 2025 rate jump (+27.9%, the largest of any state that year) was tied specifically to litigation activity in the Las Vegas market; Vermont sits at the opposite pole (low density, low litigation, low fraud, low uninsured-motorist rate) and has correspondingly low rates.

Repair/parts inflation is therefore best understood as a national cost pressure that raises the floor for every state by a broadly similar amount, while the much larger state-to-state spread in loss-cost levels is explained mainly by each state's litigation/tort environment, fraud prevalence, injury-claim behavior, and uninsured/underinsured-motorist exposure — factors layered on top of, not caused by, the shared national repair-cost trend.
