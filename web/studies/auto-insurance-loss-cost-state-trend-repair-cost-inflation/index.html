Auto insurance loss costs rose 56% nationally since 2019, far outpacing repair/parts inflation on a same-year basis but tracking it closely with a 1-2 year lag — and the corpus has no state-level loss-cost-per-vehicle table to source the state comparison directly

BLS CPI-U (NSA) motor vehicle insurance vs. motor vehicle body work indices, 2016-2025; NAIC/Triple-I state expenditure and Insurify state rate data

Summary

Nationally, the BLS Motor Vehicle Insurance CPI rose 56.5% from 2019 to 2025 (index 571.0→893.5), more than double the 25.9% rise in overall consumer prices over the same span, and well ahead of the 38.1% rise in the Motor Vehicle Body Work (collision repair) CPI, the closest available government proxy for repair-and-parts cost inflation. Measured in the same calendar year, repair-cost inflation and insurance-premium inflation are essentially uncorrelated (r = 0.04) — but lagged one to two years, the correlation is strong (r = 0.70–0.75). Repair costs spiked hardest in 2022 (+12.5%); insurance premiums did not spike until 2023–2024 (+17.4%, +17.8%), which matches how state rate-filing and approval cycles actually work — insurers pass rising loss costs into rates with a delay, not instantaneously. State-by-state loss-cost-per-vehicle data could not be sourced from the AskAmerica warehouse (confirmed gap, see Data availability below); the state comparison below instead relies on published NAIC/Triple-I state expenditure tables and Insurify's state rate-filing trackers, both external to this connector.

How much loss costs have risen (national, and by state where third-party data allows)

National: NAIC-reported average private-passenger auto insurance expenditure per vehicle rose from $1,071.74 (2019) to $1,282 (2023, latest NAIC year published as of this report) — a 19.6% increase in nominal dollars over four years, accelerating sharply in the final year (+14.0% in 2023 alone, after +7.9% in 2022). Industry-wide incurred physical-damage losses (collision+comprehensive, the claims-cost component closest to "loss cost") rose from $54.66B in 2020 to $89.50B in 2024, +63.8% in four years, per NAIC/S&P data compiled by Triple-I.

By state: Florida ($1,865), Louisiana ($1,754) and New York ($1,753) had the highest average per-vehicle expenditures in 2023; North Dakota, Maine and Iowa the lowest. On rate-change trackers (not the same series as NAIC expenditure, but the best available year-over-year state view): Insurify's 2024 forecast found California, Minnesota, and Missouri facing the sharpest single-year increases (+54%, +61%, +55% respectively, vs. a ~22% national average for that year), and a separate multi-year (2020-2025) Insurify analysis found a 29.3% mean national increase with five states — Florida, Nevada, Oklahoma, Colorado, and South Dakota — exceeding 50% cumulative growth over that period. These are filed/advertised rate changes, not the same measurement basis as NAIC's realized expenditure-per-vehicle figure, and are cited as directionally indicative of which states saw the steepest increases, not as an exact loss-cost-per-vehicle series.

How much of that tracks repair and parts cost inflation specifically

The federal government does not publish a CPI component isolated to "vehicle parts" alone at national or state grain; the closest matching BLS series is Motor Vehicle Body Work (CPI item SS45011), which blends collision-repair labor and the parts used in that labor, U.S. city average, not seasonally adjusted. From 2019 to 2025 it rose 38.1% (313.5→432.8), versus the 56.5% rise in the Motor Vehicle Insurance CPI over the same window — so repair/parts inflation explains roughly two-thirds (38.1/56.5 ≈ 67%) of the cumulative percentage-point rise in insurance costs on a like-for-like index basis, with the remainder attributable to bodily-injury/liability claim severity (which Triple-I/ISO data show rising even faster — bodily-injury claim severity rose from $19,151 in 2019 to $28,278 in 2024, +47.7%, and bodily-injury claims overtook physical-damage claims as the larger dollar share of paid claims for the first time in 2025 per CCC Intelligent Solutions), total-loss frequency (23.1% of claims in 2024/2025, a record, per CCC), and non-repair cost drivers (reinsurance, litigation/social inflation, claims handling).

Timing matters more than the raw correlation suggests. Computed directly from the BLS series (python3, numpy corrcoef): the same-year year-over-year correlation between repair-cost inflation and insurance-premium inflation is essentially zero (r=0.036, 2017-2025). But insurance premiums track repair-cost inflation from one to two years earlier much more closely (r=0.70 at a 1-year lag, r=0.75 at a 2-year lag, n=8 and n=7 respectively — small samples, so these are suggestive, not definitive). CCC Intelligent Solutions' claims data corroborates the mechanism directly: average parts prices accelerated through 2025 (roughly +4.4% in spring, +5.0% in June, +6.6% in July, largely attributed to tariff pass-through), and the number of parts quoted per repair job has risen from 5.3 (2020) to 7.4 (2024) for passenger cars as vehicles have grown more complex (more sensors, calibrations, ADAS components) — both of which raise loss costs that show up in future rate filings, not the filing year's own premium change.

Data availability in the AskAmerica warehouse (a genuine gap, confirmed)

search_catalog was queried for "auto insurance loss cost", "motor vehicle repair parts price index CPI", and "motor vehicle insurance CPI item series" — no table in the warehouse carries state-level auto insurance loss costs, premiums, or claims data (NAIC/ISO/state DOI filings are not ingested), and the word "repair" appears in zero table or column description in the corpus. The warehouse's CPI tables (econ.regional_cpi, econ.metro_cpi, econ.inflation_metrics) carry only all-items CPI, core CPI, food CPI, and water/sewerage maintenance CPI at the national/regional/metro grain — none of the motor-vehicle-insurance or motor-vehicle-repair CPI item series used in this report are loaded, and none of the four CPI tables break out by state (only 4 Census regions or 20 metro areas). Every figure in this report is therefore sourced externally (BLS CPI series as compiled and republished by Triple-I/III.org, NAIC data as compiled by Triple-I, and Insurify's state rate-filing trackers), not from an AskAmerica table — flagged here explicitly per the corpus's own sourcing rules, and reported to the maintainers as a genuine coverage gap (no state-level insurance-loss-cost table; no motor-vehicle CPI item-detail series loaded).

What This Report Does Not Answer

  • How much has it risen BY STATE specifically: No AskAmerica table carries state-level auto insurance loss costs or premiums (confirmed via search_catalog — zero matches, and 'repair' matches nothing in the corpus). Reported instead from external NAIC/Triple-I state expenditure tables (single-year state ranking, 2023) and Insurify's state rate-filing trackers (year-over-year % change by state, a related but not identical measure to loss cost per vehicle) — both flagged as external, non-warehouse sources with a different measurement basis than a true loss-cost-per-vehicle series.
  • How much of the rise tracks vehicle PARTS cost inflation specifically: BLS does not publish a standalone 'motor vehicle parts' CPI index separate from labor within the Motor Vehicle Body Work series, and no such series is loaded in the AskAmerica warehouse either. Addressed instead via CCC Intelligent Solutions' claims-level parts-price-inflation data (a proprietary industry data provider, not a government CPI series), reported as a distinct, clearly-labeled data source rather than blended into the CPI figure.
