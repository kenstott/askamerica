Auto loss cost per car was roughly flat 2019-2021 because claim frequency fell, while claim severity rose 28%; repair and parts CPI explain only a minority of that severity rise

NAIC 2021/2022 Auto Insurance Database Report (total business, 2019-2021) and BLS CPI via econ.new_vehicles_cpi. Texas not reported.

Summary

Loss cost per insured car (incurred losses per earned exposure: liability + collision + comprehensive, NAIC) rose only about 1% nationally from 2019 to 2021 (about $956 to $956-ish; countrywide $945 to $956 summed by coverage; ex-Texas $821 to $864, +5.2%). The state range was -22% (WY, MT) to +15% (LA). This is a COVID artifact: collision claim frequency fell from 6.03 to 5.21 per 100 cars while severity (loss per claim) rose 28% (+$1,344, from $4,806 to $6,150). Repair and parts price inflation tracked only part of that severity jump: BLS motor vehicle maintenance and repair rose 7.4% and parts 5.9% over 2019-2021 (body work 9.4%), roughly a quarter of the severity increase; used-car prices (+30.7%) matched it much better. Across states, 2019-21 collision severity growth had essentially no correlation with total loss-cost change (r=0.00, n=50), and BLS publishes repair CPI nationally only, so a state-level repair-cost share cannot be measured.

Loss cost by state

Computed as sum of the three coverages' pure premium (NAIC Tables 26C, 29C, 32C). Largest 2019-21 increases: Louisiana +15.4% ($1,238 to $1,428), Oklahoma +10.5%, Tennessee +7.0%, Indiana +6.6%, Arkansas +6.3%, Georgia +5.8%, Mississippi +5.7%, California +4.7%. Largest declines: Wyoming -21.6%, Montana -21.4%, Colorado -17.5%, Hawaii -15.4%, Nebraska -10.8%, Michigan -10.7%. Median state: -1.0%. The 2019 comprehensive figures for WY, MT, CO are inflated by hail/large-loss years, which drives several of the big declines; small-state comprehensive is volatile. The 2022 edition's headline (collision average incurred per claim $6,113 in 2021 to $7,191 in 2022, +17.6%; combined average premium up 14.4% over 2022-2023) indicates the post-2021 rebound, but I could not open that PDF (NAIC returned HTTP 403), so state loss cost for 2022 and later is NOT computed here. Loss cost is a different thing from premium: the BLS motor vehicle insurance CPI rose 56% from 2019 to 2025 (571 to 893).

How much tracks repair and parts inflation

  • National collision severity 2019-21: +28.0%. Repair CPI (SETD) +7.4%; body work (SETD01) +9.4%; parts (SETC) +5.9%; used cars (SETA02) +30.7%. Naively repair plus parts explain about 20-30% of severity growth; the rest reflects used-car/total-loss values, vehicle complexity and mix, and claim-mix shifts (fewer minor claims during lockdowns lifts average severity).
  • Later CPI (annual averages, 2019 to 2025): repair +45.8%, parts +26.1%, so the repair-cost pressure grew after the NAIC loss window and probably explains more of 2022-2025 severity.
  • Cross-state: regression of total loss-cost change on collision severity change gave slope 0.005, R2 about 0.00, n=50. State loss cost is dominated by frequency changes, so a state-level repair-inflation attribution is not supported. Severity growth by state (12.6% TN to 38.7% FL) is the only state-level proxy available; it is not repair CPI.

Caveats

Most recent state loss data used is accident year 2021 (the 2022/2023 edition was unreachable). Texas is excluded from pure premium. Losses are developed liability and undeveloped physical damage, so severity is slightly understated for recent years. Window 2019-2021 was chosen because it is the latest with state loss data I could read and 2019 is the pre-pandemic baseline. CPI is national, so no state attribution. The warehouse has no auto-insurance table; the NAIC figures were read from the Maryland Insurance Administration's mirror of the NAIC PDF and computed with Python; CPI came from the warehouse.

What This Report Does Not Answer

  • Most recent years (2022+): 2022/2023 NAIC PDF returned HTTP 403; only 2019-2021 state data computed
