Immigrant households pay more in taxes than they receive in benefits nationally and over their lifetimes — but the balance flips by education level, legal status, and level of government

Synthesis of Cato Institute, ITEP, National Academies of Sciences, Manhattan Institute, CBO, and CIS research, 1994-2025

Summary

Averaged across all immigrants and over time, immigrant households pay more in taxes than they receive in public benefits — but the size and even the sign of that gap depends heavily on which slice of the immigrant population, which level of government, and which time horizon you look at.

  • In aggregate, over the long run: the Cato Institute finds immigrants generated a cumulative fiscal surplus of $14.5 trillion from 1994-2023, paying about $100,000 (17%) more in lifetime taxes per person than the U.S.-born on average, in every single year of that 30-year span.
  • Undocumented immigrants specifically paid an estimated $96.7 billion in federal, state and local taxes in 2022 ($8,889/person) — more than a third of it ($33.9B) into Social Security, Medicare and unemployment insurance, programs most of them are barred from ever collecting from.
  • But the picture is not uniform. The National Academies of Sciences' lifetime model finds immigrants without a high school diploma are a net fiscal cost (-$13,000 NPV), while those with more than a high school education are strongly net-positive (+$198,000 NPV). Fiscal impact is also positive at the federal level (immigrants skew young and working-age) but frequently negative at the state/local level (which funds public education for immigrants' children, mostly U.S.-citizen kids, without recouping much of the later payoff).
  • Program participation is a different metric than net dollars. CIS finds immigrant-headed households use means-tested welfare programs (food aid, Medicaid, EITC) at meaningfully higher rates than U.S.-born households (52.7% vs. 37.3%, and 61% for undocumented-headed households) — but this measure excludes Social Security/Medicare and says nothing about how much was paid in versus drawn out, so it is not in direct conflict with the net-fiscal-balance findings above; the two organizations are answering different questions with different metrics.

There is no single defensible number for "how much immigrant households pay in taxes vs. receive in benefits" — the honest answer names which population, which government level, and which time window the number describes.

1. The headline number: aggregate net fiscal surplus over three decades

The most-cited long-run estimate comes from the Cato Institute (Alex Nowrasteh, 2024), which tracks taxes paid against benefits received by the entire foreign-born population against all levels of government, every year from 1994 to 2023:

  • Immigrants paid more in taxes than they received in benefits in every single year of the 30-year period.
  • Cumulative fiscal surplus: $14.5 trillion in real 2024 dollars (including an estimated $3.9 trillion in interest-on-the-debt savings from a smaller deficit).
  • That $14.5 trillion equals roughly 33% of total combined U.S. deficits over 1994-2023 — i.e., deficits would have been about a third larger without immigrants in the population.
  • On a per-capita basis, immigrants paid nearly $100,000 (17%) more in lifetime taxes than the average U.S.-born person over the period, while imposing lower per-person costs on old-age benefit programs, education, and public safety (a function of immigrants skewing working-age rather than retirement-age).

The Brookings Institution and the Bipartisan Policy Center summarize a broadly similar consensus: immigration currently reduces, not increases, federal deficits on net, primarily via payroll and income tax contributions from a working-age population.

The Congressional Budget Office's 2024 immigration-surge analysis (widely reported, though CBO's own site returned an access error on direct fetch and this figure is relayed via secondary press coverage rather than a primary-document read) estimated that the 2021-2026 surge of roughly 8.7 million net immigrants would raise GDP by about $9 trillion and reduce federal deficits by about $0.9 trillion over 2024-2034, with the surge population paying an estimated $788 billion in income and payroll taxes over that window.

2. Undocumented immigrants pay tens of billions despite being barred from most benefits

The Institute on Taxation and Economic Policy (ITEP, 2024) produced the most detailed estimate of undocumented immigrants' tax payments specifically — this population is the one most often singled out in the 'do they pay taxes' debate:

  • Undocumented immigrants paid an estimated $96.7 billion in federal, state, and local taxes in 2022 — $59.4B federal, $37.3B state/local.
  • That works out to $8,889 per person ($8.9 billion per every 1 million undocumented residents).
  • Their overall effective tax rate (26.1% of income) is close to the median U.S. household's rate (26.4%).
  • $33.9 billion of that — over a third — went to Social Security ($25.7B), Medicare ($6.4B) and unemployment insurance ($1.8B), programs from which undocumented workers are largely barred from ever collecting benefits.
  • In 40 of 50 states, undocumented immigrants pay a higher effective state-and-local tax rate than the top 1% of earners in that state (8.9% vs. 7.2% nationally), because sales/excise and property taxes are regressive and undocumented immigrants cannot access many income-tax credits.
  • If granted work authorization, ITEP projects their tax contributions would rise by $40.2 billion/year to $136.9 billion, mostly via higher wages and compliance.

3. It flips by education, age, and government level: the National Academies model

The definitive lifetime-impact study is the National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine's 2017 report, The Economic and Fiscal Consequences of Immigration. Its dynamic, intergenerational model estimates the net present value (lifetime taxes minus lifetime benefits) of an immigrant by education level at arrival:

  • Less than high school: -$13,000 (net fiscal cost)
  • High school diploma: +$51,000
  • More than high school: +$198,000

The NAS also identifies a structural federal/state-local split that recurs across the literature: fiscal impact is generally positive at the federal level and generally negative at the state and local level over a 75-year projection. The mechanism: state and local governments bear the cost of public education for immigrants' children (many of them U.S. citizens by birth), but recoup relatively little of the later payoff through their tax systems, whereas federal benefit spending concentrates on the elderly — and arriving immigrants are disproportionately young, working, and tax-paying.

Updating this line of research, the Manhattan Institute (Daniel Di Martino, October 2025), using a CBO-style 10-/30-year budget-window methodology, confirms the same pattern: immigrants with a bachelor's degree or higher, especially those arriving before age 40, produce large fiscal surpluses, while immigrants without a college degree — and their descendants — tend to receive more in benefits than they pay in taxes, even excluding retirement-age costs. By legal category, employment-based immigrants are the most fiscally positive and parents of U.S. citizens (a family-sponsored category) the most negative; the average lawful immigrant reduces the deficit, and the average unauthorized immigrant modestly expands it while still growing the economy.

4. A different metric tells a different story: program participation rates

Not all research on this question measures net dollars. The Center for Immigration Studies (CIS, 2024), using 2024 Census Bureau SIPP data (accessed via search-result summary; the CIS site returned an HTTP 403 to direct fetch, so these figures are relayed rather than independently re-verified against the primary table), reports a different quantity: the share of households that use at least one means-tested welfare program (food assistance, Medicaid, EITC, housing aid, TANF — explicitly excluding Social Security and Medicare):

  • U.S.-born-headed households: 37.3%
  • All immigrant-headed households: 52.7%
  • Legal-immigrant-headed households: 51%
  • Illegal-immigrant-headed households: 61%

This is a real and consistently-replicated finding, but it answers a narrower question than 'net fiscal balance' — it counts any use of a means-tested program (often driven by U.S.-citizen children in a mixed-status household qualifying for food or health aid) without netting against taxes paid or including the social-insurance programs (Social Security, Medicare, UI) into which immigrants — including many who cannot draw from them — pay heavily, as documented in Section 2. CIS and Cato have published direct rebuttals of each other's framing; the two are not measuring the same thing and neither is 'wrong' as far as what it actually counts.

Why the numbers differ, and how to read them

Four choices explain most of the disagreement across sources on this topic:

  • Unit of analysis: household vs. individual. A household containing a U.S.-citizen spouse or citizen children complicates any 'immigrant household' tally.
  • Time horizon: a single-year snapshot (Cato's annual series, CIS's SIPP snapshot) vs. a lifetime net-present-value model (NAS, Manhattan Institute) can show opposite signs for the same person at different life stages — young immigrants are net contributors while working, net beneficiaries as children and in retirement.
  • Level of government: federal (generally positive, driven by payroll/income tax and immigrants' younger age profile) vs. state/local (generally negative, driven by K-12 education costs) — a single 'net' number that doesn't split these out obscures a real and consistent pattern.
  • What counts as a 'benefit': means-tested welfare only (CIS) vs. all government spending including social insurance and a share of public goods (Cato, NAS) produces very different pictures, especially for undocumented immigrants who pay heavily into programs (Social Security, Medicare) they are excluded from drawing on.

Taken together, the weight of the peer-reviewed and government-adjacent research (NAS 2017, CBO, Cato, Manhattan Institute) supports a net-positive aggregate fiscal contribution from immigrant households, concentrated among working-age and more-educated immigrants and at the federal level — while means-tested program participation, a real and separately-documented phenomenon, runs higher among immigrant-headed households than native-born households, particularly for less-educated and unauthorized immigrants and at the state/local level.
