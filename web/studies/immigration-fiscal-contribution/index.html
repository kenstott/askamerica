Immigrant households: taxes paid roughly track or exceed benefits received in the aggregate, but the balance flips sharply by education, legal status, and time horizon

Synthesis of Cato Institute (NASEM methodology), ITEP, and Manhattan Institute fiscal-impact research, with supplementary Census ACS income-by-nativity data

Summary

Averaged across the whole immigrant population, taxes paid exceed benefits received. Cato Institute's 30-year analysis (1994-2023, using the National Academies of Sciences/Engineering/Medicine methodology) finds immigrants generated a cumulative $14.5 trillion fiscal surplus for federal, state, and local governments combined — paying more in taxes than they drew in benefits in every single year of the period, including noncitizens (a group that includes undocumented immigrants), who alone accounted for $6.3 trillion of that surplus. Undocumented immigrants specifically paid an estimated $96.7 billion in taxes in 2022 (ITEP), or $8,889 per person, at an effective tax rate (26.1% of income) close to the median U.S. taxpayer's (26.4%) — while being legally barred from most of the Social Security, Medicare, and unemployment-insurance benefits a third of that money funds.

But the aggregate surplus masks a wide split. Manhattan Institute's 2025 lifetime/CBO-window analysis finds immigrants without a college degree tend to receive more in benefits than they pay in taxes, even before retirement, while college- and graduate-degree immigrants contribute millions more in taxes than they draw in benefits. Legal status matters too: employment-based immigrants are the most fiscally positive category; the average unlawful immigrant still expands GDP but adds modestly to the debt over Manhattan Institute's near-term methodology, a result that differs from Cato's finding of a cumulative historical surplus for noncitizens — the two studies use different populations, time windows, and methods, and are not directly comparable.

The aggregate picture: a $14.5 trillion 30-year fiscal surplus (Cato Institute)

Cato Institute's report "Immigrants' Recent Effects on Government Budgets: 1994-2023" follows the NASEM methodology used by the 2017 National Academies consensus study and finds:

  • Immigrants generated a cumulative $14.5 trillion fiscal surplus (in real 2024 dollars) across federal, state, and local budgets combined over 1994-2023, including roughly $3.9 trillion in savings on interest payments on the national debt that a higher deficit would otherwise have accrued.
  • Noncitizens — a category that includes undocumented immigrants, green-card holders, and other non-naturalized residents — contributed $6.3 trillion of that total surplus on their own.
  • Immigrants accounted for about 14% of total tax revenue collected over the period but only about 7% of government spending, and impose lower per-person costs than U.S.-born residents on old-age benefit programs, education, and public safety.

This is a population-wide, historical accounting figure; it nets taxes against benefits and government services year by year and does not project forward.

Undocumented immigrants specifically: a detailed 2022 tax accounting (ITEP)

ITEP's July 2024 study, "Tax Payments by Undocumented Immigrants", is the most granular recent breakdown of what this specific subgroup pays, based on 2022 data for an estimated 10.9 million undocumented immigrants:

  • $96.7 billion total taxes paid: $59.4B federal, $37.3B state and local.
  • $8,889 per person in taxes — for every 1 million undocumented immigrants in the country, public budgets receive about $8.9 billion in tax revenue.
  • More than a third of the total ($33.9B) is Social Security, Medicare, and unemployment-insurance payroll tax — taxes that fund programs undocumented immigrants are largely barred from ever collecting on. This is the clearest case where the tax/benefit ledger is structurally lopsided in the government's favor for a specific tax dollar.
  • At the state and local level, undocumented immigrants pay an average effective tax rate of 8.9% of income — higher than the 7.2% average rate paid by the top 1% of earners, and higher than the top 1%'s rate in 40 of 50 states, mostly because of sales, excise, and property taxes that fall regardless of immigration status.
  • The total effective rate across all taxes (26.1% of income) is close to the 26.4% rate the median-income U.S. household pays.
  • If granted work authorization, ITEP projects tax contributions would rise by $40.2B/year to $136.9B, reflecting both higher wages and higher compliance.

On the benefits side, undocumented immigrants are ineligible for SNAP, most Medicaid, Social Security, SSI, TANF, and ACA marketplace subsidies in nearly all states; a handful of states extend limited state-funded Medicaid-equivalent coverage to some populations (e.g., children, pregnant women). This asymmetry — taxed under most systems, excluded from most benefit systems — is the central mechanical reason this subgroup's tax/benefit ratio runs favorable to public budgets.

The balance flips by education level and time horizon (Manhattan Institute, 2025)

Manhattan Institute's October 2025 report, "The Fiscal Impact of Immigration (2025 Update)", by Daniel Di Martino, builds on NASEM's approach but scores immigrants over CBO's standard 10- and 30-year federal budget windows rather than a full lifetime, splitting the population into 35 groups by education level (5) and age at arrival (7). Its key findings qualify the aggregate positive-surplus story substantially:

  • Education is the dominant driver. Immigrants with a bachelor's or advanced degree, especially those arriving before age 40, produce large fiscal surpluses. Immigrants with only a high school diploma or less — along with their descendants — tend to receive more in benefits than they pay in taxes, in both the short and long term.
  • Legal status matters. Employment-based immigrants are the most fiscally positive category; parents of U.S. citizens (a family-based category) are the most negative. The average legal immigrant reduces the federal deficit; the average unlawful immigrant still grows the economy but adds to the national debt over this near-term window — a different framing and result than Cato's finding of a historical surplus for noncitizens as a whole, reflecting the different time horizons and inclusion of descendants in the MI model.
  • A separate 2024 MI lifetime study (the predecessor to this update) found that, over an entire lifetime, immigrants overall contribute slightly more in taxes than they receive in benefits, but immigrants without college degrees, and those who arrive after their mid-fifties at any education level, are net fiscal burdens over their lifetime.

Supplementary corpus data: household income by nativity as a proxy for tax capacity

AskAmerica's warehouse does not carry a direct tax-payments-by-immigration-status table or a benefits-received-by-immigration-status table — search_catalog returned no match for "immigrant" or "taxes" together with a benefits/tax-incidence table. As a partial, indirect supplement, Census ACS Subject Table S0501 (census.acs_income_by_nativity) reports median household income by nativity and citizenship status, which correlates with tax-paying capacity even though it is not a tax or benefits figure itself. A household-weighted national average of the 2023 state-level medians shows:

  • Native-born households: $79,187
  • Foreign-born households (all): $79,565 — essentially on par with native-born
  • Naturalized-citizen households: $88,969 — higher than native-born
  • Noncitizen households: $67,666 — lower than native-born

This pattern is directionally consistent with the literature above: naturalized citizens (who have typically resided longer, hold work authorization, and skew toward employment-based or family-sponsored legal pathways) show the strongest income profile, while noncitizen households (a group that includes recent arrivals, temporary visa holders, and undocumented immigrants) show a weaker one — aligning with Manhattan Institute's finding that legal status and time in the country shape fiscal outcomes. This is a proxy, not a direct answer, and should not be read as itself a tax or benefits total.

Why estimates vary, and how to read a specific number

Four choices explain most of the spread across studies: (1) population defined — all immigrants, noncitizens only, or undocumented immigrants only, each a different subgroup with very different profiles; (2) unit of analysis — household vs. individual, which matters enormously for mixed-status households; (3) time horizon — a single year, a historical 30-year window, a forward 10/30-year CBO-style window, or a full lifetime, each capturing a different mix of working-age tax contributions against old-age benefit draws; and (4) what counts as a "benefit" — direct cash/in-kind transfers only, versus a full allocation of public-goods spending (defense, infrastructure, interest on the debt) across the population. A single soundbite figure ("immigrants pay $X" or "cost $Y") is rarely wrong on its own terms, but it is almost always describing one specific slice of these four choices, not a universal fact about all immigrant households everywhere.

query — 1 rows — 855 ms
SELECT "year",
  SUM(households_native) AS hh_native, SUM(households_foreign_born) AS hh_fb,
  SUM(households_naturalized) AS hh_nat, SUM(households_noncitizen) AS hh_noncit,
  SUM(median_household_income_native * households_native) * 1.0 / NULLIF(SUM(households_native),0) AS wavg_income_native,
  SUM(median_household_income_foreign_born * households_foreign_born) * 1.0 / NULLIF(SUM(households_foreign_born),0) AS wavg_income_fb,
  SUM(median_household_income_naturalized * households_naturalized) * 1.0 / NULLIF(SUM(households_naturalized),0) AS wavg_income_nat,
  SUM(median_household_income_noncitizen * households_noncitizen) * 1.0 / NULLIF(SUM(households_noncitizen),0) AS wavg_income_noncit
FROM census.acs_income_by_nativity
WHERE "year" = '2023'
GROUP BY "year"
