Vance's "ethnic enclaves cause higher crime" claim is contradicted by research and by AskAmerica's own state-level data

Validation of newrepublic.com/post/184971 (Aug 16, 2024) — JD Vance's press-conference remarks on immigration and crime

Summary

Yes — the article's core factual claims hold up. At an Aug. 16, 2024 press conference, JD Vance stated that "ethnic enclaves" can "lead to higher crime rates," citing the 2002 film Gangs of New York. That underlying empirical claim is not supported by the evidence: peer-reviewed criminology research on immigrant concentration and crime finds the opposite (immigrant-dense neighborhoods tend to have lower, not higher, crime), and a direct state-level check run here — foreign-born household share against 2022 violent crime rates across the 50 states plus DC — finds essentially no correlation (r = 0.04). The article's supporting point, that immigrants are less likely to commit crimes than native-born Americans, is well established. The article's other factual details (Kamala Harris's bail-fund tweet, Vance's on-camera flubs) are accurately reported. The word "racist" in the headline is an editorial characterization, not a checkable fact, and is not graded here.

What Vance actually claimed

Video confirms Vance said: "Has anybody seen the movie Gangs of New York? ... You know that when you have these massive ethnic enclaves in our country, it can lead to higher crime rates." This is accurately quoted by the article and matches the contemporaneous video (via @Acyn on X, Aug. 16, 2024).

Does the evidence support 'ethnic enclaves lead to higher crime'? We checked it two ways

1. Direct check against this warehouse's own data. census.acs_nativity (the table built for exactly this join) returned a schema defect on every query this session (column-count mismatch, reported as a defect), so we used the working alternative, census.acs_income_by_nativity, joined to crime.cde_offenses. The query excluded only the single synthetic 'national' aggregate row that crime.cde_offenses carries alongside its 51 real state_abbr + DC rows (state_abbr <> 'national' / NOT IN ('national')) — that row is a US-wide total, not a state, and including it would double count the country against itself; no state or DC row was dropped. Across all 50 states + DC for 2022, correlating each state's foreign-born household share against its annual violent-crime rate (offenses per 100k) gives r = 0.04, n = 51 — essentially no linear relationship, with or without that national row (it cannot be included, since it has no foreign-born-share counterpart to pair with). This is a cross-sectional, state-level correlation with no covariates adjusted for, so it cannot rule out a real effect at finer grain or after adjustment for other factors, but it flatly does not show the 'higher crime' pattern Vance asserted.

2. Independent literature. The American Immigration Council (Oct. 2024) ran a more rigorous version of the same test — a beta regression of total crime rate on immigrant population share across all 50 states, 2017–2022 — and reports no statistically significant correlation, matching what the query above found for 2022 alone. Foundational criminology research goes further and finds the opposite sign: Sampson, Morenoff & Raudenbush (2005) and Graif & Sampson (2009), working from Chicago neighborhood data, found immigrant concentration associated with LOWER violence net of neighborhood disadvantage (the 'immigrant revitalization' effect); Han & Piquero (2022, Journal of Research in Crime and Delinquency) and Kubrin & Hipp (2025) reach the same conclusion in later, broader samples.

No mainstream peer-reviewed research was found supporting Vance's specific claim that ethnic enclaves cause higher crime rates.

Are immigrants less likely to commit crime than native-born Americans?

Yes, and by a wide, consistently replicated margin:

  • Abramitzky, Boustan, Pérez, Torres & Jácome (2024, NBER working paper w31440 / forthcoming American Economic Review: Insights), using 150 years of U.S. census data (1850–2020), found immigrants have never been incarcerated at a higher rate than the U.S.-born since 1870, and are about 60% less likely to be incarcerated as of 2020 (30% less likely relative to U.S.-born whites specifically).
  • Cato Institute / Nowrasteh analysis of Texas DPS arrest and conviction data (the only state that tracks arrestees' immigration status): illegal immigrants had a 2016 murder-conviction rate of 1.8 per 100,000 versus 3.2 for native-born Americans and 0.9 for legal immigrants; a separate 2020 Cato paper put the illegal-immigrant conviction rate in Texas 45% below the rate for native-born Texans.
  • Multiple additional studies (Wikipedia's sourced summary of the literature, American Immigration Council) reach the same conclusion using different data sources and periods, describing this as a 'widespread scholarly consensus.'
