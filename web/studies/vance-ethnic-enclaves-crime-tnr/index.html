Vance did say "ethnic enclaves" cause higher crime rates — his central factual premise is contradicted by the research; other page items check out on attribution but not on exact figures

Validation of newrepublic.com/post/184971 (Aug 16, 2024) — a multi-item live-blog page

Summary

The core claim of the article — that J.D. Vance, defending remarks about historic immigrant 'ethnic enclaves,' told reporters on Aug 16, 2024 that such enclaves 'can lead to higher crime rates,' citing the film Gangs of New York — is accurately quoted, confirmed by contemporaneous video. The article's rebuttal — that immigrants actually commit crime at lower rates than native-born Americans — is well supported by a large, convergent body of criminology and economics research (NBER, Cato, Northwestern-covered studies), though this corpus's own FBI crime tables carry no offender-nativity field and so cannot recompute the figure directly; the verification rests on the cited external literature. The article's incidental detail that the film's villain is based on a real anti-immigrant nativist gang leader is also correct. Two secondary items bundled on the same live-blog page — Harris's 2020 bail-fund tweet and named fake electors' 2024 donations to Trump — are directionally accurate but the article's precise as-of-date dollar figures for the donations could not be exactly reproduced from this corpus's FEC data, which totals the full 2024 cycle rather than a point-in-time snapshot.

Claim 1 — the quote and its framing

Video from the press conference (widely circulated, e.g. the Acyn post on X) confirms Vance said: 'Has anybody seen the movie Gangs of New York? ... when you have these massive ethnic enclaves in our country, it can lead to higher crime rates.' The article's transcription is accurate. Whether this is 'racist' is a normative/opinion judgment the article makes, not a factual claim that can be validated true or false — that framing is left to the reader.

Claim 2 — is the underlying premise correct?

Vance's claim implies that concentrations of immigrants ('ethnic enclaves') raise crime. Research consistently finds the opposite direction for immigrants generally: an NBER analysis of 150 years of census/incarceration data found immigrant incarceration rates staying well below native-born rates throughout, with 2019 rates near 1,500 per 100,000 for immigrant men versus roughly 3,000 per 100,000 for U.S.-born men (see dashboard). A widely covered 2024 study (Michael Light and colleagues, covered by Northwestern) put immigrants roughly 60% less likely to be incarcerated in 2020 than the U.S.-born. Cato's analysis of Texas arrest data found undocumented immigrants arrested at under half the native-born rate for violent and drug offenses and a quarter the rate for property crimes. This corpus's own crime.* (FBI Uniform Crime Reporting) tables do not carry an offender-nativity/immigration-status field, so this could not be independently recomputed inside the warehouse — the verification here rests on the cited peer-reviewed and government-data-based external research, which is unambiguous on direction even though point estimates vary by study and era.

Claim 3 — the film reference undercuts itself

The article notes that the main antagonist of Gangs of New York, 'Bill the Butcher,' is based on William Poole, a real 19th-century nativist (anti-immigrant) gang leader — meaning Vance's own cited example was an anti-immigrant crime figure, not an immigrant one. This detail is historically accurate and the article's point stands.

Secondary items: donations and the bail-fund tweet

Harris/bail fund: Harris did tweet support for the Minnesota Freedom Fund during the 2020 protests; the fund posts bail broadly and Harris had no personal, direct role in bailing out specific rioters, so the article's characterization of Vance's claim as an overstatement holds up against contemporaneous reporting (Washington Post).

Fake-elector donations: Querying this corpus's FEC individual_contributions table confirms Tyler Bowyer, Meshawn Maddock, and David Hanna are all active 2024-cycle donors, and Bowyer's contributions to Trump National Committee JFC, Inc. total $2,714 for the full cycle in this data. The article's $645/$1,800/$25,000 figures were a point-in-time snapshot as of mid-August 2024 sourced from The Guardian's own FEC pull; this corpus's totals reflect the full cycle as ingested, a different vintage, so an exact line-for-line match was not obtained. The existence and direction of the donations are confirmed; the specific historical dollar amounts are not independently re-derived here.
