Eleven officeholders in scope were quoted in the day's politics coverage from NPR and PBS NewsHour. Most of the statements came from an Associated Press story on members of Congress reconsidering tariffs after the November election.

How to read this page
Supported
Warehouse or external primary data confirms the statement.
Unsettled
Evidence was found, but it does not decide the claim: it turns on how a law or a loose term is read, or the evidence does not speak to the specific point.
Unsupported
No evidence was found in the AskAmerica warehouse or any external factual source. This does not mean the statement is false.
Contradicted
A primary source, read directly, says something different on the specific point, but some question remains open: an inquiry is unfinished, or the source could be using a different definition or period.
False
A settled official record, read directly, rules the statement out: a published measurement, no ordinary reading of the statement that fits it, and nothing pending that could change it.
Citejacked
The speaker cites a source that does not say what the speaker claims.
Premised prediction
An opinion or prediction that rests on a factual premise. The label rates the premise only, never the opinion or prediction built on it.
Counter-statement
A link to a statement by another speaker, or to an official document, that directly addresses the same point and says otherwise. A link is not a finding about which is right, and a rating shown beside a counter-statement rates that statement only.
Said more than once
When a speaker makes the same claim several times in similar words, it is listed once, with the other wordings and where each was said shown beneath it. Versions that differ on a figure, date or cause stay separate.
Contradicts own statement
A link between two factual statements by the same speaker that cannot both be true on the same specific point. A link is not a finding about which one is right; each statement keeps its own label.
Not checkable
An opinion, prediction, promise or personal attack. No data can settle it.

Every statement is listed, whatever its kind. There are no scores. Statements are grouped two levels deep, by speaker and then topic or by topic and then speaker, and every group is collapsed. A topic with a single statement is listed under Other. The counts beside a name cover only that person's statements on this page, and are not a rating of the person. Statements that could not be checked are folded under each speaker. Selection: Every statement by an in-scope officeholder quoted in a story published on Nov. 27 or 28, 2026 in the NPR Politics and PBS NewsHour Politics feeds, as read on Nov. 28, less the stories earlier pages already used, plus every transcript from the fixed transcript sources dated in that window (none was). PBS NewsHour carries Associated Press reporting. One listed NPR story (Week in Politics, Nov. 28) returned no readable text and was not used. Transcripts from Nov. 22 to 25 were searched only to confirm quotes; none held any of the day's quotes. Reporters' and commentators' paraphrases of opinions, an AI-generated voice in a campaign ad, and statements by people outside the offices in scope (including a governor-elect and state attorneys general) were left out. Statements are worded as the outlet reported them.

SupportedFactual claim

Said to AP about Congress's past delegation of tariff authority to the executive branch.

So they've given authorities to the executive branch over a period of years.

The U.S. Code, read in AskAmerica's law.usc_sections table, gives the President or the U.S. Trade Representative power over imports under statutes enacted in different years: Section 232 (19 U.S.C. 1862, enacted 1962) lets the President act to "adjust the imports" of an article, Section 301 (19 U.S.C. 2411, enacted 1974) assigns actions to the Trade Representative, and the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (50 U.S.C. 1702, enacted 1977) lets the President regulate or prohibit importation.

How this was tested True if statutes passed by Congress in different years give the executive branch authority to impose or adjust tariffs (for example Section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962, Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974 or the International Emergency Economic Powers Act of 1977). False if no statute delegates tariff authority to the executive. Cannot be determined if the statutory record does not show who holds the authority.

Evidence: AskAmerica data law.usc_sections (19 U.S.C. 1862, 19 U.S.C. 2411, 50 U.S.C. 1702: headings and source credits) , AskAmerica data law.usc_sections (19 U.S.C. 1862(c): action by the President to adjust imports) 

SELECT title_number, section_number, heading, source_credit, substr(section_text,1,300) AS t FROM law.usc_sections WHERE (title_number=19 AND section_number IN ('1862','2411')) OR (title_number=50 AND section_number='1702')
SELECT substr(section_text, position('(c)' IN section_text), 700) AS t FROM law.usc_sections WHERE title_number=19 AND section_number='1862'
