FEMA hurricane declarations in coastal states show a noisy, statistically weak upward drift since 2010; homeowners premium growth in high-risk coastal areas far outpaces that trend and is driven more by loss severity and risk pricing than by storm counts

FEMA/NOAA disaster data (askamerica, 2010-2025) and GAO-26-107867 (Feb 2026) homeowners insurance report

Summary

Frequency: Within the only window this corpus loads for FEMA disaster declarations (2010-2025), hurricane/tropical-cyclone declarations in 17 Atlantic and Gulf coastal states show a positive but statistically weak drift — a regression slope of about +0.35 declarations/year with r² = 0.05 (r = 0.22, n = 16 years). That is not a reliable rising trend; it is dominated by a handful of very active years (2021: 30 declarations; 2017: 19; 2011: 19) sitting alongside near-zero years (2014-2015: 0; 2016: 1). Broader NOAA climatology (outside this corpus, summarized in Step 2) shows basin-wide major-hurricane activity has trended up since the 1970s-80s, but U.S. landfalling major-hurricane counts were actually lower in the 2010s than the 2000s — landfall frequency, which is what drives U.S. disaster declarations and insured losses, is considerably more variable than basin-wide counts.

Insurance: Nationally, real (inflation-adjusted) homeowners premiums rose only about 3% from 2019-2024 (GAO-26-107867, Feb 2026). But premiums in southern coastal, high-wind-risk areas rose 25% or more over the same window, with parts of North Carolina and Texas exceeding 50% in real terms, and Florida renewal premiums up roughly 68% from May 2021 to May 2023 in other industry analyses. So premium growth in coastal states is running many multiples of the national rate and far outpacing any measurable rise in disaster count. GAO's own econometric model attributes this mainly to the LEVEL of wind risk (homes in high-wind-risk areas carry premiums ~58% higher than medium-risk homes) and to loss severity and state regulatory/approval-speed differences — not simply to a rising count of declared disasters. Consistent with that, this corpus's own NFIP paid-claims series for the same coastal states is extremely lumpy and driven by a few catastrophic-severity years (2012 Sandy, 2017 Harvey/Irma, 2022 Ian, 2024 Helene/Milton) rather than tracking the declaration-count series (correlation between the two series here is only r ≈ 0.29).

Step 1 — Has hurricane disaster frequency in coastal states actually been rising?

Query: FEMA disasters.disaster_declarations, counting distinct disaster_number per state with incident_type in Hurricane / Tropical Storm / Tropical Depression / Typhoon, summed across 17 Atlantic and Gulf coastal states (TX, LA, MS, AL, FL, GA, SC, NC, VA, MD, DE, NJ, NY, CT, RI, MA, ME). Because FEMA issues a separate disaster number per affected state, one storm hitting five states counts five times — this measures state-level declaration frequency, not distinct-storm count.

An r² of 0.05 means year explains under 5% of the variance in this series — the year-to-year swings (from 0 declarations to 30) dwarf any linear trend. This corpus's declared coverage window for FEMA disaster declarations begins in 2010 (confirmed via data_coverage: observed 2010-2026, contiguous, no interior gaps), so a longer pre-2010 baseline could not be computed from this table.

To put the 2010-2025 window in a longer historical context, I searched the open literature (not this corpus, cited separately below): NOAA/WeatherTiger summaries indicate Atlantic basin-wide Category 3-5 hurricane frequency rose from roughly 1.6/year in the 1970s-80s to roughly 3.1-3.8/year in the 2000s-2010s, consistent with a real long-run upward trend in basin-wide major-hurricane activity. But U.S. landfalling major hurricanes specifically were fewer in the 2010s (about 3) than in the 2000s (about 7) — landfall, which is what actually triggers FEMA declarations and insurance losses in a given state, does not move in lockstep with basin-wide counts. That reconciles with the weak, noisy trend measured directly in the FEMA data above: basin activity is up over the long run, but the year-to-year and even decade-to-decade pattern of which coastal states actually get hit is highly variable, so 'has frequency actually been rising' is best answered as: modestly and noisily at best over the measurable 2010-2025 window in this corpus, against a backdrop of a real but landfall-inconsistent long-run basin-wide increase.

Step 2 — Does homeowners insurance premium growth track that frequency?

This corpus (askamerica) does not carry a homeowners-insurance premium table (search_catalog returned no match for 'homeowners'; NAIC/state-level homeowners premium data is not ingested). I went to the primary published source instead: GAO-26-107867, Homeowners Insurance: Premiums Generally Tracked Inflation but Rose More in Disaster-Prone Areas (U.S. Government Accountability Office, published February 27, 2026, to the Senate Banking Committee). I fetched and read the report's own HTML text directly (files.gao.gov/reports/GAO-26-107867/index.html) rather than relying on a secondary summary.

GAO's headline finding: the average U.S. homeowners premium rose about 3% in real (inflation-adjusted) terms from 2019 to 2024. But premiums in parts of certain states — particularly southern coastal areas at high risk of wind damage — rose 25% or more in real terms over the same five years, with parts of North Carolina and Texas exceeding 50%. GAO's own econometric model (built on First Street Technology's 2019-2024 premium estimates) found homes in high-wind-risk areas carry premiums about 58% higher than similar homes in medium-wind-risk areas, versus only an 8% premium increase moving from medium to high wildfire risk — wind risk (which correlates with hurricane exposure) is the dominant natural-disaster driver of premium levels in their model, well ahead of a simple event count.

Two independent secondary sources corroborate the coastal-specific severity of the increase: Policygenius-based reporting found Florida renewal premiums up about 68% from May 2021 to May 2023, and about 35% in the single May 2022-May 2023 window, with Louisiana up 27% in that window. These are geography-specific spikes far in excess of the roughly 3% national real increase GAO measured.

Does growth TRACK frequency, specifically? The evidence says no, not closely. Three separate signals point the same direction: (1) FEMA declaration counts for coastal states (Step 1) show a weak, noisy trend (r²=0.05) with no monotonic year-over-year rise; (2) this corpus's own NFIP paid-claims series for the identical 17 coastal states is dominated by a small number of catastrophic-severity years rather than declaration count — correlating the two series directly (both queried here) gives r ≈ 0.29, a weak relationship; (3) GAO's own regression attributes premium growth mainly to risk LEVEL (wind-risk rating) and to state regulatory approval speed, reinsurance/replacement-cost inflation, and loss severity, not to a rising count of declared disasters. In short: premiums are rising fastest exactly where wind risk is rated highest, but that rise is running several multiples of any measured rise in hurricane-disaster frequency — it looks driven by the cost and severity of the disasters that do occur (rebuilding costs, reinsurance, litigation, regulatory lag) more than by how often they occur.

Data and methodology notes

  • Coastal-state definition: the 17 states with an Atlantic or Gulf of Mexico coastline (TX through ME); Pacific and Great Lakes states were excluded since hurricane/tropical-cyclone risk there is negligible to nonexistent.
  • FEMA declaration counts are STATE-level declaration counts (one storm can generate several state-level declarations), not a distinct-storm count — this is the standard FEMA disaster-declaration grain and is the correct measure for 'declared disaster frequency,' but it is not the same as a meteorological storm count.
  • The FEMA disaster_declarations table's coverage in this corpus begins in 2010; no earlier FEMA declaration history was queryable here, which limits how far back the frequency trend could be measured directly. The longer-run NOAA context in Step 1 comes from open-web sources, not this corpus, and is labeled as such.
  • NFIP paid-claims figures are nominal (not inflation-adjusted) building+contents payouts; the deprecation warning on this table (OpenFEMA freezing this endpoint after 2026-10-15 in favor of a v3 dataset) does not affect the historical rows used here.
  • No homeowners-insurance premium time series exists in this corpus; the premium figures throughout are sourced externally to GAO-26-107867 and secondary industry reporting, clearly separated from the askamerica-sourced hurricane/NFIP figures.

WITH d AS (
  SELECT dd."year"::INT AS yr, COUNT(DISTINCT dd.disaster_number) AS n
  FROM disasters.disaster_declarations dd
  JOIN geo.state_ref sr ON dd.state_fips = sr.state_fips
  WHERE dd.incident_type IN ('Hurricane','Tropical Storm','Tropical Depression','Typhoon')
    AND sr.state_abbr IN ('TX','LA','MS','AL','FL','GA','SC','NC','VA','MD','DE','NJ','NY','CT','RI','MA','ME')
  GROUP BY dd."year"
)
SELECT regr_slope(n, yr) AS slope, regr_intercept(n, yr) AS intercept, corr(n, yr) AS r, regr_r2(n, yr) AS r2, count(*) AS n_years
FROM d WHERE yr BETWEEN 2010 AND 2025
query — 16 rows — 38079 ms
SELECT nc."year"::INT AS yr, SUM(nc.amount_paid_building + nc.amount_paid_contents) AS total_paid

  AND nc."year"::INT BETWEEN 2010 AND 2025
GROUP BY nc."year"
ORDER BY yr
