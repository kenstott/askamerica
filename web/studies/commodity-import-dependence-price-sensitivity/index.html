Import dependence alone doesn't predict volatility — it's China's export weaponization that does

USGS import-reliance data + industry price reporting, 2020-2026

Summary

The US is 100% net-import-reliant on roughly 10-16 nonfuel mineral commodities (USGS Mineral Commodity Summaries, 2025/2026), and these are NOT uniformly volatile. Price moves range from extreme (gallium +661% since 2020, antimony roughly +370% in just two years, 2023-2025) to modest (natural graphite +~47% since 2022) to flat-or-falling (fluorspar, manganese). The single biggest driver of which import-dependent commodities actually see extreme price spikes is not import reliance itself but whether China (the dominant or sole supplier) has deliberately weaponized export controls against the US specifically. Gallium and antimony, both explicitly targeted by Chinese export bans aimed at the US in 2024, are the two commodities with by far the largest price moves in this set. Commodities that are equally import-dependent but multi-sourced (manganese, sourced from Gabon/South Africa/Australia) or not targeted with a US-specific ban (graphite, fluorspar, though still China-dominant) show far smaller, sometimes negative, price movements over the same window.

Which commodities is the US 100% import-reliant on

Per USGS Mineral Commodity Summaries, the US is 100% net-import-reliant (imports cover essentially all apparent consumption) for roughly 10-16 mineral commodities depending on the vintage. A stable core across 2023-2026 editions includes: arsenic, fluorspar, gallium, natural graphite, indium, manganese, niobium, scandium, tantalum, and yttrium — with rare earth compounds/metals close behind at ~95%, and antimony, chromium, and titanium metal in the 80-95% range. The 2026 edition reports the US was more than 50% import-reliant for 54 nonfuel mineral commodities and 100% reliant for 16 of those (an increase from 12-13 in the 2025 edition). China is the single largest supplier for many of the highest-reliance items (gallium, graphite, tantalum, yttrium, arsenic), which concentrates both the supply risk and the exposure to any single country's trade-policy decisions.

Why import dependence and price volatility diverge

The pattern across this set of commodities suggests import reliance is a necessary but not sufficient condition for extreme price volatility. What distinguishes gallium and antimony (the two biggest movers) from graphite, fluorspar, and manganese (the smaller movers) is that China explicitly imposed export licensing and then a targeted ban on shipments to the United States for gallium and antimony in 2024, as a direct response to US semiconductor and chip-equipment export controls. That is a deliberate trade-policy shock layered on top of underlying import dependence — not dependence alone. Rare earths sit in between: China restricted a narrower set of 7 elements in April 2025 rather than a blanket US ban, and prices there show large multi-year gains but also sharp intra-year reversals (both a 30% drop and a 40%+ surge within 2025), reflecting a market still being actively managed via licensing rather than an outright embargo. Manganese, despite being 100% import-reliant, is comparatively insulated because its supply is spread across Gabon, South Africa, and Australia rather than concentrated in China, so no single country's export policy can move its price the way China's can for gallium or antimony.
