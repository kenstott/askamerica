Speculative positioning mostly follows the market narrative, not the physical supply picture -- and where it leads, it does so weakly

CFTC managed-money WTI positioning vs. EIA crude inventories, weekly 2010-2026; literature review of Kilian-Murphy, CFTC staff studies, and Granger-causality research

Summary

Mostly after, and only loosely even then. Both the published research and an original weekly correlation test built from CFTC and EIA data agree: speculative (managed-money) positioning in crude oil futures does not move meaningfully ahead of the physical supply picture. Contemporaneous and short-lag correlations between week-over-week changes in managed-money net WTI positioning and week-over-week changes in U.S. crude inventories are essentially zero (r ≈ 0.01-0.04 across ±4 weeks, n=881 weekly observations, 2010-2026). The only signal that emerges at all is a weak, statistically fragile positive correlation (r ≈ 0.13) between a positioning shift and an inventory move 4-8 weeks LATER -- i.e. positioning very mildly leads a slower-moving inventory trend rather than the reverse -- but this uses overlapping rolling windows that inflate apparent significance, and it disappears going the other direction (inventory changes do not predict later positioning any better). The published literature is more decisive: several studies using Granger-causality tests find that price changes precede speculative position changes, not the other way around, and Kilian & Murphy's (2014) structural model finds inventories move mainly on genuine flow-supply/demand and precautionary-storage shocks, with only a modest, mostly reactive role for a distinct 'speculative demand' shock. The practical picture: speculators are largely trend-followers reacting to prices (which themselves reflect the market's evolving read of the supply picture) rather than analysts front-running physical fundamentals with foresight the physical data hasn't yet shown.

What the published research says

The most-cited structural analysis of this question is Kilian and Murphy (2014), which builds a VAR of the global oil market that separately identifies flow-supply shocks, flow-demand shocks, and a 'speculative demand' shock proxied through above-ground inventories. Their headline finding: the 2003-2008 price surge cannot be explained by unexpected supply shortfalls OR by a speculative-inventory-hoarding story, because inventories did not rise the way a speculative run-up would require -- the speculative component was small relative to the flow-fundamentals-driven component. Follow-on work (Kim et al. 2020; the Dallas Fed working paper) refines this to say inventories serve a dual role -- physical buffering AND facilitating speculative trades -- but generally corroborates that inventories respond to the totality of shocks rather than being driven ahead of time by speculative flows.

On the positioning side specifically, the CFTC's own staff study of speculators in the crude oil futures market and subsequent Granger-causality literature (summarized in the CRS report on hedge fund speculation and oil prices and the MDPI literature review of the oil futures/spot lead-lag relationship) generally finds little evidence that non-commercial (speculative) position changes Granger-cause price changes; instead, price changes tend to precede position changes. Since prices themselves are the market's fastest-updating read on the supply/demand balance, this points to speculators reacting to information the market has already priced, rather than anticipating physical data releases (like the weekly EIA inventory report) ahead of time. Contemporary market commentary (Kemp, 2025; CNBC, 2026) reinforces this qualitatively: large commodity trading advisors (CTAs) are described as trend-following, black-box momentum traders reacting to realized price moves, and 'lopsided positioning often precedes a sharp REVERSAL' in price -- a pattern consistent with positioning as a lagging, extrapolative signal rather than a forward-looking one on fundamentals.

What the direct data test shows

To check this against real data rather than relying on the literature alone, weekly managed-money net positioning in NYMEX WTI (CFTC Disaggregated COT, contract code 067411, long minus short) was aligned against weekly U.S. crude oil ending stocks (EIA series WCRSTUS1), 2010-2026, 881 aligned weekly observations (COT reports Tuesday; the matching EIA stocks report is the following Friday).

At the raw weekly grain, correlating the week-over-week change in net managed-money positioning against the week-over-week change in inventories at lags from -4 to +4 weeks produced correlations all below |r|=0.04 -- no meaningful relationship in either direction at the weekly-noise level. Repeating the test on smoother, economically more meaningful 8-week rolling changes (to filter weekly EIA-print noise and look at trend-level co-movement) surfaced a modest positive correlation of the positioning change leading the inventory change by 4-8 weeks (r=0.124 to r=0.133), while the reverse direction (inventory change leading a LATER positioning change) was flat to slightly negative (r=0.0004 down to r=-0.088 at 12 weeks). Read plainly, this is a weak signal that positioning shifts show up a little before slower-moving inventory trends turn, and essentially no signal that inventory trends predict positioning. But two things temper that finding: the 8-week rolling-difference construction uses heavily overlapping windows, which mechanically inflates apparent correlation and understates the true degrees of freedom, and no covariate (price, macro news, seasonal refinery patterns) was controlled for, so a shared third driver -- most obviously, a price/news shock that moves both series without either causing the other -- cannot be ruled out with a bivariate correlation alone.

Bottom line and caveats

Putting the two pieces together: the balance of evidence, both from the literature's Granger-causality tests and from this original correlation test, points toward speculative positioning changing AFTER (or at best only very weakly and unreliably ahead of) the physical supply picture, and mostly in reaction to price moves rather than to physical fundamentals data directly. There is no strong, robust evidence that speculators systematically front-run EIA inventory reports or turning points in physical supply. The one positive signal found here (r≈0.13, positioning leading inventory trend by 4-8 weeks) is real in the data but too weak and too methodologically fragile (overlapping windows, no confound controls, and it is a correlation rather than a validated causal estimate) to describe as speculators anticipating supply changes with real foresight -- it is much more consistent with both series drifting with a shared, slower-moving market narrative (e.g., an emerging OPEC+ supply decision or demand outlook) than with speculators reading the physical tape ahead of the physical data itself.

Coverage note: the direct empirical test was run only for WTI crude oil against EIA crude stocks; natural gas COT and storage data exist in this warehouse but were not run through the identical lead-lag test in this pass, so the quantitative correlation figures above are crude-oil-specific even though the cited literature (CFTC staff study, Buyuksahin et al.) speaks to energy futures speculation more broadly.

What This Report Does Not Answer

  • Address 'energy futures' broadly, not only crude oil: The direct empirical lead-lag test was run only on WTI crude oil (CFTC managed-money net positions vs EIA crude stocks). Natural gas storage/COT data exist in the corpus but were not run through the same test in this pass; the literature review (CFTC staff study, Buyuksahin/Haigh) covers energy futures more generally including natural gas, so the qualitative conclusion is not crude-only, but the quantitative lead-lag numbers are WTI-specific.
