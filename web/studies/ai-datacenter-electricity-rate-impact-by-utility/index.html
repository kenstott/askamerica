Data-center growth has not clearly raised household electricity bills so far, but PJM capacity costs are the credible exception

EIA state retail prices 2019-2024 (energy.eia_electricity_prices); PJM BRA prices; LBNL/Brattle, E3, IMM via web

Summary

Mostly no so far, with one real exception. Across 50 states, 2019-2024, states whose commercial electricity sales surged (the best available proxy for data-center load) saw residential prices rise less on average (18.5%, n=12 states with 15%+ growth) than other states (23.7%, n=38); the correlation is -0.26. Virginia, the largest data-center market, saw residential prices rise 19.4% against 26.7% nationally. But in the PJM grid region (Virginia, Maryland, Ohio, Pennsylvania, New Jersey), data-center demand is a documented driver of sharply higher capacity auction prices, which flow into bills from 2025 on, and the data here mostly end in 2024. Evidence is descriptive, not causal.

What the data show

  • Residential price change 2019-2024: US +26.7% (13.01 to 16.48 cents/kWh). Virginia +19.4% while its commercial sales grew 45.8%. Texas +27.0%, Georgia +19.7%, Arizona +20.0%, Oregon +33.5%, Ohio +29.2%.
  • Large price rises happened where load was flat or falling (California +66.9%, New York +36.2%, Maryland +36.1%, Massachusetts +33.9%), pointing to wires, wildfire, storm and fuel costs, not data centers.
  • PJM capacity clearing price (RTO): $28.92/MW-day (2024/25), $269.92 (2025/26), $329.17 (2026/27). Residential bills only start reflecting this in 2025-26, after my price window.

What others find

The LBNL/Brattle 2026 update reports that high-load-growth states generally saw real prices fall 2019-2025 and that data-center load did not alter that conclusion, while cautioning that residential results are less clear and the pattern may not hold as growth accelerates (read via search excerpts, not the full PDF). The PJM market monitor attributes 63% of the 2025/26 capacity price increase (about $9.3 billion) to data centers; Maryland's People's Counsel puts capacity at roughly $10/month on a typical bill; E3 (published by the Data Center Coalition, an industry group) found no historical evidence of residential cost increases but attributes about half of PJM's capacity price rise to data-center load. These are secondary summaries I did not verify against primary documents.

Caveats

The com_sales_pct column (2024 vs 2019 commercial sales growth) was flagged by the engine as a broken_field (out_of_domain, 22 rows with negative values). Those values are genuine declines in commercial sales, not corrupt data, so the flag appears to be a false alarm triggered by the column name; I treated them as valid, but the proxy itself is imperfect because commercial sales include non-data-center load. State grain is much coarser than the question (a single campus affects one utility's territory). Residential price is cents/kWh, not total bills. No causal design; 12 high-growth states is a small sample. Excluded DC and the US aggregate rows (DC has little load growth). The warehouse had transient timeouts before succeeding.

What This Report Does Not Answer

  • Because of the data center (causal): Not identifiable from state aggregates; reported literature (LBNL/Brattle, E3, IMM) instead, and said so.
  • Local/utility-level evidence: No utility-level or data-center site data loaded; state is coarser than the question.
