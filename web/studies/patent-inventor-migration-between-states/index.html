California, Massachusetts, Texas and Washington are gaining inventors fastest; Vermont, West Virginia and Maine are losing them

USPTO/PatentsView distinct inventors by state of residence, 2011-2013 vs. 2022-2024

Summary

Using USPTO/PatentsView data on where a patent's inventors actually live (inventor residence, not corporate assignee location), the number of distinct US-resident inventors receiving at least one patent grant rose from about 371,000 in 2011-2013 to about 455,000 in 2022-2024 — national growth of roughly 22%. Growth is very uneven across states. California is the dominant gainer in absolute terms (+37,776 inventors, +42% — more than the next nine gaining states combined), with Massachusetts, Texas, Washington, Florida, Michigan, Virginia and Colorado all posting solid absolute and percentage gains. A small group of states is shrinking: Vermont (-38%), West Virginia (-28%), Maine (-17%), Delaware (-10%) and Alaska (-10%) all have fewer resident inventors patenting in 2022-2024 than in 2011-2013, and New Jersey, Kansas, Kentucky and Mississippi are essentially flat to slightly down.

Method

Inventor location is measured at the inventor level, not the assignee/corporate level — this matters because an assignee-based patent table would misleadingly show Delaware as a major patent hub due to corporate registration, not invention activity (a documented distortion in this data). Using patents.patent_inventors (one row per patent-inventor pair, PatentsView's disambiguated inventor_id) joined to patents.patent_grants for grant year and to patents.patent_locations for state FIPS (filtered to country_code='US'), we counted the number of distinct inventors with at least one US patent grant in an early three-year window (2011-2013) versus a recent three-year window (2022-2024), by state. Three-year windows smooth single-year noise; distinct-inventor counts (not patent counts) avoid double-counting an inventor who filed several patents in the same window. The patents schema's observed coverage is complete for grant years 2010-2025, so both windows sit fully inside the loaded data.

New Jersey is the only large-base state on this list — historically a major pharma/telecom R&D hub (Bell Labs, big pharma) — and its -6.4% decline (-898 inventors) against a national backdrop of +22.5% growth represents a real relative loss of standing, not just noise. The other losing states (Vermont, West Virginia, Maine, Delaware, Alaska, Kansas, Kentucky, Mississippi) all have small inventor bases, so their percentage declines are more sensitive to a handful of individual inventors' filing activity.

Largest absolute gains

Ranked by the raw number of additional distinct inventors (not percentage), the picture is dominated by already-large tech/R&D hubs: California (+37,776), Massachusetts (+7,894), Texas (+7,866), Washington (+7,619), Florida (+3,062), Michigan (+3,039), Virginia (+2,549), Colorado (+2,501), Georgia (+1,827) and New York (+682, the smallest gain among large states relative to its size). California alone accounts for roughly 45% of the entire national increase in inventor count over this period — the growth in US inventive activity is heavily concentrated in a small number of already-dominant states rather than broad-based across the country, consistent with published research (NSF/NCSES Science & Engineering Indicators, Congressional Research Service) documenting continuing geographic concentration of US patenting in California, particularly Silicon Valley/Santa Clara County.

Caveats

  • This measures inventors credited on a granted US patent by their disambiguated PatentsView residence, not total R&D employment or population-adjusted inventor density — a large state's raw gain partly reflects its large population/economy, not necessarily a faster per-capita innovation rate.
  • Windows are three-year averages (2011-2013 and 2022-2024) chosen to smooth annual filing/grant-timing noise while remaining well inside the patents schema's fully-loaded 2010-2025 coverage window; a different window choice (e.g. single years, or a longer 2010-2015 vs. 2020-2025 span) could shift rankings for closely-bunched states, though the largest gainers (CA, MA, TX, WA) and losers (VT, WV, ME) are far enough from the pack to be robust to that choice.
  • DC and several very small states (Arkansas, Montana, North Dakota) show the largest percentage swings but off small absolute bases — treat those percentage rankings as suggestive rather than firm.
  • PatentsView's inventor disambiguation algorithm is imperfect and can occasionally split or merge real inventors' identities; this affects year-to-year counts modestly but is not expected to bias state-level relative comparisons.

    Show SQL
    WITH inv AS (SELECT pg.grant_year, pl.state_fips, pi.inventor_id FROM patents.patent_grants pg JOIN patents.patent_inventors pi ON pg.patent_id = pi.patent_id JOIN patents.patent_locations pl ON pi.location_id = pl.location_id WHERE pg.grant_year IN (2011,2012,2013,2022,2023,2024) AND pl.country_code = 'US' AND pl.state_fips IS NOT NULL) SELECT state_fips, COUNT(DISTINCT CASE WHEN grant_year IN (2011,2012,2013) THEN inventor_id END) AS inventors_early, COUNT(DISTINCT CASE WHEN grant_year IN (2022,2023,2024) THEN inventor_id END) AS inventors_recent FROM inv GROUP BY state_fips
