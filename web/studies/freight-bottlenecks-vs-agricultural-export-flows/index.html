The Corn Belt's river system to the Gulf, and the Pacific Northwest's rail/dam corridor, are America's tightest farm-export bottlenecks — soybeans are the most exposed crop

Compiled from USDA AMS Grain Transportation Report, USDA inland-waterways studies, American Farm Bureau, U.S. Wheat Associates, and 2026 rail-tariff filings

Summary

Two US farm regions face the worst transport constraints getting crops to export: the Corn Belt / Lower Mississippi River barge corridor (Iowa, Illinois, Missouri, Arkansas, Louisiana down to the Gulf), and the Pacific Northwest wheat corridor built on the Columbia-Snake River System and BNSF rail out of the Northern Plains (North Dakota, Montana, Minnesota, South Dakota). The Mississippi corridor is squeezed by a fourth straight year (2022-2025) of historically low water that forces smaller tows and higher freight rates, layered on top of locks built in the 1930s that have exceeded their 50-year design life and fail unpredictably. The Northern Plains/PNW corridor is squeezed by rail rather than river: BNSF, the dominant carrier, raised 2026/27 shuttle tariffs by $150-250 per car to Pacific Northwest and Gulf/Mexico-border destinations, and its network posts some of the highest held-train counts of any US Class I railroad. Of the crops, soybeans are the single most transport-exposed — roughly 53-60% of US soybean exports move by barge down the Mississippi to Gulf terminals, a higher barge-dependence share than corn (about half) or wheat overall. Wheat's exposure is more concentrated: nearly 60% of US wheat exports move through Pacific Northwest elevators, and about 10% of all US wheat exports specifically depend on the four Lower Snake River dams, whose future is contested in the salmon-restoration debate. 1. The Mississippi River / Gulf corridor: the Corn Belt's single point of failure

Nearly 60% of all US grain exports (corn, soybeans and wheat combined) move by barge on the Mississippi River System to Gulf export elevators, carrying roughly 65 million metric tons of bulk ag product in a normal year. This is the artery for Iowa, Illinois, Missouri, Nebraska, Indiana, and the river-adjacent states of Arkansas, Mississippi, Tennessee and Louisiana.

The corridor has two compounding weaknesses:

  • Recurring low water. 2025 marked a fourth consecutive year of historically low Mississippi River levels at harvest time. The Coast Guard responded with draft and tow-width restrictions (barges limited to 10.5 feet of draft, tows to six barges wide near Memphis in September 2025). USDA's Agricultural Marketing Service recorded southbound grain barge tonnage collapsing from 2.4 million to about 502,000 short tons in roughly five weeks — a 79% drop — with soybean movements down 89%, corn down 72%, and wheat down 55% within that period. Barge freight rates from Cairo to Memphis rose 31% in the same one-month window ($19.53/ton). During the comparable 2022 low-water event, agricultural exports through Louisiana ports fell 3.9% (about $565 million) in the second half of the year.
  • Aging, unreliable locks. Most locks on the Upper Mississippi and Illinois River System were built in the 1930s and have exceeded their 50-year design life. Chronic underfunding of maintenance has produced a 'fix-as-fail' pattern, where an unscheduled lock closure for repair can last days to months. USDA's own inland-waterways study puts the resulting cost to shippers and operators above $44 million annually, with a potential impact on corn of up to $0.24 per bushel from these delays alone.

2. The Columbia-Snake River / Pacific Northwest corridor: the choke point for wheat

The Columbia Snake River System is the top wheat-export gateway in the country, moving wheat 360 miles by barge from Lewiston, Idaho, down to export elevators as far as Longview, Washington, through eight locks and dams. About 60% of total US wheat exports move through Pacific Northwest export elevators, and an estimated 10% of all US wheat exports specifically pass through the four locks and dams on the lower Snake River. A single four-barge tow on this system replaces roughly 538 semi-trucks and is the cheapest way to move the grain — which is exactly why any disruption to it is disproportionately costly. The system's chief vulnerability is not hydrology but policy: ongoing debate over removing the lower Snake River dams for salmon restoration threatens to eliminate this navigation route entirely, which wheat exporters and Washington/Idaho growers argue would force a costly shift to truck and rail.

Layered on top of the river system, wheat and soybean growers across North Dakota, Montana, Minnesota and South Dakota depend heavily on BNSF Railway to reach both PNW and Gulf/Mexico-border export positions, since they sit far from a barge-navigable river. For the 2026/27 marketing year, BNSF raised shuttle tariff rates by $150/car for most Minnesota and North Dakota origins to Pacific Northwest terminals, by $50/car for South Dakota origins, and by $200-250/car for a group of North Dakota origins moving to the Texas Gulf and US-Mexico border. BNSF also canceled a long-standing $105/car switching rate for grain unit trains at several locations, and as of late May 2026 posted the highest average held-train count (22.9) of any Class I railroad tracked in USDA's Grain Transportation Report — a direct measure of network congestion.

3. Which crops are most exposed

Soybeans are the most transport-exposed major US export crop. Roughly 53-60% of US soybean exports move by barge down the Mississippi to the Gulf — a higher dependence on this single corridor than either of the other two major grains. That concentration is why soybean basis (the local cash price relative to futures) in the Midsouth has been shown in agricultural-economics research to react most sharply to river disruptions, and why soybean barge tonnage fell hardest (89%) during the September 2025 low-water event cited above.

Corn is a close second, with roughly half of US corn exports moving via the same Mississippi barge system, and it carries the added exposure of the Panama Canal: during the 2023-2024 Panama Canal drought, the share of US corn exports shipped from Gulf Coast ports fell from 72.1% (Oct 2021) to 56.8% (Oct 2023) as shippers rerouted around canal transit restrictions, and USDA cut its 2023/24 soybean export forecast by 12% year-over-year, citing the same canal bottleneck and lost competitiveness against Brazil.

Wheat's exposure is more geographically concentrated but structurally different: rather than a hydrological risk (like Mississippi low water), its chief vulnerability is a policy risk (the Snake River dam-removal debate) layered on a rail-cost risk (BNSF tariff increases and congestion) for the Northern Plains growers who feed the PNW corridor.

Methodology and data-quality note

This report is built primarily from USDA/AMS Grain Transportation Report data, American Farm Bureau Federation market-intelligence pieces, U.S. Wheat Associates technical explainers, and a USDA inland-waterways economic-impact study, cross-checked across independent outlets (AgDaily, Wisconsin Public Radio, FreightWaves, Feed & Grain, Red River Farm Network) — a minimum of two independent organizations corroborate each quantitative claim above. This connector's own ag.nass_crop_production table was queried for 2024 state-level corn, soybean, and wheat production to quantify which producing states sit in each exposed corridor; the query returned rows for only 4-5 states each time (e.g., IL, ND, WI, GA for corn) and returned zero rows for Iowa, Minnesota, Nebraska, Indiana, Kansas, and Washington — the top corn, soybean, and wheat states, and exactly the states this question is about. data_coverage confirmed 2024 holds only 21,067 rows versus 257,641 in 2022, consistent with an incomplete backfill rather than a genuine absence of NASS data (NASS publishes complete state estimates every year). An issue was filed against the table; the production figures used in the dashboard and text instead come from USDA NASS's own published 2024 state releases as reported by CropProphet and other outlets citing NASS directly.
