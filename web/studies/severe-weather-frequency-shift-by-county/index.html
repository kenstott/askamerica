Damaging-weather frequency by county, 2010-2024: little net national change, but sharp local divergence

NOAA Storm Events Database via AskAmerica connector; observed table coverage is 2010-2026, not the full 20 years the question names

Summary

The connector's own data only reaches back to 2010, not 2005 — a genuine coverage gap disclosed below, so this answers the achievable 15-year version of the question (2010-2024) rather than the full 20 years asked. Within that window, damaging-weather-event frequency has not moved uniformly: nationally, total recorded storm events rose about 11% (62,809 in 2010 to 69,801 in 2024), but this trend is dominated by year-to-year volatility (a 79,091-event spike in 2011, a 75,593-event spike in 2023) rather than a steady climb, and independent NOAA/NCEI research attributes much of this apparent rise to improved reporting infrastructure rather than a real increase in storm activity for many event types. At the individual-county level the picture is genuinely mixed: comparing each county's own 2010-14 average to its 2020-24 average, 1,315 of 3,234 counties (40.6%) saw an increase and 1,740 (53.8%) saw a decrease, with the remainder flat — the average county's own event count actually declined slightly (11.7/yr to 11.3/yr, -3.6%). Large increases concentrate in fast-growing Sun Belt/Mountain West metro counties (Maricopa AZ, Cook IL, El Paso CO) and Eastern hail/severe-thunderstorm corridors (Chester and Berks County, PA); large decreases concentrate in Plains/Midwest counties that had unusually high severe-thunderstorm counts in the early 2010s (Aroostook ME, Polk IA, Sedgwick KS).

Step 1 — Literature: what is already known about this trend

NOAA/NCEI's Storm Events Database is the standard source for county-level severe-weather counts, and the published literature is explicit that raw event counts are not directly comparable across decades because reporting infrastructure, forecast-office emphasis, and spotter/insurance/public-reporting practices have changed substantially since 1950 — a documented, non-uniform collection process that makes trend detection for tornadoes, hail, and severe thunderstorms 'extremely difficult' from raw counts alone (NCEI, and independent analyses, e.g. Kevin Trenberth-style critiques of Storm Data reliability, arxiv.org/abs/1606.06973). Separately, NOAA's Billion-Dollar Weather and Climate Disasters program — a CPI-adjusted, damage-threshold series distinct from the raw event counts used here — documents a clear rise in the frequency of catastrophic-cost events: from an average of about 31 deaths/yr and $4.0B/yr in damages (1980-2009) to about 82 deaths/yr and $21.3B/yr (2010-2022) for severe-storm events, and 27 billion-dollar disasters totaling $182.7B in 2024 alone. That is a different metric (damage-cost threshold, national aggregate) from the raw county-level event-count comparison in this report, and the two need not move together: costs can rise from exposure growth (more people and property in harm's way) even where the underlying meteorological frequency is flat.

Step 2 — Data source, window, and a disclosed coverage gap

Query: disasters.storm_events / disasters.disasters_storm_impact_by_county (NOAA Storm Events Database, ingested by the AskAmerica connector). data_coverage was called on both tables before use: the connector's declared and observed coverage window is 2010-2026, not the 2005-2024 (or similar) twenty-year span the question implies. A primary-source fetch was attempted to fill 2005-2009 directly from NOAA's own public FTP (ncei.noaa.gov/pub/data/swdi/stormevents/csvfiles/), but the per-year files are served as gzip-compressed CSV and this session's fetch tool cannot decompress or parse binary/gzip content (confirmed: a direct fetch of the 2005 file returned only 'unrecognized binary content type, Content-Type: application/gzip' with no extractable text). The analysis below therefore covers 2010-2024 (15 years), the full span the connector actually holds, rather than fabricating the missing five years.

Step 3 — Method

Built a full county x year panel (3,234 counties from geo.counties x years 2010-2024, zero-filled for county-years with no reported event) so that a county with no reported storms in a year counts as zero rather than being silently absent. For each county, computed the average annual event count in an early window (2010-2014) and a late window (2020-2024), and the difference. 43% of all recorded events nationally (412,236 of 965,331 total 2010-2024 events) carry a NULL county_fips — these are state-wide or otherwise unresolved reports (not, on inspection, marine-zone artifacts as initially suspected, but simply un-geocoded rows) and were necessarily excluded from the per-county comparison, while still counted in the national year-by-year total shown in the dashboard. This is a material exclusion and is why the national total (+11.1%) and the matched-county-only total (-3.6%) point in different directions — the growth is concentrated in the excluded, non-county-resolved share of records, not in the matched county panel.

The standard deviation of the per-county change (7.0) is larger than the mean change itself (-0.4), meaning the national/average figure conceals wide dispersion: individual counties moved by tens of events per year in either direction while the overall population-wide average barely moved.

Step 5 — Caveats

  • Window is 15 years, not 20 — the connector's own data starts in 2010; this is disclosed rather than papered over.
  • Raw event counts are not adjusted for reporting-practice changes. The literature (NCEI, and independent Storm-Data-reliability critiques) documents that spotter networks, forecast-office emphasis, and public/insurance reporting channels have expanded over time, which can inflate apparent event counts independent of true storm frequency — a bias this analysis cannot separate out from the connector's raw counts alone.
  • 43% of national events could not be matched to a county and are excluded from the by-county comparison, which is why the county-matched trend (-3.6%) and the national raw total (+11.1%) disagree in direction — a data-quality gap, not a modeling choice.
  • Event counts are a frequency measure, not a severity/damage measure — a rising damage-cost trend (documented in NOAA's Billion-Dollar Disasters series) can coexist with flat or falling event counts if individual storms are becoming more damaging or striking more populated/valuable areas, which this report does not test.
