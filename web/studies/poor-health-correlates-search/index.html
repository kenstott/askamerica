Poorer, more rural, and more Republican-leaning states have worse health outcomes — but income drives most of the pattern

State-level age-adjusted mortality vs. poverty, income, rurality, and political lean, 2008-2017 (most recent full decade available at state grain)

Summary

Across US states, the places with worse health are consistently the poorer ones, and that single factor accounts for most of what looks, at first glance, like a rural or partisan effect. Using CDC/NCHS state age-adjusted all-cause mortality averaged over 2008-2017 (the most recent full decade available at state grain in this corpus — see Data Limits below) regressed on median household income, % of the state population in non-metro counties, and a -100 (most Republican) to +100 (most Democratic) political-lean index: a $1,000 higher median household income is associated with about 5.7 fewer deaths per 100,000 people (p<0.001, robust to dropping any single state). The 10 highest-mortality states (Mississippi, West Virginia, Alabama, Kentucky, Oklahoma, Arkansas, Louisiana, Tennessee, South Carolina, Indiana) average 18.0% poverty and lean sharply Republican; the 10 lowest-mortality states (Hawaii, California, New York, Connecticut, Minnesota, Colorado, Massachusetts, Florida, New Jersey, Arizona) average 12.8% poverty and mostly lean Democratic. Rurality and poverty are themselves correlated with income, and once income and political lean are held fixed, the independent effect of a state's rural population share is statistically indistinguishable from zero (coefficient -0.04 per point of nonmetro share, p=0.94) — rural states look sicker mainly because they are also poorer, not because rurality itself is an independent driver at the state level. The state political-lean effect is directionally consistent with the state-level literature (more Republican-leaning states have higher mortality even net of income) but is comparatively fragile: leave-one-out testing shows its significance flips above p=0.05 when Alaska, Vermont, or New Mexico is excluded, so it should be read as suggestive, not settled, at this grain and sample size (n=50).

What the published research says

This finding matches a substantial existing literature. County Health Rankings & Roadmaps research (Robert Wood Johnson Foundation / University of Wisconsin) finds socioeconomic factors are the strongest or a statistically significant predictor of premature mortality in the large majority of states, and that the income-mortality association is strongest among low-income counties (Singh & Siahpush, 2012, PMC3402341; RWJF, 2016). America's Health Rankings documents rural populations having a 23% higher mortality rate and 40% higher preventable-hospitalization rate than urban populations, driven by higher rates of poverty, smoking, obesity, and lack of insurance rather than rurality per se (America's Health Rankings; PMC10547156). A 2023 Milbank Quarterly study (Woolf et al.) and a related 2024 study found a growing, policy-linked divergence in mortality between Republican- and Democratic-leaning states, net of other factors (Woolf et al., Milbank Quarterly 2023; 'The Mortality of Politics', 2024). This corpus's own regression corroborates the direction of all three findings but adds a specific, checkable qualification the literature summaries don't always make explicit: at 50-state grain, the rural effect does not survive controlling for income, while the partisan effect survives but is not robust to single-state removal.

Method

Outcome: health.cdc_mortality, cause_name='All causes', source_type='annual', age-adjusted deaths per 100,000 (2000 US standard population), averaged per state over 2008-2017.

Predictors, each averaged/matched to the same state-years: median household income (census.acs_income, ACS 5-Year, state grain); % of state population in USDA ERS non-metro counties (geo.rural_urban_continuum, 2022 vintage — the only vintage loaded; RUCC changes slowly so this is used as a near-static classification, a limitation noted below); state political lean (officials.state_political_index composite index, -100 to +100, matched to the congress covering each year). Poverty rate (census.poverty_rate) was also tested but dropped from the reported model because it is highly collinear with income (r=-0.85, VIF>5) and adds no independent information (adding it produced no material change in fit).

Model: OLS, mortality ~ income + %nonmetro + political index, n=50 states (DC excluded — it has no voting congressional delegation, so its political index is undefined, and it is a majority-urban city-state that would be an extreme outlier on rurality). R²=0.59. Leave-one-out sensitivity (mcp askengine sensitivity_analysis) was run on both the income and political-index coefficients.

Data limits — why 2008-2017, not the full last decade

The question asks about the last ten years (2016-2025). CDC/NCHS's state-level age-adjusted all-cause mortality series in this corpus (health.cdc_mortality, source_type='annual') is frozen at 1999-2017 — no more recent annual, age-adjusted, state-grain vintage is loaded. Two tables that should carry a more current state mortality series were checked and found broken/empty in this session and reported as data-quality issues: health.chr_premature_death (County Health Rankings premature-death/YPLL series, current-era, state+county grain) fails on every query with an S3 404, and health.cdc_state_vital_provisional (CDC VSRR monthly provisional births/deaths by state) returns entirely NULL rows. CDC WONDER's own API rejects state-level grouping for its NVSS mortality databases (a documented CDC policy, not a gap in this corpus), so there was no live-fetch fallback to a current-decade state mortality series either. Given that, this analysis uses 2008-2017 — the most recent complete decade the corpus's working state-grain mortality series covers — as the best available proxy, and cross-checks its direction against the cited 2023-2024 political-mortality literature, which does cover the post-2017 years the data here cannot reach. The qualitative pattern (poorer and more Republican-leaning states have higher mortality) is corroborated by that more recent literature, but the specific coefficients above describe 2008-2017, not the literal last ten years.

What This Report Does Not Answer

  • Over the last ten years: The corpus's only working state-grain, age-adjusted, annual mortality series (health.cdc_mortality) is frozen at 1999-2017; the two tables that would carry a current-decade state mortality series (health.chr_premature_death, health.cdc_state_vital_provisional) were found broken/empty this session and reported as data-quality issues. Used 2008-2017 (most recent full decade the working series covers) instead, and cross-checked the direction against 2023-2024 published literature that does cover the true last decade, which corroborates the same pattern (poorer, more Republican-leaning places have worse health).
