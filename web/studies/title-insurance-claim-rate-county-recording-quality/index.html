No county-level data links recording-system quality to title-insurance claims — the best national evidence points to fraud, not record age

ALTA/Milliman industry claims analyses (2013-2022, 2014-2023) and county e-recording modernization case studies; no county-grain claims or recording-quality index exists in any public source found

Summary

The data to test this directly does not exist publicly. No source publishes title-insurance claims broken out by county, and no standardized index of county-level property-recording-system age or quality exists — confirmed by both a search of AskAmerica's full ~26-schema data catalog (nothing on title insurance or recorder-office quality) and targeted web research. What national data does exist argues against a simple 'older/lower-quality recording system = more claims' story: the largest and fastest-growing claim-cost categories nationally are fraud and forgery (roughly 21% of paid claim dollars in the 2013-2022 study, rising to over 40% of refinance-claim dollars in ALTA/Milliman's newer 2014-2023 refinance-focused study, averaging $206,976 per claim) — and the industry's own analysis found this fraud/forgery surge is consistent across states regardless of geography, including high-volume states like CA, FL, NY, and TX. Only a minority of claim causes (roughly 12-20% combined — examination/opinion irregularities and search/abstract/title-plant irregularities) are plausibly connected to the quality of underlying public records at all. The honest answer is: plausible mechanism, no data to test it at county grain.

What was checked in AskAmerica's own data warehouse

search_catalog was run against this connector's ~26 topic-area catalog for 'title insurance claims' and 'county property records recording system.' No table anywhere in the corpus carries title-insurance claims, title-insurance loss data, or any measure of county recorder's-office system age, digitization status, or record quality. The closest adjacent tables (NFIP flood claims, crop insurance, health insurance) are unrelated lines of insurance and cannot stand in as a proxy. This gap was confirmed as a known, previously-logged finding in this system's own recipe catalog for this exact question — a prior independent run reached the same conclusion, including the same recommendation to name the confounds a raw correlation would carry even if the data existed.

What the national industry data shows instead

ALTA (American Land Title Association) commissioned Milliman to analyze 127,228 claims from policies written 2013-2022, covering underwriters representing ~70% of industry premium volume (Analysis of Claims and Claims-Related Losses in the Land Title Insurance Industry, May 2024, read directly from the primary PDF). Claim-cause breakdown:

  • Basic Risks (24.0% of claims) — fraud, forgery, competency, capacity, undisclosed heirs, marital rights, zoning/permit violations.
  • Special Risks (21.0%) — mechanics' liens, prior-interest subordination, other liens/mortgages/judgments.
  • Escrow/Closing Procedures (13.7%) — improper instructions, payment failures, closing-protection-letter issues.
  • Examination & Opinion Irregularities (11.9%) — unforeseen risks, irregular omissions, failure to follow procedures — the category most plausibly tied to record quality/completeness.
  • Endorsements, Title Plant, Search & Abstract (7.8%) — take-off of public records, posting, searching/abstracting irregularities — the category closest to 'the recording system itself was hard to search.'
  • Taxes/assessments (7.0%), survey/description matters (5.2%), and smaller categories round out the rest.

Fraud and forgery make up roughly 21% of total claim dollars in that study and average $143,518 per claim (loss + defense costs) versus $26,328 for other causes — over 5x costlier. Milliman explicitly notes the 2021-2022 spike in fraud/forgery claim counts (44% of Basic Risk claims in 2022, up from a 19% average in 2013-2020) 'does not seem to be related to geography... this appears to be consistent across states,' citing CA, FL, NY, and TX as high-volume states all showing similar increases — direct evidence against a geography/recording-quality-driven pattern for the largest cost category.

A newer, refinance-focused ALTA/Milliman update covering 2014-2023 (reported by National Mortgage News, Nov. 2025) found fraud (22.4%) and forgery (17.7%) together now account for over 40% of refinance-claim-cost dollars, averaging $206,976 per claim — nearly 7x the $30,624 average for all other refinance-claim causes, and roughly 50% higher than the prior study's $143,000 figure. ALTA's separate 2026 Seller Impersonation Fraud survey of 245 title professionals across 40 states, DC, and the USVI (primary PDF, fetched directly) found 59% of firms experienced at least one seller-impersonation-fraud attempt in the prior year (up from 28% in 2024), with vacant land, absentee-owned, and free-and-clear properties as the top targets — fraud risk driven by ownership/occupancy patterns and criminal tactics (spoofed contact info, deepfakes, stolen identification), not by how old or digitized a county's recording system is.

Recording-system variation that does exist (but isn't linked to claims data anywhere)

County recording systems genuinely vary enormously in age and digitization, and this variation is real and well documented. E-recording adoption grew from 38% of documents recorded (2014) to just over 50% (2018) across 622 jurisdictions tracked by the Property Records Industry Association (PRIA), and roughly 85% of the US population now lives in an e-recording jurisdiction as of Kentucky becoming the 49th state to offer it in January 2020 (PropLogix, The Future of Land Records, fetched directly). That same source explains why recording-system quality is structurally limited regardless of age: recorders are legally prohibited from correcting or refusing documents that meet basic filing requirements, so errors in the public record persist by design — title plants (privately maintained, re-indexed, and error-corrected copies of the same records) exist specifically because the public record itself cannot be fixed at the source.

Individual county case studies illustrate the real range in modernization state. Delaware County, PA went from zero online records five years ago to 12 million records online and 86% e-recording today, cutting deed-recording time from 3 months to 3 days (Neumo case study, fetched directly). Colorado is still completing statewide digitization of analog land records dating to the 1860s as of October 2025, funded by a $2/document surcharge since 2016 that has distributed $21 million in grants to 57 counties, with rural counties (which historically couldn't self-fund digitization) explicitly prioritized (Colorado Sun, fetched directly). Denver, notably a large urban county, still had a $1.1 million digitization project only partially funded as of the same article — undercutting any assumption that urban/large counties are automatically the most modernized.

Why this can't be answered with a raw correlation even if the data appeared

If county-level claims counts and a recording-quality index were both published tomorrow, a raw correlation between them would still be confounded by at least three variables that move together with recording-system age/quality for reasons that have nothing to do with the recording system itself: property age and transaction volume (older housing stock and higher transaction/refinance volume both independently raise defect and fraud exposure — chain-of-title problems and fraud targeting both accumulate with more transactions, regardless of how well the recorder digitized); urban/rural mix and ownership patterns (the Colorado and Delaware County cases show digitization funding and pace track available local revenue and grant prioritization more than population density, while ALTA's fraud survey shows absentee-owned and vacant-land properties — disproportionately rural or investor-owned — are the top fraud targets, independent of recorder-office quality); and local fraud exposure (ALTA's own finding that the fraud spike is geography-independent argues it tracks national cybercrime trends and criminal tactics, not local record quality). Isolating a true recording-system effect would require a design like a before/after comparison of claim rates in specific counties around their e-recording go-live dates (Delaware County's 2020-era modernization and Colorado's 57 grant-funded county rollouts would be natural candidates), or county/year fixed effects controlling for transaction volume, property age, and ownership-type mix — not a cross-sectional correlation of a claims rate against a quality score.

What This Report Does Not Answer

  • Answer the actual causal/correlational question asked: Cannot be answered directly since neither variable exists at county grain.
