Fiscal stress in borrowing local governments is real but concentrated — moderate on average, acute in specific sectors and places

Census government-finance data (FY2023) cross-checked against Reason Foundation, Moody's/S&P/PIMCO outlooks, and state fiscal-monitoring programs

Summary

On average, fiscal stress among U.S. local governments that borrow is modest and not rising sharply — municipal reserves are near record highs, aggregate debt-service coverage ratios have improved since the pandemic, and Chapter 9 bankruptcy remains vanishingly rare (2 filings in 2024, 2 more so far in 2025-2026). But that headline masks real concentration. Using Census government-finance data, we compute that the roughly 90,000 U.S. local governments (counties, cities, townships, special districts, and school districts) that carry long-term debt owed $1.90 trillion outstanding at the end of FY2023 and paid $75.0 billion in interest that year (a 3.9% implied average cost of debt) — broadly consistent with Reason Foundation's independent $2.0 trillion national estimate for state-and-local bonds/loans/notes. Debt load per resident varies more than 6-fold by state (from roughly $1,300 in Idaho to $9,800 in California), and it is worst where a specific combination of factors converges: heavy unfunded pension liabilities (Illinois, Connecticut, New Jersey), reliance on local special-district and school-district bonding rather than state-level financing (California), aging Rust Belt cities and school systems with shrinking tax bases (parts of New York, Pennsylvania, Ohio), and one-off sectors under acute pressure — hospital districts and healthcare systems, which accounted for essentially all of the last two years' rare Chapter 9 filings.

What 'fiscal stress' means here, and how it's measured

Fiscal stress in local-government finance is not one number. The literature and practitioner community (NY State Comptroller's Fiscal Stress Monitoring System, Pennsylvania's Act 47, Moody's/S&P/Fitch municipal credit frameworks) converge on a common set of financial indicators: fund-balance adequacy, operating-deficit frequency, cash-on-hand, reliance on short-term borrowing, fixed-cost burden (debt service + pensions/OPEB as a share of revenue), and environmental indicators (tax-base erosion, population loss, poverty) that constrain a government's capacity to respond. We use two of these — debt load per capita and the interest actually being paid relative to debt outstanding — as the quantitative core here because they are directly measurable in a comparable, national dataset (Census's Annual Survey/Census of Governments individual-unit finance file), while the fund-balance/liquidity indicators used by NY's FSMS and rating agencies are not available at that same national grain in this dataset and are instead reported qualitatively from primary sources below.

The scale of local-government borrowing and debt service, nationally

From the Census Individual Unit Government Finance File (item code 49U = long-term debt outstanding at end of fiscal year; codes I89/I91-I94 = interest paid on general and utility debt), summed across all local government types (county, city, township, special district, independent school district) for FY2023:

  • $1.90 trillion in long-term debt outstanding
  • $75.0 billion in interest paid that year — an implied average cost of debt of 3.9%

This is a same-definition, bonds/loans-only figure (it excludes unfunded pension and OPEB liabilities, which are real fiscal obligations but not "borrowing" in the bond-market sense). It is corroborated by Reason Foundation's independently compiled 2025 State and Local Government Finance Report, which puts national state-and-local "bonds, loans, and notes" outstanding at FY2023 year-end at $2.0 trillion — our local-only figure being somewhat below that combined state+local total is directionally consistent, not a red flag.

Where it's worst: debt load per resident

Long-term local-government debt per capita (FY2023, Census data divided by ACS population) ranges from about $1,300 in Idaho to $9,800 in California, with New York ($9,679), Texas ($9,018), Colorado ($7,699), Washington ($7,398), Oregon ($6,786), Illinois ($6,117), and Nevada ($6,072) rounding out the top tier. (District of Columbia, at $32,515/capita, is excluded from this ranking as a city-state outlier that combines county, city, and school functions with no separate state layer above it.) The median across states, excluding DC, is about $4,060 per capita.

An important nuance the local-only lens surfaces: where the debt sits in the government hierarchy differs sharply by state. Reason Foundation's combined state+local ranking puts New York, Connecticut, New Jersey, and Illinois at the very top of per-capita debt (all above $30,000/resident) — but most of New York's and New Jersey's burden sits at the state level (NY's combined figure is $39,491/resident vs. our local-only $9,679), while California's burden is comparatively more devolved to local school districts, water/utility special districts, and municipalities, which is why California tops our local-only ranking even though its combined state+local per-capita debt ($20,280 in long-term terms per Reason) is lower than New York's, New Jersey's, Connecticut's, or Illinois's. A question about "local governments that borrow" specifically should weight California, Nevada, Colorado, Washington, and Oregon more heavily than a combined state+local ranking would suggest, and weight New York and New Jersey somewhat less — their acute stress is concentrated at the state level and in a few large local outliers (NYC's MTA, NYC itself) rather than being broad-based across their local sector.

Where it's worst: debt-service intensity and downstream pension/OPEB stress

Interest paid as a share of outstanding debt (a rough proxy for debt-service cost intensity, not a true weighted coupon) is highest in New York (6.3%), North Carolina (4.9%), Ohio (4.7%), Kentucky (4.6%), Illinois (4.5%), and Nevada (4.5%), against a 3.9% national average. New York's elevated ratio likely reflects a mix of older, higher-coupon debt outstanding and its unusually large volume of state-supported public-authority financings that route through local-type entities in this classification.

Layering in the unfunded-liability side of the ledger (not part of our own computed figures, but from Reason Foundation's 2025 report, sourced from governments' own ACFRs): nationally, $1.5 trillion in pension debt and $958 billion in OPEB debt sit alongside the $2.0 trillion in bonds/loans/notes. Illinois carries the highest per-capita pension debt in the country ($17,786/resident, driven substantially by Chicago and Chicago Public Schools), and New York carries by far the largest OPEB debt (about one-third of the national total, $303 billion), reflecting retiree health-care promises that are almost entirely unfunded. These liabilities compound bonded-debt stress because they compete for the same operating revenue that services bonds.

Where it's worst in practice: named governments and sectors under acute stress

Aggregate credit indicators for the municipal market as a whole are currently favorable: Moody's projects record-high median days-cash-on-hand (nearing 1,400 days) and strong debt-service coverage for the sector broadly, and state reserve balances are near record highs (about 13% of general-fund expenditure). But market commentary (Schwab, Breckinridge, PIMCO, Miller Tabak) is consistent that this masks rising dispersion: 'a market where the broad tide is no longer lifting every issuer,' with named pockets of stress:

  • Healthcare/hospital districts — facing reimbursement pressure, labor-cost inflation, and affordability challenges; both Chapter 9 municipal bankruptcy filings in 2024, and one of two in 2025 so far (a California healthcare district), were hospital-related entities, making healthcare the single sector most likely to produce an actual default event even though it is a small slice of total local debt.
  • Illinois and Chicago specifically — Chicago's own pension liability rose to $36.4 billion in 2025 per the city's own analysis, and a new 2025 state law raising police/firefighter pension parity added another $300 million to that liability; the city faces a projected $1.16 billion deficit by 2027.
  • New York State's smaller municipalities — the State Comptroller's Fiscal Stress Monitoring System designated 23 local governments in fiscal stress for FYE2024 (up from 14 the prior year), with the City of Little Falls and the villages of Cambridge, Island Park, and Saugerties in the highest "significant stress" category, and the cities of Albany and Poughkeepsie in "moderate stress." The Comptroller explicitly attributed the year-over-year increase to pandemic-relief federal funds winding down, a mechanism worth flagging: stress rose as one-time federal support receded, not (necessarily) from new structural deterioration.
  • Pennsylvania's Act 47 program for distressed municipalities has shrunk to only 3 remaining participating towns as of 2026, down from a larger historical roster (e.g., Reading, PA graduated from the program in 2022) — evidence that acute, chronic local distress is being resolved over time in at least some cases, not just accumulating.

Interpretation and caveats

Put together: fiscal stress among borrowing local governments is not a broad, worsening crisis by the numbers available here — national reserve and coverage metrics are strong, and involuntary default (Chapter 9) remains extremely rare, partly because 22 states prohibit it and 14 more allow it only conditionally. But it is real, unevenly distributed, and structurally linked to three separate mechanisms that do not always overlap in the same places: (1) sheer debt load per resident, concentrated in California, New York, and a cluster of Western/high-growth states building infrastructure through local special districts and school bonds; (2) unfunded pension and OPEB liabilities, concentrated in Illinois, Connecticut, New Jersey, and New York, which is a materially different and larger problem than bonded debt alone; and (3) idiosyncratic, sector- or entity-specific distress — hospital districts, a handful of small NY municipalities post-pandemic-aid, and historically Pennsylvania's Act 47 towns — that produces the actual defaults and stress designations even though it is a small share of total local borrowing. A reader asking "where is it worst" should get a different answer depending on which of these three lenses they mean, and this report has kept them distinct rather than collapsing them into one ranking.

Methodology notes: the $1.90T/$75.0B figures are our own computation from Census item-code data (49U, I89, I91-I94), summed across county/city/township/special-district/school-district government types for FY2023, the most recent complete year in this table (2024 rows exist but are a partial reload per the connector's coverage scan). Per-capita figures use ACS 5-year population matched to 2023. The implied interest-rate figure is interest paid divided by year-end debt outstanding, which is a rough intensity proxy, not a true weighted average coupon (it does not account for amortization schedules or debt issued/retired mid-year). We did not attempt to reconstruct fund-balance or liquidity-based stress scores (NY FSMS's own methodology) from this dataset; those figures are reported qualitatively from OSC's own releases.
