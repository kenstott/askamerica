How much fiscal stress shows up in the finances of local governments that borrow, and where is it worst?

Published report: http://127.0.0.1:64891/a/d8d9c67310eadc5160b7a19cf8cf2da1.html (local artifact link — see dashboard.png in this folder for the visuals)

Summary

Local governments that borrow (counties, cities, townships, special districts and school districts) carried $2.01 trillion in long-term debt outstanding at the end of FY2022, up 19.9% since 2017 in nominal dollars. Measured against local tax revenue, that debt equals 3.2x annual local tax collections nationally — a ratio that has been flat-to-slightly-improving since 2017 (3.3x), because local tax revenue grew almost as fast as debt. The aggregate trend is rising leverage without yet rising overall stress: unfunded pension liabilities have also improved substantially nationally since 2020.

The burden is highly uneven, however:

  • By debt per capita (2022): California ($10,112), New York ($9,583), Colorado ($9,281), Texas ($9,019), Washington ($7,903), Illinois ($7,197), Nebraska ($7,157), Oregon ($6,639), Nevada ($6,258) and North Dakota ($6,203) carry the heaviest per-resident local debt loads — all above the $3,000-5,000 range municipal-credit literature flags as a caution signal, and above the ~$6,036 national average. Idaho ($1,484), Vermont ($1,817), Wyoming ($1,856) and West Virginia ($1,881) carry the lightest.
  • By debt-to-local-tax-revenue ratio (2022): Arkansas (7.75x), Alabama (6.90x), Nevada (6.76x), Washington (5.46x), California (4.95x), Tennessee (4.77x), Kentucky (4.71x), Colorado (4.33x), Arizona (4.14x) and Texas (3.91x) rank highest — all above the 3:1 threshold flagged as high-risk in muni-credit literature. This ratio partly reflects each state's revenue mix (local-tax reliance vs. state aid), not pure overleverage — a stated caveat.
  • By individual issuer (cities with ≥$50M local tax revenue): the most extreme debt/tax ratios (16x–79x) cluster in fast-growing Sunbelt/Western metros (Arizona, Texas, California, Colorado, Washington, Ohio), most plausibly enterprise (water/electric utility) revenue-bond debt rather than general-fund distress. This corpus does not carry government unit names, only county location, so these issuers are geographically but not nominally identified — a disclosed gap.

Cross-check against independent monitoring: New York's own Fiscal Stress Monitoring System (FY2025, released March 2026) — which uses fund balance, cash position, cash-flow borrowing, and operating-deficit indicators this corpus's debt-only data cannot replicate — designated only 7 of 518 scored villages/cities (~1.3%) in fiscal stress, with just 1 (Island Park, NY) in "significant" stress. This is consistent with the debt/tax evidence: debt stock is climbing, but broad acute distress is not yet widespread, even as 2026 municipal-sector outlooks turn more cautious on labor, construction and healthcare cost growth outpacing slowing revenue growth.

Method and validation

Used AskAmerica's fiscal.govt_finance_by_unit (Census Individual Unit File / Census of Governments), item code 49U = long-term debt outstanding at fiscal year end, T01 = total local tax revenue. Item-code meanings were not documented in plain English anywhere findable, so they were validated empirically against New York City's own numbers: 2022 beginning debt (19U, $124.33B) + debt issued (29U, $13.24B) − debt retired (39U, $10.48B) ≈ ending debt (49U, $127.16B vs. computed $127.09B, <0.1% gap) — confirming the roll-forward identity holds. Per-capita figures used the per_capita tool against census.acs_population. Concentration of debt across states measured via gini_coefficient (0.63, high — expected given population-size differences, not itself stress evidence).

Literature grounding

  • Muni-credit heuristics: debt per capita >$3,000-5,000 and debt-to-revenue >3:1 signal elevated risk.
  • Nationally, unfunded public pension liabilities have declined ~$3T since 2020.
  • 2026 sector outlooks (Governing.com, HilltopSecurities) downgraded local governments to "Cautious" from "Stable" on labor/construction/healthcare cost pressure outpacing slowing revenue growth — a forward risk not yet visible in trailing 2022-2024 debt/tax data.
  • New York State Comptroller's Fiscal Stress Monitoring System (FY2025) is the best available name-level ground truth for actual designated fiscal distress and was used as an independent cross-check.

Key caveats

  1. Item codes (49U, T01, etc.) inferred and validated empirically, not from an official codebook — high confidence but not a documented mapping.
  2. No fund-balance/cash-flow data in this corpus, so a true multi-indicator fiscal-stress score (like NY's) could not be computed directly — addressed by citing NY's independent scores instead.
  3. Government unit names are not in this table (only numeric IDs + county), so the worst individual issuers are identified by county, not by name.
  4. Debt-to-tax ratio confounds state revenue structure (tax vs. state-aid reliance) and debt type (general obligation vs. enterprise revenue bonds) — flagged throughout, not fully separable with available tables.
  5. 2023/2024 are annual-survey (sample, ~24-25k units) years vs. the full ~89k-unit 2017/2022 Census-of-Governments years — levels are not perfectly comparable across the two designs.
