PILT Payments Cover Only a Fraction of the Property Tax Counties Forgo on Federal Land

DOI Payments in Lieu of Taxes, FY2015-FY2026 — national totals verified against CRS, weighed against the best available national tax-equivalency estimate

Summary

No — not close, and not by design. In FY2026, the federal Payments in Lieu of Taxes (PILT) program paid counties $733.0 million for roughly 607 million acres of tax-exempt federal land, the largest payment in the program's 49-year history and essentially the full amount owed under its own statutory formula. But the statutory formula itself was never meant to equal true property-tax equivalency: the 1964 Public Land Law Review Commission that designed PILT's predecessor explicitly built in a 10-40% "public benefits discount," and the per-acre rates and population-based payment ceilings in the formula have fallen well behind land values since. The most recent national estimate of the true gap — a 1999 USDA Forest Service study — found that a fully-funded PILT would need to be roughly 3.5 times larger to match what local governments would actually collect in property taxes if the land were privately owned and taxable. In extreme local cases, such as federal parcels adjacent to or inside Utah city boundaries, the gap is far worse: one 615,000-acre tract received $1.4 million in PILT versus an estimated $358 million or more in foregone property tax — roughly a 256-fold shortfall, though this reflects unusually high urban-adjacent land value, not a national average.

Two things stand out. First, PILT has been funded at essentially 100% of its own statutory calculation every year since FY2018 (per CRS Report R46260) — this is not a story about Congress shortchanging the formula through partial appropriations, the way it was in the 1990s and early 2000s when proration was common. Second, even so, real (inflation-adjusted) PILT funding has been essentially flat since 2018 — $730M in real 2026 dollars in FY2018 versus $733M in FY2026 — despite eight years of rising land values, development pressure, and county service costs in many of these areas. The nominal growth reader coverage often highlights ($404M to $733M, +81%) is mostly inflation plus a formula-driven decrease in a prior-year deduction, not a real increase in generosity.

Why full statutory funding still isn't tax equivalency

The confusion in most PILT commentary is between two different "full funding" concepts. "Fully funded" PILT — which the program has effectively achieved since FY2018 — means Congress appropriates the entire amount produced by PILT's own statutory formula (31 U.S.C. §§6901-6907): acres of eligible federal land x a per-acre rate (the higher of a $3.46/acre "Alternative A" standard rate or a $0.50/acre "Alternative B" minimum, FY2025 figures), capped by a population-based ceiling per county, minus certain prior-year federal payments already received (e.g., Secure Rural Schools). Tax equivalency — what a county would actually collect if that land were privately owned, assessed, and taxed at local rates — is a different and much larger number that PILT's formula was never designed to reach.

That gap is not an accident: the 1964 Public Land Law Review Commission report ("One Third of Our Nation's Land"), whose recommendations became the basis for PILT, explicitly proposed a 10-40% "public benefits discount" below full equivalency, on the theory that the intangible benefits public land provides (recreation, watershed protection, etc.) partly offset the local tax loss. In practice the gap has grown far beyond that intended discount, because the per-acre rates and population ceilings are indexed only to the Consumer Price Index, while land values — especially near growing Western cities — have risen much faster. The most concrete national estimate of the resulting gap comes from a 1999 USDA Forest Service study, cited by the National Association of Counties (NACo): a fully-funded PILT would need to be increased by a factor of almost 3.5 times to reach aggregate national tax equivalency. No more recent comprehensive national study updating that ratio was found in this research (CRS's own R46260 overview, current through August 2026, does not supply an updated equivalency estimate, and the report itself flags this as an open "issue for Congress" — whether the formula should be amended to "approximate tax equivalency").

Local case studies illustrate how much worse the gap can be where land is close to developed, valuable areas. A Utah legislative fiscal analysis found that roughly 615,000 acres of Forest Service and BLM land either inside Utah city boundaries or within one mile of them generated a combined PILT payment of about $1.4 million, versus an estimated property tax liability exceeding $358 million if that land were taxed like adjacent private parcels — a roughly 256-fold shortfall for that specific urban-fringe subset. This is not representative of PILT nationally (most PILT-eligible acreage is low-value, low-density rangeland and forest in the rural West where the tax-equivalency gap, while real, is far smaller in dollar terms), but it shows the formula's flat per-acre rate structure breaks down hardest exactly where land values are highest.

Bottom line

Federal PILT payments are, by the government's own recent record, essentially fully funded relative to the formula Congress wrote for them. But that formula was deliberately set below full property-tax equivalency from the start, and the best available national estimate of the remaining gap — dated as it is (1999) — puts true tax equivalency at roughly 3.5 times the current payment level. So the honest answer is that the payments do not come close to covering what property taxes on that land would actually generate; they cover a formula-defined fraction of it, a fraction that is smallest exactly in the higher-value, more urbanized parcels where the shortfall matters most to local budgets.
