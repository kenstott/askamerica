PILT pays about $1 an acre; against a rough proxy for lost property tax it covers roughly 10-30% nationally, though in low-value Western rangeland it can come close

Answer

Mostly no. In FY2025 PILT paid about $645M on ~607M federal acres, about $1.06 per acre (FY2026: $733M, $1.21/acre). Using a crude proxy for what the land would pay if taxed as private pasture (state NASS pasture value times an assumed effective tax rate), PILT covers about 30% at a 0.3% rate, 15% at 0.6%, and 9% at 1.0% across 48 states. No state covered the 0.6% case in aggregate. Coverage is better in cheap, arid Western rangeland (for example NM pays $2.31/acre against $630/acre pasture; at 0.3% that is roughly $1.9 of tax, so PILT would exceed it) and worse where land is valuable (FL, CA, ID).

Evidence

  • PILT per acre rose from $0.37 (FY2005) to $0.85 (FY2020), $1.06 (FY2025), $1.21 (FY2026), while federal acres stayed ~607M. Source: lands.pilt_county_payments. Part of this is catch-up, not full compensation.
  • Alaska is an outlier at $0.17/acre (225M acres); Nevada $0.60.
  • An older Forest Service study (FY1997) found potential property taxes exceeded PILT by $1.31/acre, and exceeded PILT plus revenue sharing by $0.94/acre, nationally.
  • Caveats: the proxy values federal land as pasture, which understates timber, minerals, parks and developable land but ignores that federal land often has no improvements; the tax rate is assumed; PILT also pays for services burdens and is capped by population formulas, and counties also get separate revenue sharing (Secure Rural Schools, mineral royalties) not counted here.

What This Report Does Not Answer

  • Estimate foregone property tax: No county assessed-value or tax-rate data for federal parcels exists in the corpus; I used a proxy (state pasture value x assumed 0.3-1.0% effective rate) and a 1999 Forest Service study. This is an estimate, not a measurement.
