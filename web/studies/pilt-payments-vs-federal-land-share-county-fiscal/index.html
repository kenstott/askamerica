No — PILT payments cover only a fraction of the property taxes federal land would generate; the only rigorous national study found the gap is roughly threefold

FY2025 PILT: $644.8M paid on 606.7M federal acres. FY1997 USDA Forest Service tax-equivalency study: potential property-tax liability exceeded PILT+revenue-sharing by $0.94/acre nationally — about 3x the payment level.

Summary

No, not fully. In FY2025 the federal government paid $644.8 million in Payments in Lieu of Taxes (PILT) to more than 1,900 counties and other local governments covering 606.7 million acres of tax-exempt federal land — an average of about $1.06 per acre nationally, though the formula's actual per-acre rate is $3.46 ("Alternative A") before a population-based cap reduces most counties' payments. The only rigorous nationwide study that ever compared PILT to what counties would actually collect if that land were taxable — a 1999 USDA Forest Service Rocky Mountain Research Station analysis that sent real county tax assessors to simulate property-tax liability on a random national sample of 105 counties — found that aggregate potential property-tax liability was almost three times PILT payments, and exceeded PILT plus federal revenue-sharing payments combined by $0.94 per acre nationally. Only 51% of counties were fully "tax equivalent" (PILT at or above simulated tax liability) under PILT alone; 62% were equivalent counting revenue-sharing too. No comparably rigorous national update has been published since, so this remains the best available answer, not a live measurement re-run in today's dollars.

Congressional Research Service (CRS R46260, updated August 2026) confirms that since FY2018, PILT has been funded through mandatory appropriations essentially at "100% of the full statutory calculation" every year — so the shortfall documented below is not a story of underfunded appropriations relative to the formula. Congress has been paying the formula amount in full. The gap is that the formula itself — a flat per-acre rate ($3.46/acre for the standard "Alternative A" tier in FY2025, capped by a declining population-based ceiling) — was never designed to equal local property-tax rates, which vary enormously by land-use class, local mill rate, and land value.

How far short of actual property taxes does PILT fall?

The most direct answer comes from a 1999 study Congress itself commissioned: An Analysis of PILT-Related Payments and Likely Property Tax Liability of Federal Resource Management Lands (Schuster, Beckley, Bushur, Gebert & Niccolucci; USDA Forest Service Rocky Mountain Research Station, General Technical Report RMRS-GTR-36WWW, 1999). Congress directed the BLM in the FY1996 appropriations process to determine "the equivalency between Federal payments on resource management lands and likely property taxes those lands could generate." The researchers picked a random national sample of 105 counties (25 East, 40 Interior West, 30 Pacific West, 10 Alaska), worked directly with each county's own tax assessor to build a simulated property-tax assessment for the federal land using real local tax-class categories and rates, and compared that simulated tax bill to actual PILT (and PILT plus other federal revenue-sharing) payments for FY1997.

The headline finding, quoted directly from the report's abstract: "the overall tax liability on Federal lands is almost three times the Federal payments." On a per-acre basis, nationally:

  • PILT alone fell short of simulated property tax by $1.31/acre
  • PILT plus revenue-sharing fell short by $0.94/acre

Regional variation was large — the East (higher land values, higher mill rates) showed the biggest gap at $5.40/acre short (PILT+RS basis), Pacific West $1.61/acre short, Alaska $0.59/acre short, and Interior West (arid, low-value BLM rangeland) only $0.42/acre short, the smallest gap of any region.

Despite the aggregate shortfall, the picture was not uniform: 51% of counties were individually tax-equivalent under PILT alone (PILT ≥ simulated tax), rising to 62% counting revenue-sharing. The study also computed what it would take to close the gap nationally: a fully-funded PILT would need to be increased by a factor of roughly 3.5x to reach aggregate tax equivalency — and even then, 18% of counties would still fall short, because the underlying formula is a flat national rate while local tax liability is highly county-specific.

Extrapolating the ratio to today (labeled as an extrapolation, not a new measurement)

No updated county-by-county tax-assessor study of this kind has been published since 1999, so there is no current-dollar recomputation to cite directly. Applying the 1999 study's own reported aggregate ratio (potential tax liability roughly 2.8x-3.5x PILT) to FY2025's actual $644.8 million PILT payment, as a rough extrapolation only, implies a hypothetical present-day property-tax value on the same federal acreage in the neighborhood of $1.8-2.3 billion — a shortfall on the order of $1.2-1.6 billion a year. This is arithmetic applied to a 27-year-old ratio, not a re-derived figure; land values, local mill rates, and the composition of federal land holdings have all changed since 1997, so it should be read as an order-of-magnitude illustration, not a precise current estimate.

Why the gap persists

Three structural features of the PILT formula, confirmed by CRS R46260, explain why full funding of the formula still leaves counties short of true tax equivalency: (1) the per-acre rate is a single national dollar figure adjusted only for inflation, not for local land value or local tax rates; (2) a population-based ceiling caps payments in more populous counties regardless of acreage, which is exactly where land value (and thus foregone tax revenue) tends to be highest; and (3) prior-year payments under other federal revenue-sharing programs (Secure Rural Schools, Mineral Leasing Act payments, etc.) are netted against the PILT calculation, further reducing the PILT-specific payment in counties that receive those other funds. None of these design features track actual assessed value, so full statutory funding — which Congress has provided every year since FY2018 — does not by itself close the property-tax-equivalency gap the 1999 study measured.
