No: even fully funded, federal PILT payments cover under half the property taxes counties forgo on federal land — and historically covered far less

DOI PILT National Summary (FY2024); USDA Forest Service RMRS-GTR-36 (1999) national tax-equivalency study; CRS R42439 / RL31392

Summary

No — not in the aggregate, and not by a small margin. The one rigorous nationwide study that actually estimated what property taxes federal land would generate if it were taxable (USDA Forest Service, 1999, using FY1997 tax rates and a 105-county stratified sample) found that even a fully funded PILT plus other federal revenue-sharing payments covered only about 46% of the estimated national property-tax liability ($0.68 of $1.48 per acre). PILT by itself historically covered far less — about 11% nationally when it was funded at pre-2008 appropriated levels (as low as 41% of even its own statutory formula in some years). The shortfall is not evenly spread: about half to two-thirds of individual counties ARE effectively made whole, but the gap is concentrated in high-land-value regions (the East, and parts of the Pacific West), where a small number of populous, high-assessed-value counties drag the national aggregate far below 100%.

How PILT actually works, and why it isn't designed to equal property tax

PILT (Payments in Lieu of Taxes, 31 U.S.C. Ch. 69) pays counties a fixed statutory rate per acre of federal 'entitlement land' — in FY2024, the greater of $3.35/acre (reduced by certain other federal revenue-sharing payments the county already received) or $0.48/acre (no reduction) — subject to a population-based payment ceiling, then adjusted for available appropriations. In FY2024, DOI made ~$621 million in PILT payments to more than 1,900 local governments; the program has paid out nearly $12 billion cumulatively since 1976.

Crucially, the PILT formula was never built to equal what a county could actually assess and tax the land at — it is a flat per-acre rate set by statute and adjusted for inflation and population, not an appraisal-based property-tax equivalent. Land in a national park abutting a resort town and land in a remote desert BLM grazing allotment receive the same per-acre rate, even though their taxable market values (and hence forgone tax revenue) differ enormously.

What the one direct tax-equivalency study found (Schuster et al. 1999, USDA Forest Service RMRS-GTR-36)

Congress directed USDA in FY1996 appropriations report language to have Forest Service researchers actually simulate the property taxes federal land would generate. Researchers sampled 105 counties across four regions (East, Interior West, Pacific West, Alaska), worked with local assessors to classify federal land into local tax categories, and estimated what it would be taxed at if privately owned and taxable, then compared that to actual PILT and revenue-sharing payments received in FY1997.

The study's own headline conclusions: 'in the aggregate, property taxes are substantially higher than either PILT or PILT plus revenue-sharing payments, nationally and for regions'; PILT alone covered only 11% of the tax bill at then-current (non-fully-funded) appropriations; and 'to generate an aggregate national tax equivalency, a fully funded PILT would have to be increased by a factor of almost 3-1/2 times — even then, 18 percent of the counties would still not be tax equivalent.'

The picture is more nuanced at the county level than the national number suggests: 51% of individual counties were already tax-equivalent (PILT alone >= estimated tax) in FY1997, rising to 62% counting revenue-sharing and 69% if PILT alone were fully funded. In other words, most low-land-value rural counties with lots of federal acreage are already made whole or over-compensated — the national shortfall is driven by a minority of counties (often nearer population centers or with higher-value land, concentrated in the East and parts of the Pacific West) where federal land would carry a much higher assessed value than the flat per-acre PILT rate reflects.

Has funding gotten better since 1997? Partially — but the underlying rate gap hasn't been re-measured

The funding-level side of the equation has genuinely improved. CRS reports that PILT appropriations averaged only about 60% of the program's own (already modest) statutory formula amount from FY1995-FY2007, dropping as low as 41% in FY1999. Congress moved to mandatory full funding for several years (FY2008-2012) and, per DOI's own FY2024 National Summary, the program has been fully funded again in recent years — $621.2 million in FY2024 against a fully calculated entitlement of $621.2 million (net of prior-year adjustments). Advocacy groups (NACo) report that, absent further Congressional action, current full-funding arrangements are set to lapse after 2026.

What has not been updated is the underlying tax-equivalency estimate itself: no follow-up study since 1999 has re-run the assessor-based property-tax simulation nationally. So the best available answer to 'does full funding close the gap' is the 1999 study's own answer — no, not in the aggregate: full funding alone still left national coverage around 22% (PILT only) to 46% (PILT plus other revenue-sharing programs) of estimated tax liability, because the underlying per-acre PILT rate is set by statutory formula, not by reassessing what the land is actually worth.

Caveats

  • The core quantitative comparison (the only one that actually estimates a property-tax counterfactual for federal land) is a 1999 USDA Forest Service study using FY1997 tax rates and a 105-county sample, not a current recomputation — land values, tax rates, and PILT's own per-acre rate have all changed since. It remains the best available answer because no broader replication exists.
  • The AskAmerica connector queried for this task (schemas: lands, fiscal, geo, econ) carries no PILT payments table and no federal-land property-tax-equivalency table — this is a genuine data gap in that corpus, confirmed by searching its catalog (lands schema covers BLM/NPS/USFS boundaries, ONRR mineral royalties, and timber data, but not PILT disbursements). The entire quantitative answer above is therefore drawn directly from primary government/USDA sources (DOI's own FY2024 PILT National Summary, and the USDA Forest Service RMRS-GTR-36 study), not from the connector.
  • PILT is only one of several federal land payment programs (alongside Secure Rural Schools, Refuge Revenue Sharing, Mineral Leasing Act payments, etc.); the 'fully-funded PILT + revenue sharing' column above is the more complete comparison, but even that combined figure only reaches 46% of estimated tax liability nationally.
  • Alaska is a special case: many of its governmental units (including the North Slope Borough, which alone holds ~46 million acres of entitlement land) levy no property tax at all, so any federal payment there exceeds the '$0 counterfactual' by definition — this mechanically inflates Alaska's apparent 'coverage' in ways that don't generalize.
