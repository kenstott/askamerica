No — PILT payments cover only a fraction of what federal land would pay in property taxes if it were taxable

DOI Payments in Lieu of Taxes (PILT), FY2026, vs. the only national tax-equivalency study ever conducted (USDA Forest Service, 1999)

Summary

No, not close, and structurally so. The Department of the Interior's Payments in Lieu of Taxes (PILT) program paid counties $733.0 million in FY2026 for 606.7 million acres of tax-exempt federal land — an average of $1.21 per acre. The only national study that ever actually measured what those acres would generate in real property taxes (USDA Forest Service, 1999, using FY1997 county assessor data) found that potential property-tax liability on federal land was almost three times what PILT paid — a nationwide shortfall of $1.31/acre for PILT alone, or $0.94/acre even counting mineral-revenue-sharing payments on top of PILT. About half of individual counties came out roughly whole; the other half — generally those with either very little acreage or unusually valuable land nearby — were shortchanged substantially. No comparable national study has been redone since; Congress only just directed DOI (in the FY2025-26 MORE PILT Act, S.1008) to build a market-value comparison tool, effectively conceding this gap has never been re-measured in almost 30 years.

How PILT actually works — and why it can't track real property values

PILT pays counties for tax-exempt federal land (mostly BLM, National Forest, National Park, and Fish & Wildlife Service acres) under a statutory formula (31 U.S.C. §§6901-6907). For FY2026 the base per-acre rate ("Alternative A") was $3.55/acre. But that rate is capped by a population-based ceiling: a county's maximum payment is its population (capped at 50,000 for ceiling purposes) times a per-capita rate. This ceiling has nothing to do with what the land is worth — it is driven entirely by how many people live in the county relative to how many federal acres surround them.

The result, from FY2026 PILT county-payment data: low-acreage, higher-population Eastern states (Virginia, North Carolina, Wisconsin) receive close to the full $3.55/acre rate, while vast, sparsely populated Western states are capped down to a small fraction of it — Alaska received just $0.19/acre on 224.9 million entitlement acres, and Nevada received $0.63/acre on 56.6 million acres. Nationally, the population ceiling pulls the average paid rate down to $1.21/acre against the $3.55/acre statutory rate.

Two much smaller PILT mechanisms (Sections 6904 and 6905, together 0.1% of FY2026 payments) do explicitly tie to fair market value or prior property-tax levies, but only for a narrow set of recently acquired National Park/Forest Wilderness/Redwood/Lake Tahoe parcels — not the program's core.

What the land would actually generate in property tax

The only rigorous national attempt to answer this directly is a 1999 USDA Forest Service Rocky Mountain Research Station study (Schuster, Beckley, Bushur, Gebert, and Niccolucci, An Analysis of PILT-Related Payments and Likely Property Tax Liability of Federal Resource Management Lands, RMRS-GTR-36WWW). Researchers worked directly with local county assessors, simulated how they would classify and value federal land under state tax law, and derived actual local tax rates and procedures — then compared that estimated tax liability to real PILT and mineral-revenue-sharing payments, for a sample of counties in FY1997.

The study's own headline conclusion: "the overall tax liability on Federal lands is almost three times the Federal payments." At the individual-county level the picture is more mixed — 51% of counties were "tax equivalent" under PILT alone (payments at or above estimated tax liability), rising to 62% counting revenue-sharing too — so a majority of low-value, high-acreage rural counties are actually made roughly whole or better. But that means the shortfall is concentrated: it is the counties with the most valuable federal land (often in the East, or near growing metro areas in the West) that are shortchanged the most, precisely because PILT's flat per-acre, population-capped rate does not rise with land value the way a real property tax assessment would.

The study also found that closing the aggregate national gap would require raising fully-funded PILT by roughly 3.5x — and even then, 18% of counties would still not be made whole, because a single national per-acre rate cannot simultaneously match the huge range of land values (a Nevada BLM grazing allotment vs. a National Forest parcel outside a fast-growing Eastern suburb).

Has this been re-measured recently? No.

No comparable national study has been conducted since 1999 (using FY1997 data) — nearly three decades ago. The Congressional Research Service's current overview of PILT (R46260, updated August 2026) lists "whether the current PILT formula...should be amended...to approximate tax equivalency" as an open, unresolved issue for Congress. Notably, a bill introduced in the current Congress, the MORE PILT Act (S. 1008), would direct DOI to build a market-value modeling tool specifically to compare actual land value to PILT payments within two years of enactment — an implicit admission that the federal government itself does not currently have an up-to-date answer to this question, and that PILT's per-acre rate has never been formally reconciled against real land values since the program's early years.

query — 1 rows — 1002 ms
SELECT state_code, area_name, payment_dollars, total_acres,
       CAST(payment_dollars AS DOUBLE)/NULLIF(total_acres,0) AS dollars_per_acre
FROM lands.pilt_county_payments
WHERE fiscal_year = 2026 AND state_code = 'NV' AND area_name ILIKE '%Clark%'
query — 15 rows — 1813 ms
SELECT state_code, SUM(payment_dollars) AS pay, SUM(total_acres) AS acres,
       SUM(payment_dollars)*1.0/NULLIF(SUM(total_acres),0) AS per_acre
FROM lands.pilt_county_payments
WHERE fiscal_year = 2026
GROUP BY state_code
ORDER BY per_acre DESC
