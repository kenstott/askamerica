Individual banks with the largest CRE bets are concentrated in the very metros where office towers sit emptiest

Office vacancy from CBRE, Cushman & Wakefield, Colliers, Moody's CRE and Yardi Matrix (Q1-Q2 2026); bank CRE concentration from FFIEC Call Report data via BankRegReports (Q2 2026 refresh) and CRE Daily/S&P screens of the largest 155 banks.

Summary

The metros with the emptiest offices in 2026 are Houston (27.7% vacant), Seattle (25.2%), San Francisco (24.2%), Washington DC (23.2%), and Chicago (17.8%) — all above the 17.6% national average. In every one of these metros except San Francisco, at least one bank headquartered there carries commercial real estate (CRE) loans equal to 500-600% of its total risk-based capital, roughly 2x the 300% level that triggers heightened supervisory scrutiny: Metropolitan Commercial Bank in New York (599.7%), Marquette Bank in Chicago (587.4%), Capital Bank in Houston (549.7%), and HomeStreet Bank in Seattle (540.9%, a level that pushed it into a 2026 merger with Mechanics Bank). Washington DC's EagleBank is a partial exception: it has cut its ratio from 367% (Q1 2025) to 268% (Q2 2026) after deliberately shrinking its book, though it still carries an above-average 13% office share of total loans. No San Francisco-headquartered bank appears in the current stress band at all — the bank that carried the city's largest concentrated office book, First Republic, failed in 2023 and its book was absorbed into JPMorgan Chase, whose CRE exposure is a small fraction of its $4.1 trillion balance sheet. This is the central and counter-intuitive finding: the metro with the second-highest office vacancy has effectively no locally-concentrated bank left standing to be exposed to it, because the one that was already failed.

Method and what "CRE exposure" actually measures

Two separate measurements had to be combined, and neither corpus (AskAmerica) nor any single public source carries both together. AskAmerica's schemas (search_catalog run against econ, housing, sec, geo) do not carry office vacancy rates by metro or bank-level CRE-to-capital ratios — both are absent from the loaded corpus, confirmed by catalog search. Office vacancy by metro is not a government series (no BLS/Census/BEA table tracks it); it is a private commercial-brokerage product (CBRE, Cushman & Wakefield, Colliers, JLL, Moody's CRE, Yardi Matrix). Bank-level CRE concentration IS grounded in a federal regulatory source — FFIEC Call Report Schedule RC-C (loan portfolio) and RC (capital) — but AskAmerica doesn't ingest Call Report microdata; the figures cited here come from BankRegReports' published Call Report screener, which computes the same ratio banking regulators use (total CRE loans / total risk-based capital) directly from that filing.

Important limitation banks' own Call Reports impose on this analysis: Call Report Schedule RC-C does not break CRE loans out by property type (office vs. multifamily vs. retail vs. industrial vs. construction) — this is confirmed in FDIC's own 2024 Risk Review. So the "CRE concentration ratio" reported per bank is total CRE, not office-specific; a bank's true office-loan share is disclosed only voluntarily, in supplemental investor materials (10-Ks, earnings decks), and only larger public banks report it at all (e.g., EagleBank discloses office at 13% of total loans; NYCB/Flagstar disclosed roughly $3.4B office-backed loans out of a much larger book, dominated instead by rent-regulated NYC multifamily). Community banks in the FFIEC screener report no such office breakout publicly, so their true office-specific exposure could be materially higher or lower than their headline CRE ratio implies.

Geographic matching is by bank headquarters, not loan-book geography. A bank's registered HQ city is a reasonable but imperfect proxy for where its CRE collateral actually sits — community/regional banks in this list (Capital Bank, HomeStreet, Metropolitan Commercial, Marquette, EagleBank) are genuinely local lenders whose loan books are concentrated in their home metro, but larger CRE lenders such as Bank OZK (566% CRE/equity, HQ'd in Little Rock, AR) or First Foundation Bank (513%, now HQ'd in Dallas) originate CRE construction loans nationally, concentrated in coastal high-vacancy metros (NYC, Miami, LA) their headquarters city does not reflect. Those two are flagged separately below rather than mapped to a single metro.

Two additional large, heavily-CRE-concentrated lenders do not map cleanly to a single metro because they lend nationally against coastal collateral rather than lending locally around their headquarters: Bank OZK (566% CRE/equity; HQ Little Rock, AR, but its Real Estate Specialties Group concentrates large construction loans in NYC, Miami, and LA high-rise projects) and First Foundation Bank (513%; HQ moved to Dallas, TX, but its loan book remains heavily California multifamily/CRE). Their inclusion would sharpen the San Francisco/LA and NYC exposure picture further, but attributing their book geographically would require loan-level disclosure this analysis did not locate.

Why San Francisco stands out as an exception

San Francisco has the third-highest office vacancy rate studied (24.2%) but no headquartered bank appears in the FFIEC stress band above. The explanation is not that SF banks are safe — it is survivorship: First Republic Bank, the SF-headquartered lender with by far the largest concentrated exposure to the city's office and CRE market, failed in March 2023 and its assets (including its CRE book) were sold to JPMorgan Chase in a FDIC-assisted transaction. JPMorgan's total CRE exposure is now a rounding error against its $4.09 trillion balance sheet. The remaining Bay Area community banks visible in the FFIEC screener — Five Star Bank (Roseville), Poppy Bank and Summit State Bank (Santa Rosa), Commercial Bank of California and US Metro Bank (Southern California) — are elevated (550-650% CRE/capital) but are headquartered in secondary Northern/Southern California markets, not San Francisco proper, and their exposure is more likely multifamily and suburban commercial property than downtown SF office towers specifically. This is a genuine finding, not a gap glossed over: the bank failure cycle of 2023 already cleared out the single largest concentrated point of bank-level office risk in the metro with among the worst vacancy in the country.

Caveats

  • Office vacancy figures come from competing private CRE research firms (CBRE, Cushman & Wakefield, Colliers, Moody's CRE, Yardi Matrix) whose methodologies and building samples differ slightly; figures cited are the most recent (2026) vintage found per metro and are not perfectly time-aligned quarter-to-quarter.
  • Bank CRE-to-capital ratios are TOTAL commercial real estate (including multifamily, retail, industrial, and construction), not office-specific — FFIEC Call Reports do not require a property-type breakout, per FDIC's own 2024 Risk Review. Where a bank voluntarily discloses an office-specific share (EagleBank: 13% of loans; Flagstar/NYCB: ~$3.4B office-backed), that is noted separately.
  • Bank headquarters city is a proxy for loan-book geography, reasonable for the local community/regional banks listed but explicitly wrong for national CRE construction lenders like Bank OZK and First Foundation, which are flagged rather than mapped.
  • This analysis could not access AskAmerica's own data corpus for either half of this question — office vacancy and bank-level Call Report CRE ratios are both outside its loaded schemas (confirmed via search_catalog) — so every figure here is sourced externally via web research rather than validated against a government-hosted dataset in this system.
