National water rates rose about 28% since 2019, but the increase across communities ranges from single digits to over 5x

BLS CPI Water &amp; Sewerage Maintenance (AskAmerica econ.inflation_metrics) and Bluefield Research 50-city rate index

Summary

Nationally, water and sewer costs have risen substantially faster than overall inflation: the BLS Water & Sewerage Maintenance CPI rose about 28% from 2019 to 2024 (and roughly 35% through 2025), computed directly from AskAmerica's econ.inflation_metrics table. That matches independent industry data: Bluefield Research's 50-city rate index reports a 24% rise in typical household water+sewer bills from 2019-2024 (4.6% in 2024 alone). But the national figure hides enormous variation between communities — in 2024 a typical monthly bill ranged from about $21.76 in Phoenix, AZ to $122.94 in San Francisco, CA, a roughly 5.6x spread, and Northeast utilities averaged $141.53/month versus $118.21/month in the South. Local rates depend on each utility's own infrastructure age, capital spending needs, and rate-setting decisions, not a single national driver.

2019 → 2024 change: (615.5/481.9 − 1) = +27.7%, a compound annual rate of about 5.0%/year — roughly double the ~2.5-3%/year pace of headline CPI over comparable recent years. 2019 was chosen as the baseline because it is the last full pre-pandemic year and is also the baseline Bluefield Research uses in its city-level report, letting the two figures be compared on the same window. AskAmerica's table only declares reliable coverage through 2024 (2-year BLS publication lag caveat), so the 2025 figure above is shown as directional, not final.

Independent confirmation: a third-party CPI tracker (keepingupwithinflation.com), using the seasonally adjusted counterpart series CUSR0000SEHG01, separately reports the same index up 36% from January 2019 to July 2026 — consistent in direction and magnitude with the NSA figure computed here once the extra 18 months to mid-2026 are included.

Variation between communities

AskAmerica's warehouse has no community/utility-level water-rate table (checked via search_catalog; the closest tables — environment.drinking_water and drinking_water_violations — carry system characteristics and compliance violations, not rates or bills). For the community-level comparison this report relies on Bluefield Research's U.S. Municipal Utility Water Rates Index 2024, an annual survey of rates in 50 of the largest U.S. cities / 61 utilities covering about 20% of the U.S. population, as reported by Water Finance & Management (waterfm.com), 2025-02-17.

  • City range, 2024: monthly household water+sewer bill as low as $21.76 (Phoenix, AZ) and as high as $122.94 (San Francisco, CA) — roughly a 5.6x spread for comparable usage.
  • Regional spread: Northeast utilities averaged $141.53/month combined water+sewer in 2024 versus $118.21/month in the South, attributed mainly to older infrastructure in the Northeast requiring costlier upgrades.
  • Composition: sewer charges have consistently been the larger share of the combined bill, about 59% of the total on average since 2019, exceeding water charges by roughly $19.33/month.
  • Affordability: in cities such as Birmingham, AL and Cleveland, OH, combined bills now exceed the EPA's 4.5%-of-median-income affordability threshold; the report estimates minimum-wage earners in some cities must work up to 20 hours/month to cover the bill.

Bluefield's explanation for the variation is structural, not a single driver: local infrastructure age, capital investment needs, regulatory compliance costs, and state/local rate-setting policy each differ city to city, so a city's rate trajectory reflects its own utility's capital plan rather than a shared national cost shock.

Caveats and what is genuinely computed vs. sourced

The national index trend (2019-2025 annual averages and the +27.7% figure) was computed directly from AskAmerica's econ.inflation_metrics table via query() in this session. The city-level dollar figures, the 24% five-year Bluefield figure, the regional averages, and the affordability findings were NOT computed from AskAmerica — that table does not exist here — and are cited from Bluefield Research / Water Finance & Management's published report instead; those numbers should be attributed to Bluefield, not to this connector. A CPI index measures price change for a fixed basket, not the dollar bill itself, so the national and city figures are not on directly the same footing (a percent-change comparison is valid; a dollar-level comparison between the CPI index and any dollar bill is not).
