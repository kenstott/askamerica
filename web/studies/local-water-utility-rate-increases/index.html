Water rates have risen far faster than general inflation nationally, and increases vary widely by community — from under 4% to nearly 15% in a single year

BLS CPI-U water/sewerage index (national, 2000-2026); Bluefield Research 50-city rate index (2020-2025); individual city rate actions for 2026

Summary

Water and sewer bills have risen substantially faster than general prices for at least a quarter-century, and the pace of increase varies a lot by community. Nationally, BLS's water-and-sewerage-maintenance price index rose about 147% from January 2000 to January 2026, versus 93% for overall consumer prices (CPI-U) over the same span — roughly 1.6 times the general inflation rate. Bluefield Research's 50-city water-and-sewer bill survey found a 24.2% cumulative rise from 2020 to 2025, with 2025 alone up 5.1% (a five-year high), still outpacing general inflation. Locally, the spread is wide: recent single-year rate actions range from New York City's proposed 3.7% (its lowest in four years) up to San Diego's approved 14.7% for 2026 (and another 14.5% for 2027) — a roughly fourfold difference in the pace of increase between the mildest and steepest cases reviewed. Bluefield also reports that 5 of the 50 utilities it tracks actually held rates flat or cut them in 2025, underscoring that the local variation runs in both directions, not just fast vs. slow increases.

The national trend

Two independent national series agree on the direction and rough scale:

  • BLS CPI-U, water and sewerage maintenance (U.S. city average, series CUUR0000SEHG02): index value went from 267.3 (Jan 2000) to 661.1 (Jan 2026) — a 147% rise, versus 93% for the all-items CPI-U (168.8 to 325.3) over the same period. Over just the last decade (Jan 2016 to Jan 2026), the water/sewer index rose about 51%, versus 37% for overall CPI-U. Over the last 5 years (Jan 2021 to Jan 2026), water/sewer rose about 29% versus 24% overall — the gap has narrowed slightly in relative terms in the most recent 5 years, but water/sewer has still consistently outrun general inflation in every window checked.
  • Bluefield Research's U.S. Municipal Water & Sewer Rate Index (residential bills across 50 major cities, ~20% of the U.S. population): 2025 combined water-and-sewer bills rose 5.1% year over year, the fastest annual increase since the index's five-year lookback began, and a 24.2% cumulative rise from 2020-2025. Within that, water-only rates rose faster (6.0%) than wastewater/sewer rates (4.8%) in the latest year. Bluefield also finds that since January 2000, prices for water, sewer, and trash collection combined (a broader BLS category) are up 207% against 93% general inflation — consistent with the water/sewer-specific BLS series above.

That is roughly a fourfold spread — from under 4% to nearly 15% — in the pace of increase communities are experiencing in the very same 2025-2026 window. Bluefield's broader 50-city panel confirms this isn't a fluke of the handful of cities above: it reports that 5 of the 50 tracked utilities actually reported flat or declining rates in 2025, at the same time others were posting double-digit increases, and that combined monthly bills average highest in the Northeast (~$147/month) and West (~$143/month), regions Bluefield attributes to aging/combined-sewer infrastructure and water-quality investment in the Northeast, and climate-resilience investment (flood control, seismic upgrades) in the West.

Reasons utilities themselves cite for their specific increases also vary by locality rather than reflecting one uniform national driver: Baltimore cited a 22% jump in treatment-chemical costs and a wastewater construction bid 14% over estimate; Oklahoma City cited an 85% rise in electricity costs and a 155% rise in chemical costs; Corpus Christi cited pipeline/supply-contract costs, lower consumption spreading fixed costs over less billed volume, and debt service; San Diego and Philadelphia are financing large capital programs. This heterogeneity in local cost drivers (chemicals, energy, debt service, one-off capital projects, declining per-customer consumption) is the direct explanation for why the rate of increase differs so much city to city, even though the national trend is consistently upward and outpacing inflation.

Data and methodology notes

The AskAmerica warehouse (askengine) does not carry a table of municipal water utility billing rates or rate-increase percentages by city — its environment schema covers water quality, drinking-water system population served, and violations, not prices. It does carry the BLS inflation index used above (econ.inflation_metrics, series CUUR0000SEHG02 for water/sewerage maintenance and CUUR0000SA0 for the CPI-U all-items comparison), which was queried directly and is the source of the national index chart. The city-level rate figures came from web research (each city's own utility/council announcement and local news coverage) and from Bluefield Research's published national 50-city index and press release, since no government table in this connector's catalog carries a cross-city panel of water rate changes. A dedicated cross-city rate table (e.g., AWWA's or Circle of Blue's raw survey data by city and year) is a genuine coverage gap in this connector, not a table that was overlooked.
