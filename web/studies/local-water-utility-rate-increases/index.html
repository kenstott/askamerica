Water rates: +142% nationally since 2000, with city-level increases in 2025 ranging roughly 2% to 14%

BLS CPI-U Water & Sewerage Maintenance index (national) and Bluefield Research's 50-city Municipal Water & Sewer Rate Index

Summary

Nationally, water/sewer costs have risen far faster than general inflation: the BLS CPI-U index for water and sewerage maintenance rose about 142% from 2000 to 2025 (index 271.9 → 658.2), versus roughly 93% for the all-items CPI-U over the same span — more than a doubling in the rate of price growth. Over the most recent 5 years (2020–2025) the national index rose about 29.5%, and independent city-level tracking (Bluefield Research, 50 largest U.S. cities) puts the comparable 5-year cumulative rise at 24.2%, with a 5.1% rise in the most recent year (2024→2025) alone — a five-year high, outpacing general inflation.

The variation between communities is large. In the most recent Bluefield survey, 5 of the 50 tracked utilities reported flat or declining rates while others posted double-digit increases; single-city examples found here span roughly 2% (Seattle, 2025) to 6–8% (Houston, in separate 2025 and 2026 rate cases) up to a 14%-plus multi-year combined increase reported for Philadelphia. Regional averages differ too: Northeast ($147/month) and West ($143/month) households pay the highest combined average water+sewer bills, reflecting different drivers — aging combined-sewer infrastructure and water-quality investment in the Northeast versus climate-resilience and supply investment in the West.

Cross-community variance — external sources

Because AskAmerica's warehouse has no city-level water/sewer rate table, community-level variance here comes from Bluefield Research's U.S. Municipal Water & Sewer Rate Index (50 cities, ~20% of U.S. population, press release dated 9 Mar 2026) and local press coverage of individual rate cases:

  • National (50-city) average: +5.1% in 2025 (a 5-year high), +24.2% cumulative 2020–2025. Water rates alone rose 6.0% and sewer rates 4.8% between 2024 and 2025.
  • 5 of the 50 tracked utilities reported flat or declining rates in the same period — the variance runs from negative to double digits.
  • Seattle: ~2.0% average increase for 2025.
  • Houston: ~6% average increase effective April 2025; ~7.87% for the combined rate effective April 2026 (separate rate cases in consecutive years).
  • Philadelphia: cited in press coverage at a 14.1% combined water+sewer increase (multi-year framing, not directly comparable to the single-year figures above).
  • Regional averages: Northeast households pay the highest combined average bill (~$147/month), followed by the West (~$143/month); sewer charges have exceeded water charges by an average of $19.23/month over the past five years nationally.
  • Longer view: since January 2000, water/sewer/trash CPI rose 207% vs. 93% for overall CPI-U (Bluefield's framing, which folds in trash collection and uses a slightly different composite than the standalone BLS water-and-sewerage-maintenance series queried above).

Because these city figures are not directly comparable in period length or exact scope (single-year rate-case increases vs. multi-year cumulative press figures), the bar chart above should be read as illustrating the SPREAD across communities, not a precise ranking.

Caveats

1) The warehouse table is a national index, not a rate series, so it establishes the overall trend but cannot itself quantify cross-community variance — that required external sources, flagged as such throughout. 2) City-level figures come from a mix of press releases and local news, with differing time windows (single-year vs. multi-year); they were not independently re-derived from underlying rate schedules. 3) Bluefield's report itself notes it did not find data for Philadelphia's specific 2025/2026 single-year increase, so that figure is a multi-year composite cited by press coverage, included for scale rather than exact comparison. 4) CPI index changes reflect a national market-basket weighted average and do not equal the simple percent change in any individual household's bill.
