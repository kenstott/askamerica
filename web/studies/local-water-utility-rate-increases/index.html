U.S. water and sewer bills have risen about 25-50% over five to ten years, and the rate of increase varies nearly as much between communities as the bills themselves

BLS CPI (national) and Bluefield Research's 50-city household bill survey, cross-checked against each other, 2016-2026

Summary

Nationally, water and sewer bills have risen roughly 25% over the past five years and about 49% over the past ten, consistently outpacing headline consumer inflation. The most recent year (2025) was the sharpest in at least five years, with combined water-and-sewer bills up 5.1% for a 50-city sample tracked by Bluefield Research, against BLS's national water/sewer/trash CPI component up roughly 4.4-4.7% year over year in 2025-2026. But the AVERAGE hides enormous variation: monthly bills across major U.S. cities range from under $22 in Phoenix to over $120 in San Francisco and Northeast metros — a roughly 5-6x spread — and even the direction of change differs: 5 of the 50 utilities Bluefield tracked in its most recent survey had flat or DECLINING rates while others posted double-digit percentage jumps driven by specific local cost shocks (e.g., Oklahoma City cited an 85% rise in electricity costs and a 155% rise in treatment-chemical costs behind its rate increase; Baltimore cited a 22% jump in treatment-chemical costs and a construction bid 14% over estimate).

How much rates have risen, nationally

Two independent measurements agree closely once put on a matching window, which is itself a useful check on both:

  • BLS CPI (recomputed here from the primary source): the BLS series for "Water and sewer and trash collection services, U.S. city average" (CUUR0000SEHG) rose from an index of 248.85 in January 2020 to 310.24 in January 2025 — a 24.7% five-year increase — and to 324.92 by January 2026, a 48.8% increase over the ten years from January 2016. Over the same ten years the headline all-items CPI (CUUR0000SA0) rose 37.3%, so water/sewer/trash costs rose about a third faster than overall prices.
  • Bluefield Research's household-bill survey of 50 of the largest U.S. cities (about 20% of the U.S. population) reports a nearly identical 24.2% cumulative five-year increase (2020-2025) in actual combined water-and-sewer bills, independently corroborating the BLS index. Within that, 2025 alone was the steepest single year in the survey's history: bills up 5.1%, with water rates (+6.0%) rising faster than wastewater/sewer rates (+4.8%).
  • Since 2000, Bluefield puts the cumulative rise in water/sewer/trash prices at 207%, versus 93% for overall consumer inflation over the same 25 years — a multi-decade pattern, not a recent spike.

Year-over-year, the BLS series has posted 3.0-5.5% annual increases every year since at least 2016, including through periods (2020-2021) when headline inflation was much lower — utility capital and operating costs move on their own schedule, largely independent of the broader inflation cycle.

How much this varies between communities

The variation across communities is large on at least three distinct dimensions:

  • Bill level: In Bluefield's 2024 city survey, monthly water bills ranged from $21.76 in Phoenix, Arizona, to $122.94 in San Francisco, California — nearly a 6x spread for water charges alone. Combined water-and-sewer bills averaged $141.53/month in the Northeast versus $118.21/month in the South in 2024, a regional gap Bluefield attributes largely to the age of Northeast infrastructure (including combined sewer systems requiring costly separation/upgrades) and higher regional energy costs.
  • Direction and pace of change: Rate changes are not uniform even within the same year. Bluefield's most recent index found 5 of the 50 tracked utilities had flat or declining rates, while others faced sharp increases tied to specific local cost shocks: Oklahoma City cited an 85% increase in electricity costs and a 155% increase in treatment-chemical costs as drivers of its upcoming rate schedule; Baltimore cited a 22% increase in water-treatment chemical costs and a wastewater construction bid 14% above estimate.
  • System size / rural vs. urban: Outside the large-city surveys, small and rural water systems (serving under 3,300 people, roughly 81% of all U.S. public water systems) face structurally higher per-customer costs — spreading pipeline, treatment, and compliance costs over far fewer ratepayers than urban utilities do — and these systems account for a disproportionate 93% of federal drinking-water violations, which is itself a driver of future rate increases as deferred infrastructure comes due. Multiple industry sources describe small-system per-capita deferred-maintenance costs as more than double those of large urban systems.

Affordability consequences follow directly from this variation: in cities such as Birmingham, Alabama, and Cleveland, Ohio, combined water-and-sewer bills already exceed the EPA's commonly cited affordability threshold of 4.5% of median household income, and Bluefield estimates that covering an average monthly water-and-sewer bill nationally now requires roughly 11.5 hours of minimum-wage labor — a burden that is far larger, proportionally, in lower-income and higher-bill communities than in others.

Methodology and what askamerica added

The AskAmerica connector's own government-data catalog was searched first (search_catalog, describe_table) for a water-rate or water-utility-billing table; it has none — only water quality, drinking-water system inventories (EPA SDWIS), and violation records, none of which carry price/rate data. This is a genuine coverage gap in the connector, not an oversight in the search. To answer the question, the BLS's own public CPI time series (CUUR0000SEHG for water/sewer/trash, and CUUR0000SA0 for all-items) were fetched directly and the 5-year and 10-year percentage changes were computed here (via Python) from the raw index values, rather than repeating a media-reported figure. That computed 24.7% five-year rise independently reproduces Bluefield Research's separately-collected 24.2% figure to within half a percentage point, which is a real cross-validation between two different methodologies (a national price index vs. a 50-city household-bill survey) rather than one number repeated twice.
