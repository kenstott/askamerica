Water rates have risen about 2.4x faster than general inflation since 1983, and vary nearly 5x between communities

National trend from BLS CPI-U (askamerica econ.inflation_metrics); community-level variation from Bluefield Research and MWRA Advisory Board rate surveys

Summary

Nationally, water and sewer prices have risen about 515% since 1983 versus 215% for the overall Consumer Price Index over the same period — water rates have climbed roughly 2.4 times faster than general inflation. The rise has accelerated recently: Bluefield Research's 50-city rate index shows a 24.2% cumulative increase from 2020-2025, including a 5.1% jump in 2025 alone, the sharpest year-over-year rise in five years. How much rates vary between communities is large: in the 2023 MWRA Advisory Board survey of 26 major U.S. cities, an annual residential bill for standardized usage (120 hundred cubic feet) ranged from $770 in Milwaukee to $3,609 in San Francisco — a 4.7x spread — and year-over-year changes in the same survey ranged from +14.3% (Houston) to -4.4% (San Antonio) in a single year.

This is a genuinely long, contiguous series (1983-2026, no gaps), and it is the best available proxy for a household's water bill nationally — but it is important to be precise about what it measures: BLS constructs this index from a national sample of local rate schedules and typical consumption, not a simple average of published local rates, and it captures the price of the same service tier over time, not what any individual household actually paid (usage, tier, and local surcharges vary). It also carries no geographic breakdown below the national level — BLS does not publish a metro- or state-level water/sewer CPI — which is why the community-variation half of this question had to come from industry rate surveys rather than this connector's own tables (confirmed via search_catalog/describe_table: this corpus's fiscal.govt_finance_by_unit table carries water utility revenue totals by government unit (item code A91), not per-customer rates or tariffs, and there is no crosswalk in this corpus from that revenue figure to a per-household bill).

Cross-validation against an independent published figure

Bluefield Research, an industry water-market research firm, separately reports that water/sewer/trash-collection CPI rose 207% from January 2000 to December 2025 versus 93% for overall CPI in the same window — a ratio of about 2.2x, closely matching the 2.4x ratio computed independently above from this corpus's own 1983-2024 CPI series. The two measurements, built from different but overlapping windows and (likely) slightly different BLS series definitions, agree in direction and rough magnitude, which is the check this answer relies on rather than taking either figure alone at face value.

How much rates vary between communities

Because BLS publishes no sub-national water/sewer price index, community-level variation is documented here from two industry surveys that directly bill-shop across cities using a standardized usage assumption:

  • Level of rates (2023, MWRA Advisory Board, 26 U.S. cities, 120 HCF/month standardized usage): combined annual water+sewer bill averaged $1,643 across the surveyed cities; San Francisco was highest at $3,609/year and Milwaukee lowest at $770/year — a 4.7x ratio between the highest- and lowest-billing cities in the same survey, for the identical usage assumption.
  • Pace of increase (year-over-year, same 2023 survey): Houston's combined bill rose 14.3% and Philadelphia's 14.1% year-over-year, while San Antonio's combined bill fell 4.4% — so even the rate of change, not just the level, differs by nearly 20 percentage points across cities in a single year.
  • Regional pattern (Bluefield Research, 50 cities, 2024): the Northeast and West have the highest average combined monthly bills (roughly $141-147/month) versus the South (roughly $118/month), attributed to aging infrastructure and combined sewer systems in older Northeastern cities and climate-resilience capital spending in the West.

Drivers cited by Bluefield for the variation and the recent acceleration include utility-specific cost shocks — e.g., Baltimore reported a 22% jump in water-treatment chemical costs and a wastewater construction bid 14% over estimate in 2025, and Oklahoma City cited an 85% rise in electricity costs and a 155% rise in chemical costs feeding into its rate schedule — alongside aging infrastructure investment needs that differ sharply by region and utility.

What this corpus could and could not answer directly

askamerica's own tables answered the national trend question directly and with a long, checkable series (BLS CPI-U via econ.inflation_metrics). They could not answer the cross-community variation question directly: fiscal.govt_finance_by_unit carries water-utility revenue by government unit but not per-customer rates, and no table in this corpus carries a metro- or city-level water/sewer price index or tariff schedule. That gap is a genuine data-coverage gap, not a data-quality defect, and industry rate surveys (Bluefield Research, MWRA Advisory Board) were used as the independent source for the community-comparison half of the answer instead.
