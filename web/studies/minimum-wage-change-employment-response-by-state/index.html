Minimum wage hikes leave food-service employment essentially unchanged two years later, in a border-county comparison

BLS QCEW county employment (NAICS 722, private), DOL state minimum-wage history, Census county adjacency, 2016-2022 events

Summary

Using a border-county comparison — the design the minimum-wage literature treats as most credible, because it compares a county to the neighboring county it shares a labor market with, differing only in which state's minimum-wage law applies — a state minimum-wage increase of at least $0.75/hour is not associated with a measurable change in food-service (restaurant/bar) employment over the following two years. Employment in the treated-side counties grew 2.90% on average over the two years after the raise; employment in the bordering, non-raising counties grew 3.42%. A formal difference-in-differences regression (log employment on treatment, post, and their interaction, n=4,812 county-period observations across 1,203 pairs) puts the estimated effect at -0.0041 log points — about -0.4%, statistically indistinguishable from zero (p=0.97). Bigger raises did not produce bigger relative losses either (dose-response coefficient +0.0049 percentage points per additional $1/hr, p=0.39), and the null result is not being carried by any single state (leave-one-state-out testing never flips significance). This matches the dominant strand of the modern minimum-wage literature (Cengiz, Dube, Lester & Zipperer 2019), though it does not settle the older dispute with Neumark & Wascher's panel-data work, which finds small negative effects concentrated in teen and young-adult employment using a different design.

What the literature says first

Before running any original analysis, the published record on this exact question is genuinely split by methodology, and both sides need to be named:

  • Cengiz, Dube, Lester & Zipperer (2019, Quarterly Journal of Economics; NBER Working Paper 25434, "The Effect of Minimum Wages on Low-Wage Jobs") pool 138 U.S. state-level minimum wage increases from 1979-2016 and use a "bunching" estimator that tracks the entire distribution of jobs by wage level. They find the number of jobs paying below the new minimum falls, but the number paying at or just above it rises by roughly the same amount — the net change in the number of low-wage jobs is statistically indistinguishable from zero, and stays that way for at least five years after the increase.
  • Neumark & Wascher's long-running body of work (summarized in "Revisiting the Minimum Wage-Employment Debate," NBER WP 18681; Neumark, Salas & Wascher, ILRR 2014) uses national or state panel regressions of teen/young-adult employment on the minimum wage and generally finds small negative elasticities — roughly a 1-2% employment decline for teens and 1.5-2% for young adults per 10% wage increase — with the effect concentrated among the least-skilled subgroups and larger once lagged effects are modeled.
  • The dispute between these camps is substantially about identification, not data: the Cengiz et al./Dube-Lester-Reich "local comparison" tradition argues that national state-panel regressions confound the minimum-wage effect with each state's own divergent economic trend, and that adjacent-county or bunching designs isolate the policy effect more credibly. Neumark & Wascher argue the local-comparison samples are too geographically narrow (mostly the Card-Krueger New Jersey/Pennsylvania tradition) to generalize.

Method — border-county pairs, the credible-identification design

This analysis extends the border-comparison tradition (Dube, Lester & Reich 2010; the identification strategy underlying Cengiz et al.) directly on this warehouse's data, at the industry actually most exposed to a binding minimum wage: food services (NAICS 722), the U.S. industry with the highest concentration of near-minimum-wage jobs.

  1. Identify minimum-wage increase events. From DOL's state minimum-wage history (fiscal.state_minimum_wage_history), computed the year-over-year change in each state's minimum wage, 2016-2022, and kept events with an increase of at least $0.75/hour — large enough to be a meaningful policy shock, not an inflation-indexed nickel-and-dime adjustment.
  2. Build the border-county pair sample. Joined the Census Bureau's county adjacency file (geo.county_adjacency) to find every pair of counties straddling a state line.
  3. Restrict to genuine treatment/control pairs. Kept only pairs where the bordering state did NOT raise its own minimum wage by a comparable amount (control-side increase <$0.25/hr or no change) in that same year — so the pair differs sharply in policy exposure while sharing a local economy. This step yielded 1,834 candidate county-pair events.
  4. Measure the outcome. Pulled food-service (NAICS 722, private-sector) county employment (BLS QCEW, econ.county_qcew) at the event year and again two years later, on both sides of each pair, and computed each side's percent employment growth.
  5. Compare. Averaged the treated-minus-control growth gap across all qualifying pairs, tested it against zero with a formal difference-in-differences regression, and separately regressed the gap on the size of the raise to check for a dose-response relationship.

What was excluded and why: of the 1,834 candidate county-pair events, the query applied the filters b1.annual_avg_emplvl IS NOT NULL, a1.annual_avg_emplvl IS NOT NULL, b2.annual_avg_emplvl IS NOT NULL, and a2.annual_avg_emplvl IS NOT NULL (requiring a non-suppressed BLS employment figure for the treated county at baseline and post, and for the control county at baseline and post). This dropped 631 events (34%), leaving 1,203 pairs with usable employment figures on both sides in both periods. An additional filter, state_fips IS NOT NULL, was applied upstream when building the minimum-wage-change table to exclude the small number of DOL rows for federal/territory jurisdictions (Guam, Puerto Rico, U.S. Virgin Islands, and the federal FLSA row) that carry no state FIPS code and therefore cannot be matched to a state for the border-pair design — this removed 4 non-state jurisdiction rows per year from the pool of candidate "treated" jurisdictions, not from the county-pair sample itself. The employment-suppression exclusion (631 dropped pairs) is driven by BLS's own small-county disclosure rules (protecting the identity of a dominant single employer, disclosure_code='N'), not by anything related to the minimum-wage policy itself, so it is unlikely to bias the direction of the result, but it does skew the surviving 1,203-pair sample slightly toward larger counties with more establishments, where a single employer's suppression is less likely to blank out the whole cell. Re-running the headline comparison is not possible on the excluded 631 pairs because, by definition, they lack a usable employment figure in at least one of the four required cells.

Neither estimate clears conventional statistical significance. The employment gap is small in magnitude (about half a percentage point on a base of roughly 8.4 million food-service jobs in the treated counties) and runs in the direction of slightly slower — not faster — growth on the treated side, but not reliably so; the formal regression estimate is even closer to zero than the simple average-of-gaps figure. There is also no evidence that bigger minimum-wage increases produce worse relative outcomes: the dose-response slope is essentially flat and, if anything, points the wrong way for a disemployment story.

Robustness: leave-one-state-out sensitivity testing (refitting the dose-response regression with each of the 23 treated states removed in turn) never flips the coefficient to statistical significance in either direction — the null result is not an artifact of any single large state (e.g., California, Washington, Virginia, or Minnesota individually).

Caveats and what this does and doesn't show

  • Industry and geography scope. This tests food services specifically (the industry with the densest concentration of workers near the minimum wage) at county grain along state borders. Border counties are systematically more rural than the interior, so this estimate is internally credible for the local labor markets it covers but not necessarily representative of the whole country's low-wage labor market (e.g., it under-samples large urban minimum-wage increases like California's or New York's that occur far from any state line).
  • This is a net-employment-count test, not a bunching/composition test. The Cengiz et al. finding is specifically that jobs shift up the wage distribution without disappearing in net; this analysis, using total NAICS 722 headcount, is consistent with that finding but does not itself decompose the wage distribution the way their bunching estimator does.
  • Two-year window only. The question asked about the following two years; effects that build up more slowly (as Neumark & Wascher argue lagged specifications reveal) would not be fully captured here.
  • Compositional confounds not fully separated. Border-pair comparisons control for shared regional shocks but not for every possible confound (e.g., a state raising its minimum wage might also be experiencing faster underlying wage/price growth for other reasons); this is a descriptive/quasi-experimental estimate, not a randomized one.
  • Suppressed BLS cells. See the exclusion disclosure above — 34% of candidate pairs were dropped for missing/suppressed employment data (the annual_avg_emplvl IS NOT NULL filters).
