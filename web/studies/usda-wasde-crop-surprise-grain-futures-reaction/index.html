USDA Crop Reports Move Corn and Soybean Futures — and Bigger Surprises Reliably Produce Bigger Moves

A synthesis of the agricultural-economics event-study literature, 1985-2025 — this connector carries no CME futures tick data, so all figures below are drawn from published research and CME's own statistics, not computed in-warehouse

Summary

Yes on both counts. USDA's monthly WASDE and Crop Production reports reliably move corn and soybean futures, and decades of event-study research confirm that the size of the market's surprise — the gap between what USDA reports and what traders (via Bloomberg/Reuters pre-report surveys) already expected — is what predicts the size of the price reaction, not the headline number itself. A report that matches expectations barely moves the market even if the absolute figures look big; a small deviation from a tightly-clustered consensus can move prices sharply. Typical report-day moves run from a few cents to 10-15+ cents per bushel in the minutes after release, with intraday trading ranges on report days roughly 15-20% wider than on non-report days. Reaction is strongest for the August-November Crop Production reports and the January/March Grain Stocks reports, and it is amplified when multiple reports land the same day. This connector (AskAmerica) has no table of CME futures prices or USDA report-day surprise data, so every figure below comes from published research (event studies regressing price change on measured 'surprise') and CME's own statistics, cited explicitly — not computed from this warehouse.

1. Does the market move on report day? Yes, and it is well documented.

The broadest literature review of this question (Isengildina-Massa, Karali & Irwin, 2023 NCGA-commissioned synthesis of 80+ prior studies) concludes there is "extensive evidence of futures market reaction to USDA reports in the form of larger return variance." Mattos & Silveira (2016) find WASDE releases raise the conditional standard deviation of corn futures returns by 28.1% during August-October (the growing-season/harvest window) and by 11.9% the rest of the year — several times larger than the reaction to the next-largest foreign competitor, Brazil's CONAB reports (11.4%, and only significant Nov-Jan). CME Group's own comparison of 2024 trading days shows the front-month corn contract's average intraday high-low range was 8.62 cents/bushel on days with an ag report (Acreage, Crop Progress, Export Sales, WASDE, or Prospective Plantings) versus 7.17 cents on non-report days — about 20% wider.

Not every report moves markets equally. Isengildina-Massa et al. (2021, using 1985-2018 data) find that most standalone WASDE releases do not produce statistically significant reactions by themselves — the big moves cluster on days when WASDE lands alongside Crop Production (August), Grain Stocks (January, March, June, September) or the Crop Production Annual Summary (January). Reaction is concentrated in the August-November Crop Production releases and the January/March Grain Stocks releases.

2. Does the size of the surprise predict the size of the move? Yes — this is the core finding of the "market surprise" literature.

A second, more precise strand of research does not just ask whether prices move on report day; it regresses the price change immediately after release on the measured surprise (USDA figure minus the pre-report private-survey consensus, from Bloomberg/Reuters or, historically, Conrad Leslie/Sparks Companies). This event-study design (Karali, Irwin & Isengildina-Massa 2020; Karali et al. 2019; Plante & Dhaliwal 2017; McKenzie 2008; Good & Irwin 2006; Garcia et al. 1997) consistently finds a statistically significant positive relationship between surprise magnitude and price-change magnitude — markets react to the unanticipated component of a report, not the level of the report itself. Removing the "noise" of the anticipated component and isolating the surprise produces an even stronger measured reaction than simply comparing report days to non-report days.

USDA's own Economic Research Service (Arnade, Hoffman & Effland, ERR-293, 2021) built month-specific regressions of daily corn futures on the surprise in USDA's corn-output projection (this month's projection minus last month's), controlling for stocks, consumption, and season-average-price projections. They find the output-surprise coefficient is statistically significant in every month tested, with August and September carrying the strongest influence on price, and that the surprise also moves the day's high-low trading range (their proxy for realized variance), not just the closing level. Errors in past USDA projections (a 3-year weighted average of prior forecast misses) also carry a small but statistically significant effect on how the market prices in the current month's projection — evidence traders partially discount USDA's track record, not just the raw surprise.

Two commodity-specific asymmetries are worth noting: Plante & Dhaliwal (2017) find corn and soybean futures react specifically to grain-inventory (ending-stocks) surprises, not to oil-inventory surprises (which move energy futures instead, with ethanol sitting at the intersection of both). And per the NCGA literature synthesis, corn-price surprises tend to be bearish on average while soybean surprises tend to be bullish on average across the three major report types — a directional tendency layered on top of the surprise-size relationship.

3. How big are the moves in practice?

Concrete, recent example: the September 12, 2025 WASDE contained several bearish surprises for corn and soybeans (larger-than-expected corn acreage, smaller-than-expected yield cuts, weaker soybean export demand). In the first 15 minutes after the 11:00am CT release, December corn futures fell as much as 6 cents/bushel and November soybean futures fell as much as 13 cents/bushel — a typical-sized immediate reaction. That day was unusual in that both contracts fully reversed and closed up 10 cents (corn) and 13 cents (soybeans) versus the prior close, as traders concluded the survey-based yield estimate understated further downside risk from dry late-season weather; farmdoc's review of every 2025/26 WASDE and the June Acreage report found only two other instances (out of six releases) where the initial 15-minute reaction and the full trading-day reaction moved in opposite directions — the norm is that the initial and full-day reactions move the same direction, with the initial 15 minutes capturing most of the eventual move.

Using intraday data, Lehecka, Wang & Garcia (2014) find the strongest price reaction happens in roughly the first ten minutes after release, with no evidence of systematic under- or over-reaction afterward (i.e., the initial move is a reasonably efficient estimate of the eventual adjustment, consistent with markets pricing in the surprise quickly rather than drifting). Kauffman (2013) and Wang, Garcia & Irwin (2014) find the heightened volatility from extended/continuous trading around release typically does not persist beyond 30-60 minutes.

4. Does the reaction mean more uncertainty, or less?

Counter-intuitively, the price volatility spike on report day is evidence of reports resolving rather than adding uncertainty. Isengildina-Massa et al. (2008) show implied volatility in corn and soybean options drops following about 70% of WASDE releases (1985-2002) — by roughly 0.3 percentage points for a stand-alone WASDE and by 1.1 (corn) and 1.5 (soybeans) percentage points when WASDE coincides with NASS Crop Production. Cao & Robe (2022) extend this: the drop in implied volatility persists for up to five trading days, and is larger specifically when pre-report analyst forecasts had been more dispersed — i.e., the bigger the pre-report disagreement, the bigger the post-report resolution, which is the volatility-side mirror of the price-side surprise-predicts-move-size finding.
