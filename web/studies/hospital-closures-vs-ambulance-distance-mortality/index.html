Rural hospital closures add roughly 3 to 15 miles for those affected and raise inpatient mortality in the best study, but the national county data cannot confirm a death effect

CMS Provider of Services termination dates, CHR premature-death (YPLL) county trends 2005-2022; literature for distance and mortality

Summary

Distance: the added trip is usually modest on average but large for some. A Medicare surgical study found median distance for people who lost their nearest hospital rose from 13.1 to 16.4 miles; KFF case studies found the next hospital 12 to 15 miles away; a UNC study of the rural South found about 6 million people with worse travel time, and ambulance times rose 76% in affected ZIP codes (Troske and Davis). Mortality: the best-identified study (California, 1995-2011) finds rural closures raise inpatient mortality by 0.78 percentage points (8.7%), with no effect for urban closures. My own national county test is inconclusive: 26 rural counties that lost their last hospital show no significant change in premature death through 4 years, and a late rise (+733 YPLL per 100k, p=0.07) after 5+ years that I would not lean on.

What I computed

Closures: counties whose every CMS-certified hospital (category 01) has a termination date, excluding ownership-change and merger codes and successor CCNs, with the last closure in 2010-2018 and nonmetro status (USDA RUCC). Controls: 295 nonmetro counties with no terminated hospital. Outcome: County Health Rankings premature death (years of potential life lost before 75), county-year, 2005-2022. Event study with county and year fixed effects, clustered on county. Pre-trend test p=0.06, borderline, so parallel trends are not clearly supported.

Limits: only about 26 usable counties; CMS termination data cannot separate true closures from conversions (for example to Rural Emergency Hospital or critical-access status) cleanly; YPLL is a broad all-cause measure that would dilute effects concentrated in heart attack, stroke and trauma; early CHR years are 3-year rolling windows; the staggered design can bias two-way fixed effects. I did not run leave-one-out checks because the estimates are null. The warehouse has no county-to-hospital distance data, so the distance figures come only from the cited studies.

Bottom line

Expect a typical added drive of a few to about 15 miles, longer in the most remote places, and a measurable rise in deaths among time-sensitive conditions (heart attack, stroke) per the California study. The data I could test here is too thin to confirm or rule out a countywide mortality effect.
