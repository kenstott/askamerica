Rural hospital closures push median travel distance up 15-40 miles, and raise inpatient mortality for time-sensitive conditions by about 9% — but the mortality finding is not universal

GAO-21-93 (2021); Gujral & Basu, NBER WP 26182 (2019, rev. 2020); Mullens et al., Health Affairs Scholar (2025); Joynt et al., Health Affairs (2015)

Summary

Two things are well established from federal audits and peer-reviewed research: distance grows, reliably and substantially. Mortality rises too, but only in the studies that separate rural from urban closures and look at time-sensitive conditions — a national study that pooled all closure types found no measurable mortality effect. Distance: GAO found the median distance to general inpatient care in an affected rural service area rose from 3.4 to 23.9 miles (+20.5 miles) after closure, and to substance-abuse treatment from 5.5 to 44.6 miles (+39.1 miles), based on GAO's own analysis of 100+ rural closures between January 2013 and February 2020. A 2025 Medicare-claims study of common surgeries found a smaller but still real increase, 13.1 to 16.4 miles, for beneficiaries who lost their nearest hospital. Mortality: the most-cited causal estimate, Gujral & Basu (NBER Working Paper 26182, using California data 1995-2011), found rural hospital closures raised adjusted inpatient mortality for time-sensitive conditions (sepsis, stroke, COPD/asthma, AMI) by 0.78 percentage points, a 8.7% relative increase — with no measurable effect from urban closures, and larger effects (11-13%) for Medicaid patients and racial minorities. But a separate national study (Joynt et al., Health Affairs 2015) using a similar difference-in-differences design found no significant mortality difference between rural areas that did and did not experience a closure. The honest read: distance increases are consistent and large; mortality increases are real for some conditions and populations but are not detected in every study, and the surgical-quality literature specifically finds no mortality effect for common operations even as travel distance rises.

Step 1 — How much farther do people travel

GAO-21-93 (2021), the authoritative federal accounting, examined the areas served by rural hospitals that closed between January 2013 and February 2020 (over 100 closures) and computed median distance to the nearest remaining provider of each service, before and after closure:

  • General inpatient care: 3.4 miles (2012) -> 23.9 miles (2018), an increase of 20.5 miles
  • Substance abuse treatment (subset of 11 closed hospitals offering it): 5.5 miles (2012) -> 44.6 miles (2018), an increase of 39.1 miles

A separate 2025 study using Medicare claims 2010-2020 (Mullens et al., Health Affairs Scholar) found median travel distance for four common operations (colectomy, cholecystectomy, appendectomy, hernia repair) rose from 13.1 to 16.4 miles for the 36,884 beneficiaries who lost their nearest rural hospital — but was unchanged for the 41,185 beneficiaries who lost only their second-nearest hospital, because a closer option remained. A geospatial study (McCarthy et al. 2021, cited in the Mullens scoping review) found 106 rural closures pushed 812,314 US residents outside a 15-minute drive to any hospital, 474,435 outside 30 minutes, and 22,023 outside 60 minutes, concentrated in the South and Southeast. For baseline context, rural Americans already live an average of about 10.5 miles / 17 minutes from the nearest hospital before any closure, roughly twice the urban average.

Step 2 — Does mortality rise

The literature is genuinely split by study design and population, not uniformly "yes":

  • Gujral & Basu, NBER Working Paper 26182 (Aug 2019, rev. June 2020) — difference-in-differences on California hospital admissions 1995-2011 for time-sensitive conditions (sepsis, stroke, COPD/asthma, AMI). When rural and urban closures are pooled, there is no measurable effect. Split apart: rural closures raise adjusted inpatient mortality by 0.78 percentage points (an 8.7% relative increase); urban closures show no measurable impact. Stroke/AMI patients specifically saw a 4.4% increase; Medicaid patients a 11.3% increase; racial minorities a 12.6% increase.
  • Joynt et al., Health Affairs (2015) — a national difference-in-differences study using AHA, Medicare and Dartmouth Atlas data found no significant difference in rural Hospital Service Area-level mortality rates between areas with and without a closure (mortality moved from about 5.8% to 5.3% in closure areas vs. 5.8% to 5.7% in non-closure areas — not a statistically meaningful gap).
  • Mullens et al. 2025 (surgical quality) — no significant impact on 30-day mortality, complications, reoperations, or readmissions for common surgeries, despite the measured rise in travel distance.
  • Cancer-specific survival (SEER-Medicare, 2000-2020, published 2025) — worse survival after closure for colorectal and lung cancer patients, but not for breast or prostate cancer patients — evidence that the effect, where it exists, concentrates in time-sensitive or diagnosis-dependent care rather than applying uniformly.

Read together: the strongest, most-cited mortality effect (Gujral & Basu) is real but specific — it applies to acute, time-sensitive conditions, is driven by rural (not urban) closures, and is concentrated among Medicaid patients and racial minorities. Broader national studies that don't isolate those conditions/populations, and studies of scheduled/common surgery, do not find a general mortality bump. The honest summary is: distance goes up for essentially everyone affected; mortality risk goes up specifically for people with acute, time-sensitive conditions (stroke, heart attack, sepsis, COPD exacerbation) and disproportionately for Medicaid patients and racial minorities, while it is not clearly elevated for scheduled surgical care or in a pooled national accounting that doesn't separate these subgroups.

Step 3 — Scale of the ongoing exposure

This is not a historical curiosity. As of the current CMS snapshot, 1,382 Critical Access Hospitals remain operating nationally (askamerica health.cms_hospital_quality) — the category of small rural facility most exposed to closure risk. Per the Chartis Center for Rural Health's 2025 state-of-the-state report, 46% of remaining rural hospitals operate at a negative margin and 432 are rated vulnerable to closure, with 182 rural hospitals closed or converted since 2010 (18 in the most recent year alone). The AskAmerica warehouse does not carry a historical rural-hospital-closure event panel (dated open/close records with pre/post service-area geography) — that gap is why this answer relies on GAO's and the cited studies' own closure-event analyses rather than a warehouse-computed distance or mortality regression; the corpus's health schema carries only current-snapshot CMS facility data (health.cms_hospital_quality, health.cms_pos_facilities), sufficient to count today's at-risk facilities but not to replicate the before/after distance or mortality designs above.
