Rural hospital closures push care 20-40 miles farther away and raise mortality roughly 9% for time-sensitive conditions

Synthesis of GAO travel-distance analysis, NBER/California inpatient-mortality study, and 2026 SEER-Medicare cancer-survival study

Summary

When a rural hospital closes, people typically have to travel roughly 20 miles farther for common inpatient services, and up to 40 miles farther for less common services like substance-use treatment, according to a GAO analysis of 2012-2018 closures. A separate Medicare-beneficiary study found the median distance to the nearest hospital rose from 13.1 to 16.4 miles for people who lost their closest facility. Nationally, the number of people living more than an hour from any hospital rose 82% between 2005 and 2015 as closures accumulated, and by mid-2026 the UNC Sheps Center's tracker counted 197 rural hospital closures/conversions since 2005 (109 complete closures, 88 that ended inpatient care but kept some services).

Yes, more people die. The strongest causal estimate located, published as NBER Working Paper 26182 (Gujral & Basu), reports rural hospital closures raised inpatient mortality for time-sensitive conditions (stroke, sepsis, heart attack, COPD/asthma) by 0.78 percentage points, an 8.7% relative increase — while equivalent urban closures showed no measurable mortality effect at all, evidence that distance/access is the driving mechanism rather than some confound shared by all closures. A 2026 SEER-Medicare study of six states found colorectal cancer patients in counties that lost a rural hospital had a 14% higher hazard of death (HR 1.14) than patients in matched counties that kept theirs; breast and prostate cancer patients showed no significant effect, so the harm concentrates in conditions where speed-to-treatment or continuity of nearby acute care matters most.

How much farther is "farther"

GAO (GAO-21-93, closures 2012-2018): after a nearby rural hospital closed, residents had to travel about 20 miles farther on average for common inpatient services, and about 40 miles farther for less common services such as alcohol/drug treatment. Rural Americans already live an average 10.5 miles (17 minutes) from the nearest hospital — roughly twice the distance and 5-7 minutes longer than suburban and urban residents, per a Pew Research Center analysis reported by the American Hospital Association — so a 20-40 mile addition is a large relative jump, not a marginal one.

Health Affairs Scholar (Medicare beneficiaries, common surgical procedures, 2010-2020): for beneficiaries who lost their nearest hospital, median travel distance rose from 13.1 miles pre-closure to 16.4 miles post-closure. For those who lost their second-nearest hospital (their true-nearest survived), distance barely moved (11.5 to 11.1 miles) — showing the burden is concentrated specifically on people whose closest option disappears, not diffused across the whole population.

National scale: the number of Americans living more than an hour from any hospital rose 82% between 2005 and 2015 as closures mounted (cited in the 2026 Journal of Rural Health study). Checked live against the UNC Cecil G. Sheps Center's own closure tracker (fetched 2026-09-20): 197 rural hospital closures/conversions have occurred since January 2005 (109 complete closures, 88 converted to non-inpatient status), 154 of them since 2010 (86 complete, 68 converted). This corpus's own CMS Provider-of-Services termination table (health.cms_pos_termination_history) was queried directly and confirms hospitals are terminated from Medicare participation every year (68-365 terminations/year 2015-2026 across all hospital types nationally), but that table is not filtered to rural facilities or true permanent closures versus ownership-change terminations, so the Sheps Center's rural-specific, closure-versus-conversion-classified count is the more precise source for this question.

Do more people die

NBER Working Paper 26182 (Gujral & Basu) is the strongest causal evidence located in this research, covering California hospital closures from 1995 to 2011 and adjusted inpatient mortality for time-sensitive conditions. Pooling all closures together, the paper reports no detectable mortality effect — but once closures are split by rurality, it reports rural closures raised inpatient mortality by 0.78 percentage points (8.7% relative increase), while urban closures had no measurable effect. The paper also reports a worse impact for stroke and heart-attack patients specifically (4.4% general increase) and worse impacts still for Medicaid patients (11.3%) and racial minorities (12.6%) — groups with the fewest alternative-transportation resources. This urban/rural asymmetry is itself evidence, per the authors, that distance and the loss of nearby emergency capacity, not some other confound common to all closures, drives the result.

Journal of Rural Health (2026), SEER-Medicare linkage, 2000-2020, six states (CA, KY, LA, NJ, NY, TX): the study reports colorectal cancer patients in counties that lost a rural hospital had significantly higher all-cause mortality (HR 1.14, 95% CI 1.03-1.26) than patients in propensity-matched counties that kept their hospital. Lung cancer patients showed a related pattern: cancer-specific mortality improved over the study period among patients in control counties (HR 0.906) but showed no significant improvement among patients in closure counties. Breast and prostate cancer patients showed no significant closure effect in this study — plausibly because those cancers' treatment pathways depend less on nearby acute/surgical capacity and more on scheduled specialty care that can tolerate longer travel.

Together, the acute-condition study (NBER) and the cancer-survival study (JRH) point to the same underlying pattern from two very different designs, time periods, and geographies: closures raise mortality specifically for conditions where speed of access or continuity of nearby care matters, and the effect is not uniform across all patients or all diseases.

Caveats

  • The NBER causal estimate is drawn from California only (1995-2011); it is the best-identified study located but is not automatically the exact national magnitude today.
  • The 2026 cancer study covers six states with matched propensity-score controls, not a national random sample, and found significant effects for only 2 of 4 cancer types tested — the null results for breast/prostate cancer are a real, reported finding, not a gap in the search.
  • This corpus's health schema carries hospital closure/termination records (health.cms_pos_termination_history) and rural classification tables (geo.rural_urban_continuum, geo.ruca_codes) but no distance-to-care or mortality outcome variable tied to closures, so the quantitative distance and mortality findings above are drawn from the primary research literature (GAO, NBER, Health Affairs Scholar, Journal of Rural Health) rather than computed fresh from this warehouse. The one query run against this corpus's own closure data (national hospital terminations, all types, 2015-2026) is reported above as a cross-check, not as the rural-specific closure count.

Every query behind this report

1 warehouse call ran in this session, in order. Each is reproducible against the same snapshot.

query — 12 rows — 1552 ms
SELECT EXTRACT(YEAR FROM termination_date) AS closure_year, COUNT(*) AS n_terminated
FROM health.cms_pos_termination_history
WHERE provider_category_code = '01' AND is_terminated = true
  AND termination_date >= DATE '2015-01-01'
GROUP BY EXTRACT(YEAR FROM termination_date)
ORDER BY closure_year
