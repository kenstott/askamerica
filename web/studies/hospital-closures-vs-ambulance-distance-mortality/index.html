Rural hospital closures add real travel distance for the affected population, but the mortality evidence is genuinely mixed

Synthesis of peer-reviewed studies (2019–2026) plus AskAmerica warehouse context on rural physician density

Summary

For people who lose their nearest hospital to closure, travel distance for the care that hospital used to provide rises meaningfully — roughly 3 more miles for common surgeries (13.1 to 16.4 miles), 13.8 more minutes on average for obstetric care nationally (16.9 minutes in rural counties specifically), and in the most severe maternity-care cases, a national median of 43 minutes to the nearest alternative in-state hospital in rural areas versus 13 minutes in urban areas. But when you average across all discharges in a closure county rather than isolating people who lost their nearest option, one large national Medicare study found no statistically significant change in average travel distance at all — patients simply redirected to other hospitals already in range.

Mortality is the more contested question. The most frequently cited figure — a 5.9% increase in inpatient mortality after rural hospital closures — comes from a single-state study (California, 1995–2011) that found no comparable effect for urban closures. But more recent, methodologically stronger national studies using staggered difference-in-differences designs on Medicare claims (2010–2020 and 2015–2017) generally find no statistically significant increase in 30-day mortality after a rural closure, with effects showing up instead in secondary measures like readmissions, and concentrated specifically among patients near the most geographically isolated hospitals. The honest read of the literature is: distance goes up for the people who actually lose their local option, especially for specific services like obstetrics and general surgery that aren't offered everywhere; mortality plausibly rises in the most isolated communities, but the broad-population, most-rigorous recent evidence does not show a robust average mortality effect nationwide.

How far do people have to travel? — the studies disagree by design, not by accident

Different studies ask different questions and get different answers, and the difference is informative rather than contradictory:

  • Isolating the people who actually lost their nearest hospital (Mullens et al., Health Affairs Scholar, 2025 — national Medicare claims 2010–2020, dynamic difference-in-differences): median travel distance for common surgeries (colectomy, cholecystectomy, appendectomy, hernia repair) rose from 13.1 to 16.4 miles. Beneficiaries who lost only their second-nearest hospital — the control group — saw no change, confirming the effect is specific to losing the nearest option, not a general area-wide shift.
  • A specific, unevenly-distributed service — obstetrics (Ranchoff et al., O&G Open, Aug. 2026 — 60,516 census tracts, 2010–2021, Callaway-Sant'Anna staggered DiD): counties that lost all in-county obstetric services saw travel time rise by 13.8 minutes on average (95% CI 13.1–14.4), and the effect was much larger in rural counties (+16.9 minutes, CI 15.9–17.8) than urban ones (+10.3 minutes, CI 9.4–11.1). Separately, a national analysis of Medicaid maternity-care hospitals found the median drive time to the closest in-state alternative is 43 minutes in rural areas versus 13 minutes in urban areas — a baseline disparity that a closure widens further.
  • Averaging across an entire closure county, all discharges included (Hoffman, Ha, Fan & Li, Health Services Research, 2024/2025 — 100% Medicare claims, 32 rural closures 2015–2017, staggered DiD): the average change in distance between a patient's ZIP and the hospital they used was 0.29 miles, 95% CI −1.06 to 1.64 — not statistically distinguishable from zero. The authors' interpretation: closures triggered care-delivery adjustments (patients rerouting to other, often higher-quality, hospitals already within reach) rather than a uniform area-wide increase in distance. The one significant finding: among patients near more isolated hospitals specifically, closures raised the likelihood of 30-day readmission by 0.10 percentage points (95% CI 0.00–0.19).
  • Baseline access in the rural South (cross-sectional, 2025 — PubMed 41116094): even without a recent closure, mean travel time/distance to the nearest and second-nearest hospital in the region is already 15.9 miles (22.8 min) and 26.4 miles (35.4 min) respectively — context for how much a further closure can compound.

Bottom line on distance: a rural closure reliably adds miles and minutes for the subset of people who lose their nearest facility or a specific service line (obstetrics, general surgery) that isn't duplicated nearby. Averaged across an entire county's Medicare population, the effect can wash out to statistically insignificant, because much day-to-day care simply shifts to another hospital already in the network.

Do more people die? — one high-profile estimate versus a harder-to-replicate national picture

The number most widely repeated in press coverage is a 5.9% increase in inpatient mortality following a rural hospital closure, with no measurable effect from urban closures, from Kritee Gujral and Anirban Basu's NBER working paper "The Impact of Rural and Urban Hospital Closures on Inpatient Mortality" (NBER w26182), using California inpatient discharge data from 1995–2011 — a single state, pre-2011 vintage, widely cited by CAP, Brookings' Hutchins Roundup, and national press.

More recent national studies using stronger causal designs (staggered/dynamic difference-in-differences rather than a single before/after comparison) generally do not replicate a broad mortality effect:

  • Mullens et al. (2025), national Medicare 2010–2020, common surgical procedures: no significant change in 30-day postoperative mortality, complications, serious complications, reoperations, or readmissions for beneficiaries who lost their nearest rural hospital — despite the travel-distance increase documented above. The authors explicitly note that limited prior evidence of higher inpatient mortality for time-sensitive conditions (stroke, MI, COPD) after rural closures came from a single state and "was not corroborated in a larger nationally representative sample."
  • Hoffman et al. (2024/2025), 100% Medicare claims, 32 closures 2015–2017: no significant change in county-level 30-day mortality or population-level death rates overall; the one significant adverse signal was a modest rise in readmissions (not deaths) among patients near the most isolated hospitals. Hospitalization rates actually rose after closure (111.6 more quarterly hospitalizations per 10,000 older adults, 95% CI 53.4–170.9), which the authors read as evidence people sought care elsewhere rather than forgoing it.
  • Separately, cancer-outcome research finds rural closures associated with worse survival for colorectal and lung cancer patients specifically, but not for breast or prostate cancer patients — suggesting any mortality effect, where it exists, is condition-specific (time-sensitive or diagnosis-dependent care) rather than a uniform across-the-board increase.

Bottom line on mortality: the literature is not settled. The single most quoted number (+5.9%) is from an older, single-state study with strong methodology for its era but no national replication at that magnitude. The newer, national, staggered-DiD studies — generally considered more robust against confounding from staggered closure timing — mostly find null average mortality effects, with harm concentrated in secondary outcomes (readmissions) and in the most geographically isolated communities rather than showing up as a broad national mortality bump.

Why the effect concentrates where it does — supporting context from the warehouse

One structural reason the two literatures (isolating the "lost nearest hospital" population vs. averaging a whole county) diverge: rural counties already have thinner backup capacity, so a closure's bite depends heavily on how isolated the community was to begin with. Querying health.ahrf_physician_supply (HRSA Area Health Resources File, county grain) directly in the AskAmerica warehouse for primary-care physician density by USDA Rural-Urban Continuum Code confirms this gradient: 59.1 primary-care physicians per 100,000 residents in metro-core counties (RUCC 1) versus 31.9 per 100,000 in the most rural, non-metro-adjacent counties with under 2,500 urban population (RUCC 8) and 41.4 per 100,000 in the most rural counties overall (RUCC 9) — roughly 30–45% less primary-care capacity per capita than in metro cores. A separately queried snapshot of health.hospital_with_geography (current CMS facility roster) also shows Critical Access Hospitals make up 57% of nonmetro hospitals (16,425 of 28,620 facility-metric rows) versus only 8% of metro hospitals — meaning the rural hospital stock is disproportionately composed of the smallest, single-facility-per-area designation that a closure removes entirely rather than one of several nearby options. This is consistent with why a closure that removes someone's only nearby obstetric or surgical unit produces a large, statistically clear travel-time effect, while a closure in a county with more redundant hospital capacity produces a smaller, sometimes statistically indistinguishable-from-zero, average effect.

Every query behind this report

2 warehouse calls ran in this session, in order. Each is reproducible against the same snapshot.

query — 2 rows — 11206 ms
SELECT metro_nonmetro, COUNT(*) AS hospital_count, COUNT(*) FILTER (WHERE hospital_type = 'Critical Access Hospitals') AS cah_count
FROM health.hospital_with_geography
WHERE metro_nonmetro IS NOT NULL
GROUP BY metro_nonmetro
ORDER BY hospital_count DESC
query — 9 rows — 3105 ms

FROM health.ahrf_physician_supply
WHERE rural_urban_code IS NOT NULL
GROUP BY rural_urban_code
ORDER BY rural_urban_code
