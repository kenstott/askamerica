Rural hospital closures roughly double-to-triple nearest-facility distance in the affected county, but this corpus cannot itself establish a mortality effect — the closure counties were already losing population 8x faster than the median U.S. county before the hospital shut, and no county-grain, current-window mortality table exists to test the relationship directly.

Sheps Center closure roster; AskAmerica geo/health/census schemas; NBER Gujral & Basu (2019); Hoffman et al. 2025 (Health Services Research). County-centroid distance method.

Summary

Across six documented 2022–2024 rural hospital closures, the county-centroid distance to the nearest remaining hospital ranged from about 4.4 to 30.3 miles (median 9.5 mi). Five of the six closure counties were already losing population faster than the median U.S. county in the seven years before the closure (median -4.5% vs -0.55% nationally, 2012–2019) — direct, computed confirmation of the pre-closure economic/demographic decline the question flags. This corpus, however, has no county-grain mortality table for 2019–2024 (CDC mortality here is state-grain and its rate field stops at 2017), so the mortality-outcome relationship cannot be estimated from AskAmerica data alone; the coefficient below is passed through from published literature, with the finding that published estimates themselves disagree on sign and significance.

Step 2 — Distance to nearest remaining facility, method stated

Method: county-centroid to nearest currently-operating hospital (haversine great-circle miles), computed in SQL against geo.gazetteer_counties (2020-vintage county internal-point centroid) and health.hospital_with_geography joined to geo.gazetteer_zctas (hospital's ZIP-centroid), filtered to facilities with emergency services and hospital_type in {Acute Care, Critical Access}. Post-closure distances: Jellico (TN) 4.4 mi to LaFollette Medical Center; Spring Valley (IL) 4.9 mi to OSF Saint Clare; Herington (KS) 7.0 mi to Memorial Hospital; Martin Co. (NC) 14.0 mi to ECU Health Bertie; Keokuk (IA) 19.5 mi to Southeast Iowa Regional; Mexico (MO) 30.3 mi to Boone Hospital Center.

This is a coarser method than population-weighted travel distance, and the question's own premise — that the difference matters in large rural counties — is directly demonstrable here. For Audrain County (the 30.3-mile case), computing the same nearest-hospital distance separately for each of its 14 ZCTAs gives a range of 10.3 to 34.4 miles depending which part of the county a resident lives in — a 24-mile spread the single county-centroid number hides entirely. A true population-weighted average across those ZCTAs was attempted but could not be computed: geo.gazetteer_zctas.population and geo.gazetteer_places.population are entirely NULL in this corpus for every vintage year checked (2012–2025), and no tract/ZCTA-level population table exists as a substitute (census.acs_population stops at county grain). Both gaps were logged via report_issue. The county-centroid figures above should be read as a lower-bound, could-be-off-by-a-factor-of-two proxy for what a resident actually experiences, not the travel-time figure a rigorous study (e.g. Hoffman et al. 2025, ZIP-of-residence to discharging-hospital ZIP, median 13.1→16.4 miles) would use.

Step 3 — Distance change and mortality: what could and could not be computed

Could not be computed in AskAmerica. health.cdc_mortality is state-grain only, and its age_adjusted_rate field — the only mortality rate column in the entire corpus — is populated for annual rows covering 1999–2017 only, entirely before this closure cohort (2022–2024). There is no county-grain mortality table anywhere in the corpus (checked via search_catalog on 'mortality', 'death rate county', 'life expectancy', 'PLACES county health'). This is a genuine, logged data gap, not a query mistake: a valid within-corpus regression of distance-change on county mortality, at the grain and window the question asks for, is not possible with what is loaded here.

Passed through from literature (not computed here), and disagreeing with each other:

  • Gujral & Basu (NBER w26182, California OSHPD inpatient data 1995–2011, difference-in-differences): rural closures raise inpatient mortality overall by 5.9%, with condition-specific effects of +3.1% for stroke and +4.5% for AMI, alongside an average 11-minute (76%) increase in ambulance travel time the year after closure. This design compares treated (closure) counties to control counties before/after — it does not, by itself, rule out the confound in Step 4; the paper's own robustness checks are what argue for causality, not the raw comparison.
  • Hoffman et al. (Health Services Research, 2025; staggered difference-in-differences with an augmented inverse-probability-weighting estimator; 32 closures 2015–2017; ~3 million Medicare fee-for-service discharges; distance = patient ZIP-to-hospital-ZIP): found no change in 30-day mortality (0.00 percentage points; 95% CI − 0.00 to 0.00 ppt) despite travel distance rising materially, and found no compositional shift in patient demographics/comorbidities that would explain a null result via selection.

The two studies differ in cohort (all inpatients vs. Medicare FFS only), era (1995–2011 vs. 2015–2017), and outcome window (all-cause inpatient vs. 30-day post-discharge), any of which could explain the disagreement — this is exactly the kind of arbitration this corpus is supposed to help settle, and it cannot here because it lacks the county-grain outcome variable both papers had access to in their own restricted-use data.

Step 4 — What the design can and cannot identify; confound quantified

Can identify (computed here): that counties which subsequently lost a hospital were, on average, already declining faster than typical U.S. counties in the years before closure. Using census.acs_population at county grain, 2012→2019 (pre-closure window for this 2022–2024 cohort): Bureau Co. IL -4.8%, Lee Co. IA -4.2%, Dickinson Co. KS -4.7%, Audrain Co. MO +0.1%, Martin Co. NC -6.0%, Campbell Co. TN -2.0% — median -4.5%. Across all 3,218 U.S. counties with data for both years, the median change was -0.55%. Five of six closure counties declined faster than the national county median; the median decline in the closure cohort was roughly 8x the national median decline. This matches the published literature's finding (BMC systematic review, 2025) that adverse income/employment/population trends typically precede closure by up to seven years — confirmed directly from this corpus's own data for this specific cohort, not just cited.

Cannot identify from this corpus: a causal distance→mortality effect, for two independent reasons. (1) Any raw correlation between post-closure distance and a county mortality trend would be confounded by this same pre-existing decline — a county already losing population, income and jobs plausibly has worse baseline health trends for reasons unrelated to the hospital's absence, which is precisely the confound the question anticipates. Separating the two requires a design like Gujral & Basu's or Hoffman et al.'s difference-in-differences / event-study comparison against a matched control group of similar counties that did NOT lose a hospital, with pre-trends tested (an event_study call, not just a diff_in_diff point estimate) — that is a valid design in principle, but (2) it needs a county-grain, contemporaneous mortality outcome to run on, and this corpus does not have one. The literature answers exist because those studies used restricted-use vital-statistics or Medicare claims microdata this corpus does not carry. A rigorous county-grain design here would additionally need to test whether the effect (if any) is driven by one or two of the more severe cases — e.g. Audrain County's 30-mile post-closure distance — via a leave-one-out check (sensitivity_analysis), which was not run because there is no dependent variable to run it on.

What This Report Does Not Answer

  • Relate distance change to a mortality measure at county grain, reporting coefficient and n: No county-grain, 2019-2024-window mortality table exists anywhere in this corpus (cdc_mortality is state-grain and its rate field ends 2017) -- confirmed via search_catalog and describe_table, and logged as a data gap. Reported literature coefficients instead (Gujral & Basu: +5.9% inpatient mortality, DiD, n not disclosed here; Hoffman et al.: 0.00ppt 30-day mortality, n~3M discharges/32 closures), explicitly labeled as passed-through, not computed, and flagged that the two disagree.
