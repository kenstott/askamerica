Rural hospital closures add roughly 20 miles of travel for common services — but the mortality evidence is genuinely mixed, not uniformly bad

Synthesis of GAO, NBER, and Health Services Research literature (2019-2025); AskAmerica's own corpus has no historical hospital-closure or pre/post-distance panel, so this answer is literature-based, not warehouse-computed

Summary

Yes, people travel meaningfully farther after a rural hospital closes: GAO found the median distance to common inpatient services roughly tripled to septupled, from about 3-5 miles pre-closure to 24-45 miles post-closure, depending on the service line, and a separate national Medicare surgical-access study put the more modest median increase at 13.1 to 16.4 miles for patients who lost their nearest hospital. EMS response also gets slower: total 911-to-arrival ('activation') time rose about 7-9 minutes in the year(s) after a closure.

On mortality, the honest answer is it depends on which study, which population, and how isolated the community is — this is not a settled, one-directional finding. The most-cited study (Gujral & Basu, NBER, California 1995-2011) found rural closures raised inpatient mortality for time-sensitive conditions by 8.7% overall, with much larger effects for Medicaid patients (+11.3%) and racial/ethnic minorities (+12.6%), while urban closures showed no effect. But a 2024 NBER study of ED closures nationally (2005-2018) found no overall effect on all-cause, drug/alcohol/suicide, or heart-attack mortality — only scattered subgroup and regional effects (e.g., AMI deaths up in the South/West, drug deaths up among young white women). And a 2025 Health Services Research study of 32 closures using Medicare claims found essentially no change in average travel distance nationally and no broad mortality or access harm — except a modest uptick in 30-day readmissions specifically for patients of the most geographically isolated closed hospitals. The throughline across all of them: harm, where it appears, concentrates in the most isolated communities and the most vulnerable patients, not in the average case.

How much farther do people travel

GAO-21-93 (2021) tracked Medicare beneficiaries in the service areas of the 100+ rural hospitals that closed between January 2013 and February 2020, comparing 2012 (before most closures) to 2018 (after). Median distance to the nearest remaining provider of a given service rose:

  • General inpatient care: 3.4 -> 23.9 miles
  • Emergency department care: 3.3 -> 24.2 miles
  • Coronary care unit: 4.5 -> 35.1 miles
  • Alcohol/drug treatment (only 11 closed hospitals offered it): 5.5 -> 44.6 miles

These are large jumps because in the thinnest rural markets the closed facility was often the only nearby option; the 'next nearest' provider can be a genuine regional hub. A separate, more conservative estimate — cited in the surgical-access literature and echoing a national Medicare cohort — put the median distance increase for patients who lost their nearest hospital at 13.1 to 16.4 miles, a smaller but still substantial jump, reflecting a difference in cohort and service definition rather than a contradiction.

Distance is only part of the delay. Miller, James & Holmes (Health Services Research, 2020), using NEMSIS EMS records, found that in the year a rural hospital closed, average EMS transport time rose 2.6 minutes and total 911-to-encounter-completion ('activation') time rose 7.2 minutes versus comparable rural areas without a closure; in the following year the gaps widened to 4.7 and 9.5 minutes. Because rural EMS response already runs close to or beyond the 8-9 minute 'gold standard' benchmark, the study's authors and prior emergency-medicine literature note that even one-minute increases in time-to-care are associated with higher mortality risk for time-sensitive conditions.

Hoffman, Ha, Fan & Li, Health Services Research (2025)~3 million Medicare FFS discharges, 32 rural closures, staggered diff-in-diffNo significant change in average distance traveled (+0.29 mi, CI crosses zero), no change in 30-day mortality or PAC functional decline overall. The one significant harm: a 0.10 percentage-point increase in 30-day readmissions, concentrated among the most geographically isolated closed hospitals. Authors suggest patients adapted by seeking care at higher-quality facilities, blunting the expected harm.

Reading these together: the size and even the direction of the mortality effect is not a single, stable number — it depends heavily on (1) how isolated the closed hospital was (isolated/frontier closures are consistently worse than closures with a nearby backup facility), (2) which patient subgroup (Medicaid enrollees and racial/ethnic minorities fare worse across multiple studies), and (3) which era and geography (the NBER ED-closure study's null result nationally 2005-2018 sits alongside Gujral & Basu's positive result for California 1995-2011). A reader should not take any single number here as 'the' national mortality effect of a closure — the responsible summary is that harm is real and well-documented in the most vulnerable/isolated cases, and null or negligible in many average cases where patients could redirect to another facility.

What AskAmerica's own data could and could not add

The AskAmerica corpus (searched via search_catalog) carries CMS hospital quality and Provider-of-Services facility tables (health.cms_hospital_quality, health.cms_pos_facilities) and a rural-urban classification (geo.rural_urban_continuum, geo.ruca_codes) — but these are current-quarter snapshots only, with no historical roster of which hospitals closed and when, and no pre/post patient-level travel-distance or outcome panel. That means this connector cannot itself reproduce a GAO-style before/after distance calculation or a diff-in-diff mortality estimate for rural closures — this is a genuine data-coverage gap, not a shortcut taken. The answer above is therefore a literature synthesis, cross-checked across five independent studies from three different research teams and two different eras of data, rather than a number computed fresh from this warehouse.
