Across US counties, places with more police per resident have higher reported crime rates, and the link is positive within counties over time too

FBI CDE police employment (LEOKA) + FBI Return A offenses, county roll-up, 2017-2024 (about 1,200-1,370 counties per year)

Answer

Yes, but not in the direction most people expect. In 2017-2024 county data, counties with more sworn officers per 1,000 residents have higher reported violent and property crime rates. Cross-sectionally the log-log elasticity of violent crime on officers per capita is about 0.73-0.88 each year (correlation 0.31-0.42; 2019: 0.84, robust SE 0.07, R2 0.14, n=1,277 counties). It stays at 0.82 after controlling for county population. Within the same county over time (two-way fixed effects, 1,337 counties, 9,993 county-years) the elasticity is smaller but still positive: 0.53 for violent crime (cluster SE 0.087) and 0.45 for property crime (SE 0.082). This is an association, not evidence that police cause crime. Departments are bigger where crime is higher, so the relationship mostly reflects police staffing responding to crime. I could not cover a full twenty years.

Method

Sworn officers (male plus female) per agency from crime.cde_police_employment_by_agency, summed to county using the FBI-published county label, divided by the covered population. Violent crime (murder, rape, robbery, aggravated assault) and property crime (burglary, theft, motor vehicle theft) counts from crime.cde_reta, joined on the 7-character ORI. Kept only agencies reporting all 12 months, counties with at least 25,000 covered residents and 0.2-10 officers per 1,000, and dropped multi-county and unspecified-county agencies (state police, universities, tribal). Rates are per 100,000 and logged.

  • Mean officers per 1,000 is about 1.8 and stable across years; mean violent rate is about 260-290 per 100,000.
  • Year-by-year log-log slopes for violent crime: 0.88, 0.87, 0.84, 0.86, 0.84, 0.83, 0.73, 0.79 (2017-2024).
  • Property crime slopes run 0.63-0.77.

Caveats

  • Window: eight years, not twenty; crime counts before 2017 are not loaded at agency level.
  • 2021: FBI NIBRS transition cut full-year reporters; only 1,189 counties and 182M covered residents versus about 230-248M in other years. Pooled panel includes 2021; year effects absorb the national shift but not county-specific dropout.
  • Reverse causality and confounding: urban, high-poverty and high-density counties have both more police and more crime. Only population was controlled in the cross-section; poverty, density and commuting inflow were not.
  • Denominators: city police cover people who commute in and out; sheriffs and city departments in one county are summed against their own covered populations, which differ from the Census county population.
  • Not run: leave-one-state-out sensitivity and an instrument for police levels; so the fixed-effects coefficient is not shown to be robust to single states.
  • Reported crime depends on how many police are there to record it, which can inflate the positive link.

What This Report Does Not Answer

  • Twenty-year window: Agency-level crime counts load only for 2017-2025 (cde_reta), so the county analysis covers 2017-2024 (eight years, excluding 2025). Employment goes back to 2010 but there is no county crime series to pair it with before 2017.
