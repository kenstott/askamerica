China's export-sales swings show up in USDA's weekly data within 1–8 business days, and a resumption week can be 3–6 times the size of a normal week

USDA FAS Export Sales Reporting Program methodology; 2018 and 2025 US-China soybean episodes

Summary

Speed: A China purchase or cancellation can become publicly visible in as little as one business day if it is a "large" sale (≥100,000 metric tons in a single day to one destination, or ≥200,000 MT cumulative in the reporting week) — those trigger USDA's daily "flash sale" announcement at 9:00 a.m. ET the next business day. Everything else, including cancellations and smaller purchases, surfaces in the weekly aggregate U.S. Export Sales report, published every Thursday at 8:30 a.m. ET for the reporting week that ran the prior Friday–Thursday — so the maximum lag for an ordinary transaction is about 6 business days, longer if a sale narrowly misses the Thursday cutoff and rolls into the next weekly cycle.

Size of the swing: A normal, active-season week of total U.S. soybean export sales (all destinations combined) runs roughly 1.0–1.5 million metric tons (MMT). China has typically taken roughly half of all U.S. soybean exports in recent years, so a routine active-demand "China week" is plausibly a few hundred thousand MT. Against that baseline the swings from a pause or resumption are large: during the 2025 pause, sales to China ran at essentially zero for roughly six months (May–October 2025), and even after the late-October truce, China imported no soybeans from the U.S. for three straight months (through November) because shipments take time to arrive after being booked. At resumption, the reporting week ending November 27, 2025 alone recorded about 3.0 MMT of net soybean sales to China — a single week equal to two to three normal all-destination weeks, and roughly 6–10x what a normal single-destination China week looks like. The mirror-image 2018–19 episode saw China's soybean-export share (previously ~60–62%) collapse for months after China's July 2018 retaliatory tariff, with U.S. soybean exports to China down about 25% and outstanding China sales down 53% year-over-year by mid-2019.

Corpus-coverage note: AskAmerica's own government-data warehouse does not carry USDA FAS's weekly Export Sales dataset (checked via search_catalog and list_tables against the ag schema and the econ trade tables) — only monthly Census trade figures and annual USDA NASS/ERS series are loaded, and this table's specific weekly China-purchase cadence is not one of them. Every weekly-cadence figure in this report is sourced directly from USDA FAS's own program documentation and closely-reported coverage of its weekly releases, not from a query against this corpus.

How the reporting mechanism works, and why the lag varies

USDA's Export Sales Reporting Program (established 1973, after the Soviet Union's unexpected 1972 grain purchases caused a domestic price shock) has two tiers:

  • Daily "flash" reporting. Exporters must report any single sale that reaches the "large sale" threshold — 100,000 metric tons or more in one day to one destination, or cumulative sales of 200,000 MT or more to one destination within the weekly reporting period (soybean oil uses a lower 20,000/40,000-ton threshold) — by 3:00 p.m. Eastern the next business day. USDA compiles and releases those reports publicly at 9:00 a.m. Eastern the following business day. This is the fastest channel: a big China purchase made Monday afternoon is public Tuesday morning.
  • Weekly aggregate reporting. All other sales, cancellations, and destination changes flow into the weekly U.S. Export Sales report. The reporting week runs Friday through Thursday; exporters must submit by 11:59 p.m. Eastern the following Monday; USDA publishes the compiled weekly summary every Thursday at 8:30 a.m. Eastern. A transaction on a Saturday, for instance, is captured in that reporting week and appears in the report published the following Thursday — about 5–6 days later. A transaction that just misses a Thursday cutoff rolls into the next weekly cycle, extending the effective lag toward two weeks in the worst case.

So the answer to "how quickly" depends on transaction size: a large single sale or cancellation is visible in about a day via the flash system; smaller sales and the fuller weekly detail (marketing-year splits, cumulative outstanding sales, by-destination breakdowns) show up on a roughly weekly cadence with up to about a week's lag. Note separately that the weekly sales report reflects bookings/commitments, not physical arrivals — China's customs data showed zero soybean imports from the U.S. for three straight months after the late-October 2025 truce even as USDA's weekly sales reports showed large new China bookings, because cargoes booked in a given week take weeks to ship and clear customs.

How big the swings are relative to a normal week

For scale: in marketing year 2024/25 the U.S. exported about 68.7 MMT of soy products in total, with roughly 51.2 MMT as whole beans — averaging out to a bit under 1 MMT/week across a full year, though actual weekly totals swing well above and below that within a season (single weeks of 1.17–1.49 MMT total net sales were reported in December 2024–January 2025). China has accounted for roughly half of total U.S. soybean exports in recent years (versus roughly 60–62% before the 2018 trade war), so a normal active-demand week of China-bound sales alone is plausibly in the several-hundred-thousand-MT range.

Two real episodes illustrate the swings against that baseline:

  • 2025 pause → resumption. Sales to China ran at "near-zero" for roughly six months (through October 2025) — a complete stop against a baseline that would otherwise have been several hundred thousand MT/week. After the late-October/November 2025 trade truce, USDA recorded six separate large sales to China totaling more than 1.9 MMT between October 30 and November 28, and traders told Reuters that more than 7 MMT had been purchased since the truce by late December, en route to the roughly 8 MMT China had bought by year-end against its ~12 MMT pledge. For the single reporting week ending November 27, 2025, USDA's weekly report showed about 3.0 MMT of net soybean sales to China in that one week alone — roughly two to three times an entire normal week of all-destination U.S. soybean sales, and several multiples of a routine single-destination China week.
  • 2018–19 pause (the first trade war). After China's July 6, 2018 retaliatory 25% tariff, which hit a market that had been taking about 62% of U.S. soybean exports, sales to China collapsed for an extended period; by mid-2019 total U.S. soybean exports to China were down about 25% year-over-year and outstanding (booked but unshipped) sales to China were down 53%. That period also saw net cancellations and destination reassignments recorded in the weekly reports (e.g., previously committed China cargoes resold to Bangladesh and Iran) — the mirror image of the 2025 resumption spike, but the decline was stretched across many weeks rather than concentrated in one.

Put together, the pattern across both episodes is consistent in shape even though the direction differs: a pause shows up in the weekly data as a multi-week run of near-zero or net-cancellation figures for China specifically, while a resumption tends to show up as one or a small number of outsized weeks — multiples of a normal week's total sales — as buyers work through pent-up demand and negotiated purchase commitments, rather than as a smooth return to the prior weekly pace.
