A China soybean purchase shows up in USDA data within 1-7 days — and one flash sale can dwarf a normal week

USDA FAS Export Sales Reporting mechanics, and the 2025 pause/resumption episode

Summary

A China agricultural purchase pause or resumption shows up in US government data in two speeds. The fast channel is USDA's daily "flash sale" announcement: a single sale of 100,000+ metric tons of soybeans to one destination in one day must be reported by the exporter by 3pm ET the next business day, and USDA publishes it by 9am ET the following morning — roughly a 1-business-day lag from transaction to public disclosure. The slower, comprehensive channel is the Weekly Export Sales report, covering a Friday-Thursday window and published every Thursday at 8:30am ET, a lag of up to about a week. A pause is invisible by construction: it shows up only as an absence of announcements and near-zero net-sales lines over several weekly reports, not as any single flagged event. A resumption is loud: it typically appears first as one or more oversized daily flash sales, ahead of that week's full aggregate. In the actual 2025 episode, China froze all US soybean purchases for roughly six months (May-October) and then resumed with a single-day sale of 792,000 tons — by itself comparable to or larger than a whole normal week's China demand — followed by about 1.9 million tonnes across six sales over four weeks. Separately, weekly export INSPECTIONS (physical shipments, a related but distinct USDA weekly series) swung from a 1.6-million-tonne peak week in mid-October to 800,000 tonnes five weeks later, with essentially none of that volume yet bound for China — illustrating that sales data and shipment data can move on different timelines and even different directions during the same event.

How fast: the two reporting channels

  • Daily flash sales – USDA's mandatory reporting rule requires any exporter who sells 100,000+ metric tons of soybeans (or the equivalent threshold for corn, wheat, etc.) to a single destination in a single day to notify USDA by 3:00pm ET the next business day. USDA's Foreign Agricultural Service (FAS) then publishes the sale publicly by 9:00am ET the following business day — a large single-day China purchase is public within about 24 business hours of the trade.
  • Weekly Export Sales Report – exporters report all sales activity for a Friday-through-Thursday reporting week, with a filing deadline of 11:59pm ET the following Monday; USDA compiles and releases the full weekly summary (net sales, outstanding sales, shipments, by commodity and destination) every Thursday at 8:30am ET. A trade near the start of a reporting week won't appear in the comprehensive weekly numbers for up to about 6-7 days, even if it was already flagged via an earlier flash-sale release.
  • A pause has no positive signal – there is no report that announces "China stopped buying"; a halt only becomes visible as an absence — zero or near-zero net sales/no China lines — across consecutive weekly reports, and as the disappearance of flash-sale announcements. Confirming a genuine pause (versus a routine lull) takes at least one full weekly reporting cycle, typically several.
  • USDA revises both daily and weekly figures for cancellations and destination reclassifications in later releases, so early figures in a resumption episode are commonly revised upward as "unknown destination" bookings get reassigned to China.
  • Sales versus shipments are separate USDA weekly series that can diverge sharply. As of November 20, 2025 — three weeks after the flash-sale resumption began — USDA's separate weekly export-inspections data showed zero physical soybean shipments bound for China and total inspections down to their lowest level in years, even as the sales-side ESR/flash-sale reports were already showing large new China bookings. A resumption shows up in the sales data before it shows up in the shipment data.

How big: the swings versus a normal week

US soybean exports run roughly 44-51 million metric tons per marketing year across all destinations, an average of very roughly 0.85-1 million metric tons per week averaged over a full year — but actual weekly volume is highly seasonal (concentrated in the fall harvest/shipping season) and can swing heavily on single-buyer decisions, so "normal" varies a great deal by week and season. Against that backdrop, the 2025 China episode shows swings that are large multiples of routine weekly activity:

  • Pause (May-October 2025): China's net soybean purchases from the US fell to essentially zero for about six months during the tariff dispute — a complete drop-out of what is normally the single largest destination for US soybeans (roughly half of exported US soybeans in a normal year), not a partial slowdown.
  • Resumption, sales side (late October-November 2025): the single largest one-day sale on resumption was 792,000 tons — China's largest single-day US soybean purchase in two years — comparable to or larger than a whole normal week's China demand on its own. Over the following month (October 30-November 28), USDA recorded six separate major sales totaling more than 1.9 million tonnes.
  • Resumption, shipment side: weekly export inspections (all destinations) fell from a roughly 1.6-million-tonne peak the week of October 16 to about 800,000 tonnes the week of November 20 — roughly half — even as sales-side headlines were turning positive, because the underlying price/tariff dynamics (a 10% Chinese tariff still made US beans barely competitive with Brazil) meant purchases were not yet translating into shipments.
  • Commitment scale: China ultimately committed to roughly 12 million metric tons for the final two months of 2025 alone, then pledges of 25 million tons/year for 2026-2028. By spring 2026, cumulative exports to China from January-March were reported up 57% year-over-year, but China's share of total US soybean exports since the September 2025 marketing-year start remained under 30%, roughly half its typical share — underscoring that even a large headline sales-side rebound can take many months to close a gap opened by a multi-month pause.

The general pattern: because China is typically the largest single buyer of US soybeans, its absence or return doesn't just nudge the weekly total — it can be the difference between a near-zero print and a print several times a routine week's volume, concentrated in one or two headline sales rather than spread evenly across the week, with the shipment data lagging the sales data by additional weeks.

Data availability note

AskAmerica's own government-data corpus does not currently carry USDA FAS's Export Sales Reporting (ESR) system — neither the weekly Thursday report nor the daily flash-sale releases. It holds monthly Census trade data (econ.trade_exports) and USDA NASS/ERS/RMA/FSA production and payment tables, none of which is the weekly, commodity-by-destination export-sales series this question actually asks about; no query was run against those tables for this answer since they cannot isolate weekly soybean flash sales. This gap has been filed as a sourcing issue (report_issue) recommending ingestion of USDA's Export Sales Query System as a new table. The figures above come instead from USDA's own published Export Sales Reporting Program rules and from press coverage that reads USDA's weekly and daily data directly (Grain Journal; CZ app, authored by former USDA ERS economist Fred Gale, citing USDA export-inspection and Chinese customs data; Agri-Pulse; Investigate Midwest/Tennessee Lookout citing USDA export data); direct fetches of fas.usda.gov itself returned HTTP 403 during this research, so no figure here was read first-hand from USDA's own table.
