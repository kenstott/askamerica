In 2023, deposits fled the biggest US banks and small failed institutions — not into megabanks as widely reported

FDIC Summary of Deposits and Quarterly Banking Profile, Federal Reserve H.8, and FDIC BankFind failure records

Summary

Roughly $850-900 billion in bank deposits left the US banking system on net in the year spanning the 2023 crisis, and contrary to the popular narrative, the biggest banks lost the most, not the least. FDIC's own Summary of Deposits survey (year ending June 30, 2023 — the cleanest mergers-adjusted, apples-to-apples window available) shows total deposits fell $874.1 billion (4.8%), the first annual industry decline since 1995. The 14 largest US banks (assets over $250 billion — JPMorgan, Bank of America, Wells Fargo, Citigroup and peers) shed 6.8% of their deposits, more than any other size class, and accounted for 77.5% of the industry-wide decline. Separately, Federal Reserve H.8 data show JPMorgan, Bank of America and Wells Fargo alone lost a combined $464.7 billion in deposits between April 2022 and April 2023 — more than twice what all ~4,000 small banks lost combined ($210 billion) over the same period. Community banks (mostly under $1 billion in assets) actually grew deposits slightly (+1.0%). The five institutions that failed outright in 2023 held $440.6 billion in deposits at the moment of failure, with First Republic ($176.4B), Silicon Valley Bank ($175.4B) and Signature Bank ($88.6B) accounting for 99.95% of that; two small, unrelated Midwest failures (Heartland Tri-State Bank and Citizens Bank of Sac City) totaled barely $180 million between them. Much of the money that left banks went into money market funds and, within the banking system, into reciprocal/brokered deposit arrangements that let mid-size banks re-insure existing depositors rather than attract new ones.

How much moved, and over what window

The 'how much' answer depends heavily on the window used, and this matters because deposit levels moved sharply within 2023 itself:

  • Q4 2022 to Q1 2023: domestic deposits fell $472.1 billion (2.5%) in a single quarter — the largest quarterly decline the FDIC has recorded since it began collecting Call Report data in 1984, driven overwhelmingly by uninsured deposits (down $663.3 billion, 8.3%) fleeing in the SVB/Signature/First Republic panic.
  • Year ending June 30, 2023 (FDIC's own annual Summary of Deposits comparison window): total domestic deposits fell $874.1 billion (4.8%) to $17.2 trillion — the first annual decline since 1995.
  • Q4 2023: deposits actually rose $186.9 billion (1.1%) — the first quarterly increase in seven quarters — as the panic fully subsided and time-deposit growth (chasing higher rates) offset continued erosion of non-interest-bearing balances.

Because the FDIC only publishes its mergers-adjusted bank-size breakdown on a June-to-June basis (its Summary of Deposits survey year), that is the cleanest lens for 'who gained, who lost' even though the question asks about calendar 2023; the qualitative pattern — biggest banks lose share, smallest banks hold roughly steady — is consistent with the Q4-2022-to-Q1-2023 crisis quarter and with the Fed's separately-published weekly H.8 series for named megabanks.

Which institutions lost the most — beyond the failures: the megabanks

The less-told part of the story is that the nation's largest surviving banks also lost enormous deposit volumes over calendar 2023, undercutting the 'flight to safety into JPMorgan/BofA/Wells Fargo' narrative that dominated March 2023 coverage. Using the Federal Reserve's weekly H.8 series (assets and liabilities of commercial banks), deposits at the three largest US banks fell between April 2022 and April 2023:

  • JPMorgan Chase: -$184 billion
  • Bank of America: -$162 billion
  • Wells Fargo: -$118.7 billion
  • Combined: -$464.7 billion — 72% of the total $644 billion decline across the 25 largest US banks, and more than double the $210 billion lost by all ~4,000 small banks combined over the same period

Both Bank of America and Wells Fargo saw deposits fall in every one of five consecutive quarters, including Q1 2023 — the very quarter headlines described as a windfall of crisis-driven inflows for 'too big to fail' banks. JPMorgan did see a small first-quarter uptick (managers separately estimated retaining about $50 billion of March inflows), but its deposits were still down year-over-year. This pattern matches the FDIC's own SOD finding above: the >$250B bank tier had the industry's steepest annual decline, not the shallowest.

Charles Schwab's bank subsidiary is a distinct, non-crisis case worth naming separately: its deposits fell from $465.8 billion to $325.7 billion year-over-year through Q1 2023 (roughly -30%, on the order of $100-140 billion), driven by structural 'cash sorting' as brokerage clients moved idle cash from low-yielding bank sweep accounts into higher-yielding money market funds and Treasurys — a rate-driven, not panic-driven, outflow that continued through most of 2023 and into 2024.

Where the money actually went

Three destinations account for most of the outflow: (1) money market mutual funds, which offered yields far above what banks were paying on deposits and which absorbed much of the uninsured-deposit exodus; (2) within the banking system, growth in brokered and reciprocal deposits — arrangements that spread an existing depositor's balance across a network of banks to multiply FDIC insurance coverage without bringing in a single new dollar. Reciprocal deposits jumped roughly 20% in 2023 at banks with $1-100 billion in assets as institutions like PacWest and Western Alliance used them to reassure spooked uninsured depositors; and (3) a shift in composition rather than total volume — time deposits (CDs) grew about 42% year-over-year industry-wide as banks competed on rate to retain money that would otherwise have left for money funds, while noninterest-bearing (checking-type) balances declined for seven consecutive quarters.

Method and what this adds beyond news coverage

This answer combines: (a) the FDIC's own primary Summary of Deposits and Quarterly Banking Profile publications, fetched and read directly rather than relied on via secondary summary, for the size-class breakdown and the industry aggregate; (b) the FDIC's keyless BankFind failures API, queried directly, to get the complete and verified population of five 2023 failures rather than assuming the three famous cases were the whole list; and (c) Federal Reserve research (Liberty Street Economics, Cleveland Fed) and financial press reporting of the Fed's H.8 weekly release for named-bank figures the FDIC's own aggregate tables do not break out by company. Note: this connector's SEC XBRL tables (sec.financial_line_items) declare 2010-2026 coverage for bank 'Deposits' concepts but, on inspection, currently hold only 2026-vintage filings for the banks checked (JPMorgan CIK 0000019617) — a real data-coverage gap, reported separately — so company-level 10-K deposit figures here are sourced from FDIC/Fed data and financial press rather than pulled directly from this connector's SEC tables.
