Bank branch closures cut local small-business lending by roughly 10% net (19% gross), for up to six years

Answer built from the peer-reviewed literature — AskAmerica's own data catalog has no bank-branch-location/closure table (confirmed gap, FDIC BankFind is the external source that would fill it)

Summary

When a bank branch closes, small-business lending in that immediate area (the census tract) falls sharply and stays depressed for years. The best-identified estimate, from Hoai-Luu Nguyen's study of merger-driven closings in the 2000s (published in the American Economic Journal: Applied Economics, 2019), finds a 19% gross decline in new small-business loan dollar volume originated by the closed bank itself. About half of that gap is filled by nearby small banks and credit unions stepping in, leaving a net community-wide decline of roughly 10% in small-business lending (about $453,000/year off a $4.7 million tract-level baseline), persisting for up to six years and cumulating to about $2.7 million in forgone loans per closed branch. The effect is highly localized — it fades out within about six miles — and mortgage lending is unaffected, because mortgage underwriting relies on hard, transferable data rather than the local "soft information" that drives small-business credit decisions. Effects are worse during recessions, in low- and moderate-income and minority neighborhoods, and are muted where fintech lending has a stronger local presence.

The headline number, and why two different figures (19% and 10%) both appear in the literature

Coverage of this topic (and some secondary summaries) cites both a '19%' figure and a roughly '9-10%' figure for the lending decline after a branch closes. These are not two competing estimates from different studies — they are the same paper's gross and net figures, and reporting only one without the other misstates the finding:

  • Gross effect (19%): new small-business loan originations from the closed bank's own branch network fall by $871,000/year against a $4.7M tract baseline — a 19% decline (Nguyen 2019, p.18-19, Table 7).
  • Substitution: nearby small banks absorb about 27% of that gross decline, and credit unions absorb a further ~21%, as borrowers redirect their search for credit.
  • Net effect (~10%): netting out that partial substitution leaves a residual decline of about 10% of baseline lending ($453,000/year) that is never replaced by any other lender — this is the number in the paper's abstract and headline result.

So the honest answer to "how much does lending fall" is: about a fifth of the closed bank's own lending disappears immediately, but roughly half of that gap is made up elsewhere in the local credit market, leaving the community about a tenth poorer in small-business credit on net, for years afterward.

How the effect was identified

Nguyen's identification strategy addresses the obvious confound: banks tend to close branches in areas that are already declining, which would bias a naive before/after comparison. She instruments for branch closings using within-county, tract-level variation in exposure to post-merger branch consolidation — i.e., which nearby tracts happened to have overlapping branches from the two merging banks, a source of variation plausibly unrelated to each tract's own economic trajectory. The data are tract-level CRA small-business lending disclosures (required of all banks with assets over $1 billion, covering an estimated 86% of the small-business lending market per Greenstone, Mas & Nguyen 2017) plus Call Report data for non-CRA-reporting banks and credit unions, and HMDA mortgage data as a placebo outcome (which shows no effect, supporting the soft-information mechanism rather than a generic local-demand shock).

Scale of the underlying exposure: how many communities this affects

This is not a rare event. NCRC's analysis of FDIC branch-location data found the U.S. lost 6,008 of 95,018 bank branches (about 6.5%) between 2008 and 2016, split roughly 82% urban / 18% rural. Losses were far from evenly distributed: several major metro counties lost 15-25% of their branches (Baltimore County -25.2%, Clark County/Las Vegas -17.4%, Philadelphia -18.7%, Wayne County/Detroit -15.8%, Marion County/Indianapolis -21.1%), and 86 new rural "banking deserts" (no branch within 10 miles) formed over the period, disproportionately in majority-minority and lower-income tracts. Separate Federal Reserve Bank of Chicago research (Toussaint-Comeau, Wang & Newberger, 2020) found that when banks — particularly community development financial institutions and minority depository institutions — close in low- and moderate-income (LMI) and minority neighborhoods, the resulting credit void persists for up to three years and is not automatically filled, because minority depository institutions originate a much higher share of their loans to LMI-neighborhood borrowers (38%) than non-MDI banks do (21%), meaning their closure removes a disproportionately targeted credit source.

Mechanism, mitigating factors, and boundary conditions

  • Why small-business lending and not mortgages: small-business credit decisions rely on 'soft information' — a loan officer's judgment built from a relationship with the borrower — that does not travel well once the branch and its staff are gone. Mortgage underwriting relies on standardized, verifiable data (income, credit score, collateral) that transfers easily to another lender, so Nguyen finds no significant effect on local mortgage originations from the same closings.
  • Geographic decay: the lending effect dissipates within about six miles of the closed branch — it is a local-market phenomenon, not a regional one.
  • Business-cycle sensitivity: the effect is worse during the 2008 financial crisis, when substitute lenders were themselves credit-constrained and less able to absorb displaced borrowers.
  • Fintech as a partial offset: more recent research ("When Banks Leave Town," a deposit-driven consolidation study) finds the local lending effect of closures is weaker in areas with greater fintech-lending penetration and weaker during credit booms, suggesting technology has begun to substitute for some of what branch-based soft information used to provide — though it has not eliminated the effect, consistent with Nguyen's broader finding that distance still matters for informationally intensive credit.
  • Real-economy consequence: the same research line finds that closing every branch in a census tract (about two branches, on average) reduces the local business entry (new-firm formation) rate by about one-quarter of a standard deviation — the credit contraction has a measurable effect on whether new businesses get started at all, not just on incumbent firms' loan renewals.

What AskAmerica's own data could and could not add

AskAmerica's catalog was searched for a bank-branch-location or closure table to attempt an independent replication (e.g., joining FDIC branch history to CRA/SBA lending by tract or county). None exists in this corpus — search_catalog returned no branch-location or Summary-of-Deposits table in any schema, and explicitly flagged this as a genuine coverage gap, pointing to the FDIC BankFind Suite API (api.fdic.gov) as the external source that would carry it. The corpus does hold fiscal.sba_loan_approvals (SBA 7(a)/504 loan approvals by borrower geography), but without a branch-closure event table there is no way to construct the before/after, closure-exposed-vs-unexposed comparison the question requires, so no original AskAmerica-data computation is reported here — the figures above are drawn entirely from the cited primary literature, each verified by fetching the source document directly rather than relying on secondary summaries.
