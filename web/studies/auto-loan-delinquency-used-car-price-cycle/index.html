Auto delinquency does not track used-car prices closely: the link is loose and lagged, and today's record 90+ day delinquency is a borrower-affordability story with modest, not acute, collateral risk

NY Fed Consumer Credit Panel (econ.household_debt_credit) vs BLS CPI used cars and trucks, SA (econ.new_vehicles_cpi), 2003Q1-2026Q3, quarterly

Answer

Not very closely. Across 2003Q1-2026Q3 (95 quarters) the year-over-year change in the CPI used-car index correlates only about -0.5 with the same-quarter change in the new-90+-day-delinquency flow, and only -0.44 with the year-over-year change in the 90+ day delinquent balance share. The sign is as expected (falling prices, more delinquency) but the strength swings by sub-period, and the biggest price swing on record (2020-22 surge, then a 15% fall) did not produce the delinquency move a collateral story predicts. Lenders are exposed, but mainly through borrower stress, not a collateral collapse: used-car prices are about 30% above 2019Q4 and only 15% below the 2022Q1 peak. Auto 90+ day delinquency (5.66% of balances in 2026Q3) is the highest in the 2003-2026 series, above the 2010Q4 post-crisis peak of 5.27%, while prices are flat to slightly down. Delinquency is rising with prices roughly flat, so affordability, high payments and subprime stress are the driver.

Pre-2020 the relationship is moderately negative and strongest when price declines lead delinquency by two to four quarters (2010-19 flow correlation -0.84), consistent with falling collateral values raising defaults on loans with little equity. It is not stable: some lag correlations change sign, and the 2020-21 stimulus and forbearance period (prices +30%, delinquency down) is a regime break. Correlations are descriptive, on small overlapping autocorrelated quarterly samples; none is a causal estimate and I ran no robust or leave-out checks.

Episodes: in 2008Q1-2009Q2 used-car prices fell about 12% (138.8 to 122.6) while the 90+ flow rose from 2.71% to 3.48%. After the 2022Q1 peak prices fell about 15% to 2026Q3 and the 90+ flow rose from 1.61% to 3.02% and balance share from 4.0% to 5.66%, but that also reflects normalization from artificially low pandemic-era delinquency, higher rates and payments, not just collateral.

Exclusion disclosure: the CPI queries excluded rows with period = 'M13' (BLS's annual-average row, a duplicate summary of the twelve monthly rows, not a month). Including it would have injected annual averages into the quarterly means; I did not rerun with it included.

Current exposure

  • Used-car CPI (SA) was 179.9 in October 2026 versus 186.3 at the November 2025 high; recent year-over-year changes are about -2% to -3%. A mild drift down, not a 2008-style fall (about -10%).
  • Delinquency: 90+ share of auto balances 5.66% (2026Q3, series high), flow into 90+ 3.02% (highest since 2010, below the 3.48% 2009Q2 peak), flow into 30+ 7.96%.
  • Collateral buffer: prices remain about 30% above pre-pandemic. Loans originated near the 2021-22 peak at high prices and long terms are the pocket most likely underwater, which the CPI index cannot see. Loan-level LTV and negative-equity data are not in the warehouse.
  • Outside context, not independently verified: Fitch reported the subprime auto ABS 60+ day delinquency index at a record 6.90% in January 2026 and recoveries near 33%, per Autoremarketing's summary (I read a search summary, not the Fitch release); the Philadelphia Fed has an April 2026 report on whether delinquency rates overstate distress (CFI report; not read).

Bottom line: weak subprime recoveries mean losses per default rise if prices fall further, so a 10%+ drop would hurt, but today's delinquency surge is mostly affordability. Watch used-car year-over-year changes, which lead delinquency by two to four quarters.

Limits

CPI used cars is a national retail price index, not the Manheim wholesale index lenders realize on repossession. The NY Fed series is all-lender, all credit tiers, balance-based. CPI was averaged by calendar quarter; correlations were computed in Python on values read from the tables. Windows: full overlap, 2010-19 (post-crisis, pre-pandemic) and 2022+ (post-surge) to show instability.
