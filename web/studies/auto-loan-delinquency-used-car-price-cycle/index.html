Auto delinquency and used-car prices decouple: a series-record 5.5-5.6% delinquency rate now coincides with prices still 17% below their 2022 peak

NY Fed Household Debt and Credit Report (Q2 2026) vs. BLS CPI Used Cars and Trucks, 2003-2026

Summary

Loosely, and with a roughly two-year lag whose sign flips across credit cycles -- it is not a tight, stable relationship. Full-sample quarterly correlation (2003Q1-2026Q2, n=90) between the auto 90+ day delinquency rate and used-car CPI year-over-year growth is a weak r=0.17 at zero lag, and it does not strengthen meaningfully at any lag out to four years; it peaks around r=-0.11 at a 2-year lag (price growth predicting lower future delinquency) and r=+0.24 at a 4-year lag. The two most informative episodes point in opposite directions: in 2008-2009 prices and delinquency rose together (recession squeezing both), while in 2021-2022 used-car prices spiked +37% and delinquency fell to a low (pandemic stimulus and loan-forbearance masked stress). The current period looks like the overdue unwind of that second episode: auto 90+ delinquency hit a series record 5.60% in 2026Q1 (above the 2010 Great Recession peak of 5.27%), even as used-car prices sit about 17% below their February 2022 peak. On exposure: yes, lenders that originated at 2021-2022 peak prices are carrying loans against collateral now worth materially less than at origination, and originations hit a nominal record $211B in Q2 2026 even as delinquency worsened -- a combination that concentrates collateral risk in recent vintages rather than reducing new exposure.

What the data show: two series, weakly linked

Delinquency -- the share of outstanding auto-loan balances 90+ days past due, from the New York Fed's Consumer Credit Panel/Equifax-based Quarterly Report on Household Debt and Credit (Q2 2026 vintage, quarterly, 2003Q1-2026Q2) -- rose from about 2.3% in 2003 to a Great Recession peak of 5.27% (2010Q4), fell to a cycle low of 3.14% (2014Q3), climbed steadily through the late 2010s to 5.05% just before the pandemic (2020Q1), fell to 3.73% by late 2022 as stimulus and forbearance suppressed defaults, and has since climbed past the old GFC peak to a new series record: 5.60% (2026Q1), easing only slightly to 5.49% (2026Q2).

Prices -- BLS CPI-U Used Cars and Trucks (NSA, series CUSR0000SETA02, retail rather than wholesale) -- was roughly flat in the 140s-150s index range for most of 2010-2020, then spiked to an all-time high near 217 by February 2022 on pandemic-era supply shortages, fell about 19% to a trough of 174.7 by August 2024, and has since partly rebounded to ~180 (August 2026) -- still about 17% below the 2022 peak.

A direct correlation of the two series' levels is r=0.32; of delinquency level against used-car CPI year-over-year growth it is r=0.17 contemporaneously, essentially zero at a 1-year lag, and only reaches -0.11 (the theoretically expected sign -- falling prices later followed by more delinquency) at roughly a 2-year lag before reversing again. In short: the corpus does not support a claim that auto delinquency "tracks" used-car prices in any simple, stable way. The relationship is regime-dependent -- it behaves one way in a demand-driven recession (2008-09, when both series worsen together) and another way in a supply-shock boom-bust (2021-2024, where price and delinquency move opposite directions with a multi-year lag as loans originated at peak prices season into delinquency).

The current setup: delinquency up, collateral value down from peak

The 2026 numbers put both halves of the mechanism in place for a genuine, if lagged, price-delinquency linkage: (1) a large cohort of auto loans was originated in 2021-2022 against vehicles priced near an all-time-high CPI level (~210-217), often at correspondingly high loan-to-value ratios; (2) used-car prices have since fallen materially from that peak (-19% trough-to-peak, still -17% versus peak as of August 2026); and (3) delinquency on that vintage is now surfacing at a series record. The NY Fed reports auto originations hit a nominal record $211 billion in Q2 2026, and total outstanding auto debt was $1.685 trillion (9.0% of total consumer debt) as of Q2 2026, against total household debt of $18.8 trillion.

For lenders, this combination matters through loss severity, not just default frequency: a repossessed vehicle now recovers less against the loan balance than it would have if collateral values had stayed near their 2022 peak, because many of those loans were written against inflated prices. That a genuine record origination volume is occurring at the same time delinquency is at a series high also means new exposure is not shrinking even as existing exposure deteriorates.

Data and methodology

Delinquency figures come from the NY Fed's Quarterly Report on Household Debt and Credit, "Percent of Balance 90+ Days Delinquent by Loan Type" (Page 12 Data), pulled directly from the underlying-data workbook for the August 2026 (2026Q2) release, covering 2003Q1-2026Q2. Price figures come from the BLS public API, series CUSR0000SETA02 (CPI-U, Used Cars and Trucks, seasonally unadjusted), pulled directly in three date-range calls covering 2003-2026. Correlations were computed in Python (numpy) on quarter-end-month CPI values matched to each NY Fed quarter, with used-car CPI year-over-year percent change tested at lags of 0-16 quarters against the contemporaneous/future delinquency reading.

Neither auto-loan delinquency nor a used-car price index exists as a loaded table in the AskAmerica corpus (search_catalog returned no match on "delinquency" and no wholesale/retail used-car price series), so both series were sourced directly from their primary federal/quasi-federal publishers rather than computed from this connector's own tables. The Manheim (Cox Automotive) wholesale used-vehicle index -- the series lenders themselves watch most closely for collateral recovery values -- was not accessible without a paid data license; CPI used-car prices are a reasonable retail proxy but move somewhat differently from wholesale auction prices, which is one limitation of this analysis.
