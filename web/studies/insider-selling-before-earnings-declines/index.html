Insiders sell more before bad news — but mostly months in advance, not right before the announcement

Literature review (goodwill-impairment insider trading, Marin & Olivier crash study) plus an original AskAmerica check on 2026 SEC data

Summary

How much insiders sell before bad news depends entirely on how far out you look. Months in advance (roughly 6 to 23 months before the bad news becomes public), insiders are meaningfully more likely to be net sellers of their own stock: in the best-documented academic study of this exact question, insiders were net sellers in 43-47% of firm-quarters ahead of a goodwill-impairment write-down (bad news), versus 36-37% for comparable firms with no such write-down — a gap of 6 to 10 percentage points, or roughly 15-25% relatively more likely, significant at p<0.01. But that gap collapses to nothing in the final weeks right before the announcement, because corporate insider-trading policies routinely impose a trading "blackout" window in the run-up to an earnings release. We confirmed this pattern independently: querying AskAmerica's own SEC Form 4 insider-transaction data alongside stock-price reactions to 2026 earnings releases, we found no statistically significant difference in the share of companies with any insider selling in the 30 days immediately before earnings that turned out badly (17.4%) versus other earnings (17.6%; chi-square p=0.84).

So: yes, insiders sell more before bad results — but the effect is a slow-building, months-out signal (roughly a 15-25% relatively higher chance of being a net seller), not something you'd see spike in the final days before the number actually comes out.

What the published research finds

The single most directly relevant study is Muller, Neamtiu & Riedl, "Insider Trading Preceding Goodwill Impairments" (Harvard Business School working paper 10-007, fetched directly from hbs.edu). It studies 612 firm-quarters that reported a goodwill impairment (a large, GAAP-mandated write-down that is unambiguously bad news, averaging 11.9% of market equity) under SFAS 142 between 2002 and 2007, benchmarked against 49,574 firm-quarters from otherwise-similar firms with no impairment in the same period.

  • 43.5%, 46.8%, and 42.9% of the bad-news (impairment) firms had insiders who were net sellers of stock in the windows 23-18, 17-12, and 11-6 months before the impairment was announced, respectively.
  • Only 36.4%, 36.4%, and 37.1% of the non-impairment (comparison) firms were net sellers over the same windows — a gap of 6-10 percentage points each time, significant at p<0.01 for both the means and the medians.
  • The gap disappears in the window immediately preceding the announcement (5 months prior through the announcement itself, window -5 to 0) — the authors explicitly report finding no significant difference in net insider selling there.
  • The selling is informative when it happens: firms whose insiders were net sellers ahead of the impairment saw an average abnormal stock return of -23.4% over the 18 months leading up to the write-down, versus only -10.5% for impairment firms where insiders were not net sellers.

This lines up with the broader academic literature. Marin & Olivier's landmark "The Dog That Did Not Bark: Insider Trading and Crashes" (Journal of Finance, 2008) finds that at the individual-stock level, insider sales peak many months before a large stock-price crash, while insider purchases peak only the month right before a large jump — an asymmetric pattern where sellers anticipate bad news earlier than buyers anticipate good news. Other work (e.g. the MDPI 2024 study on executive pay disparity and insider trading before earnings news) confirms insiders' pre-earnings trades are systematically informative about the direction of the coming surprise, though it does not provide a comparable single headline percentage.

What we checked directly in SEC data (2026)

To see whether this pattern shows up in the most current SEC filings, we built an original event study using AskAmerica's own connector, entirely independent of the papers above:

  1. Pulled every distinct earnings-related 8-K (Item 2.02) filing date for every company in 2026 from sec.earnings_transcripts — 9,858 distinct company-earnings events.
  2. Matched each event to the closing stock price on the trading day just before the filing and the closing price 1-5 trading days after, from sec.stock_prices, to compute a short-window announcement return — 5,942 events had usable price data on both sides.
  3. Classified an event as "bad results" if the stock fell 5% or more in that window (1,667 events, 28% of the sample) and everything else as "other" (4,275 events).
  4. For every event, summed insider Form 4/5 sale transactions (sec.insider_transactions, transaction code S, disposed shares) in the 30 calendar days immediately before the filing date, for insiders at that same company.

Result: 17.4% of "bad results" events had any insider selling in the preceding 30 days, versus 17.6% of "other" events — statistically indistinguishable (chi-square = 0.039, p = 0.84, n = 5,942). A parallel comparison of the average dollar value sold (conditional and unconditional) was also not significant (Welch's t-test, p = 0.62; the point estimate was actually higher before bad results, $11.1M vs $6.0M average per event, but with heavy right-skew and no significance at this sample size).

This null result for a close-in, 30-day window is exactly what the goodwill-impairment study above would predict: it also found no gap in its closest-in window (-5 to 0 months). The most likely mechanical reason is insider-trading blackout policies — the vast majority of US public companies bar officers and directors from trading in the weeks immediately surrounding a quarterly earnings release, regardless of whether the news will be good or bad, which mechanically suppresses any signal in exactly the window we could test with 30-day resolution.

Data quality note and scope limits

Coverage gap: AskAmerica's sec.insider_transactions and sec.earnings_transcripts tables both declare a 2010-2026 ingest window, but a full row scan showed both tables are populated almost entirely with 2026 rows only (insider_transactions: 477,966 of 477,966 scanned rows are from 2026; earnings_transcripts: 68,210 of 68,256 rows are from 2026, with only 23 rows each in 2024 and 2025, and none in 2010-2023). This is why our own check above is confined to 2026 and cannot replicate the goodwill-impairment study's multi-year, multi-decade design — it is a single-year cross-section, not a historical panel. We reported this gap to the connector's maintainers via report_issue.

Data defect found and excluded: 503 rows in sec.insider_transactions carry implausible price_per_share values (up to $12.5 million per share, implying single trades worth trillions of dollars) — almost certainly a Form-4 parsing bug for a small number of filers. These rows were excluded from our dollar-value comparison (filtered to price_per_share between $0 and $100,000); the incidence comparison (any selling yes/no) is unaffected since it does not depend on the dollar magnitude.

Proxy limitation: our own check defines "bad results" by the stock's short-window price reaction to the earnings release, not by an analyst-estimate-based earnings surprise (no analyst-estimates table exists in this connector) — a reasonable, commonly used proxy, but not identical to the goodwill-impairment study's definition (a mandated accounting write-down) or to a consensus-EPS-miss definition used elsewhere in the literature.
