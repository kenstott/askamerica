Rural America's health penalty is real, has grown for two decades, and is mostly a within-state gap, not a between-state one

County Health Rankings (CHR) premature-death rates joined to USDA Rural-Urban Continuum Codes, plus published mortality/life-expectancy literature, 2000-2026

Summary

Rural America is measurably and increasingly less healthy than urban America. In the most recent County Health Rankings data, rural (nonmetro) counties average 11,051 years of potential life lost before age 75 per 100,000 people, versus 9,359 in metro counties — rural counties lose about 18% more years of life to premature death. Published research tracking this gap back further shows it is not a snapshot artifact: it barely existed in 1969, opened into a ~2-year life-expectancy gap by 2009, and by 2019 the excess natural-cause death rate for rural 25-54 year-olds had grown to 43% above the urban rate (up from just 6% in 1999) — a genuine, widening 20+ year trend, not noise.

The more interesting finding is about WHERE the gap lives. Decomposing county-level premature-death rates, only about 29% of the total county-to-county variation is explained by which state a county sits in — the other 71% is variation among counties inside the same state. And the rural penalty itself is not an artifact of rural counties being concentrated in already-unhealthy states: controlling for state (a fixed-effects regression that compares rural to urban counties within the same state) leaves the rural penalty unchanged or slightly larger (1,797 vs. 1,692 YPLL/100k), and the rural-worse-off direction holds in 43 of 47 states with both county types. So the rural health disadvantage is overwhelmingly a within-state phenomenon — a Texas, Georgia, or California rural county trails its own state's cities — even though the SIZE of that gap varies enormously by state (from essentially zero or reversed in a handful of states to over 7,000 YPLL/100k in the worst).

How large is the rural-urban health gap, and has it been growing for 20 years?

Current gap (askamerica, CHR 2025 release joined to USDA RUCC): Averaging health.chr_premature_death (County Health Rankings' age-adjusted years-of-potential-life-lost-before-75 measure) across all 3,080 U.S. counties with both a mortality and rurality record, nonmetro counties average 11,051 YPLL/100k vs. 9,359 in metro counties (a simple 2-group OLS gives a coefficient of +1,692, p<0.001, R²=0.05 — rurality alone explains only a modest share of all county variation, which is expected since health outcomes have many drivers). Breaking rurality into USDA's full 9-point continuum shows a fairly monotonic gradient: the most urban counties (RUCC 1) average 8,571, and the most remote rural counties (RUCC 9) average 11,246 — with the very worst code (8, rural-but-metro-adjacent) at 11,393.

The 20-year trend (published literature, since this connector's premature-death table carries only the current release, not a historical county series broken out by rurality): Multiple independent lines of evidence agree the gap has been widening for decades, not just present today.

  • In 1969 there was no significant urban-rural life-expectancy gap. A gap opened in the 1990s; by 2009 it was a statistically significant ~2 years (78.8 urban vs. 76.8 rural).
  • From 1999-2009, urban life expectancy grew 1.19 more years (women) and 0.86 more years (men) than rural; from 2010-2019 rural counties saw absolute DECLINES in life expectancy (-0.20 women, -0.30 men) while urban counties still gained.
  • For working-age adults (25-54), the rural excess natural-cause mortality rate grew from just 6% above urban in 1999 to 43% above urban by 2019 (CDC data, reported by CBS News/Univ. of Arizona Center for Rural Health).
  • A peer-reviewed decomposition (Spencer et al. 2018, Health Services Research, using CDC mortality 1980-2010) found the growing divide is driven by CHANGES IN THE RETURNS to county characteristics over time (e.g., education and income mattering more for urban health outcomes each decade), not by rural and urban counties simply having different characteristics — meaning the gap has a structural, self-reinforcing driver rather than a fixed, one-time cause.

Is the gap mostly between states, or between places inside the same state?

This is the sharper and less commonly asked half of the question, and the askamerica data gives a clean, decomposable answer.

Variance decomposition. Splitting the total sum-of-squares of county premature-death rates (n=3,080 counties) into a between-state component (variance of each state's mean) and a within-state component (variance of counties around their own state's mean): the between-state sum of squares is 13.1 billion against a total of 45.2 billion — 29% between-state, 71% within-state. Most of the difference in how healthy a county is has nothing to do with which state it is in; it is about which COUNTY inside that state.

Does controlling for state explain away the rural penalty? No — if anything the opposite. A plain rural-vs-urban regression gives a coefficient of +1,692 YPLL/100k. Adding state fixed effects (comparing rural counties only to urban counties in the SAME state, i.e., asking the within-state question directly) gives +1,797 — essentially unchanged, or slightly larger. If the rural penalty were mainly a between-state story (rural population concentrated in states that are unhealthy for other reasons — poverty, policy, climate), the coefficient would have shrunk sharply once state was held fixed. It did not. The rural disadvantage shows up county-by-county inside the same state, not just state-by-state.

But the SIZE of the gap is very state-dependent. Computing each state's own rural-minus-urban premature-death gap separately (47 states with both county types) gives a mean gap of 1,784 YPLL/100k but a standard deviation of 1,456 — ranging from about -1,260 (a few states, e.g. parts of the Mountain West, where urban counties actually fare worse) up to +7,131 in the worst-affected state. The DIRECTION is consistent — rural counties are worse off in 43 of the 47 states (91%) — but the MAGNITUDE of that disadvantage differs by a factor of several-fold depending on the state. So: the existence of a rural penalty is a within-state phenomenon found almost everywhere; the severity of that penalty is shaped by state-level context (rural health infrastructure, Medicaid expansion status, industry mix, demographics).

Data and method notes

What askamerica computed directly: the current rural/urban gap and its RUCC gradient (health.chr_premature_death joined to geo.rural_urban_continuum, 3,080 counties), the OLS and state-fixed-effects regressions, the between/within-state variance decomposition, and the per-state gap distribution — all reproducible via the SQL and tool calls cited below.

What came from outside literature, and why: askamerica's CHR premature-death table carries only the current (2025) release with no historical vintage, so a genuine 20-year county-level rural/urban time series is not available from this connector. The 20-year trend narrative above is drawn from CDC mortality data as analyzed and reported in peer-reviewed and CDC-sourced sources (cited below) — it is not an askamerica-computed figure, and is labeled as such.

Caveats: CHR's premature-death measure (years of life lost before age 75) is a truncated mortality measure, not a full all-ages age-adjusted rate — appropriate for relative comparisons like this one but not identical to a life-expectancy figure. USDA RUCC classification is from the 2022/2023 vintage (most recent available; RUCC updates only once per decade), so the "rural" label reflects current classification applied to current mortality data — a genuinely joint 2005-era rural classification with 2005-era mortality was not available. DC and a handful of very small-county states were excluded or noted separately where they would distort a state-level average.

  8. AskAmerica: state fixed-effects regression of premature-death rate on rural indicator (within-state estimate)
    Show tool call
    panel_fixed_effects(outcome="rate", predictors=["rural"], entity_col="state_fips", time_col="t")
  9. AskAmerica: between-state vs within-state variance decomposition of county premature-death rates
    Show SQL
    WITH county AS (SELECT p.premature_death_rate rate, p.state_fips FROM health.chr_premature_death p JOIN geo.rural_urban_continuum r ON p.fips_code=r.county_fips WHERE p.geo_level='county' AND p.premature_death_rate IS NOT NULL), state_means AS (SELECT state_fips, AVG(rate) sm, COUNT(*) n FROM county GROUP BY state_fips), grand AS (SELECT AVG(rate) gm, COUNT(*) N FROM county) SELECT (SELECT SUM((rate-(SELECT gm FROM grand))*(rate-(SELECT gm FROM grand))) FROM county) AS total_ss, (SELECT SUM(n*(sm-(SELECT gm FROM grand))*(sm-(SELECT gm FROM grand))) FROM state_means) AS between_state_ss
  10. AskAmerica: distribution of the rural-minus-urban gap across 47 states
    Show SQL
    WITH county AS (SELECT p.premature_death_rate rate, p.state_fips, CASE WHEN r.metro_nonmetro='Nonmetro' THEN 1 ELSE 0 END rural FROM health.chr_premature_death p JOIN geo.rural_urban_continuum r ON p.fips_code=r.county_fips WHERE p.geo_level='county' AND p.premature_death_rate IS NOT NULL), gap_by_state AS (SELECT state_fips, AVG(CASE WHEN rural=1 THEN rate END) - AVG(CASE WHEN rural=0 THEN rate END) AS gap, COUNT(CASE WHEN rural=1 THEN 1 END) n_rural, COUNT(CASE WHEN rural=0 THEN 1 END) n_metro FROM county GROUP BY state_fips) SELECT COUNT(*) n_states, AVG(gap) avg_gap, STDDEV_SAMP(gap) sd_gap, MIN(gap) min_gap, MAX(gap) max_gap, SUM(CASE WHEN gap>0 THEN 1 ELSE 0 END) n_positive FROM gap_by_state WHERE n_rural>0 AND n_metro>0

The 20-year historical trend is drawn from published CDC-based research, since AskAmerica's premature-death table carries only the current release, not a rurality-tagged historical series.
