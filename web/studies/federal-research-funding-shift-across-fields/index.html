Life sciences' share of federal research funding fell from 52% to 41% over the last 20 years, and its real dollars actually shrank

FY2005-FY2024, NCSES Survey of Federal Funds for R&D via AAAS's compiled historical series; current and CPI-U-real 2024 dollars

Summary

Federal research money has shifted markedly away from the life sciences over the last twenty fiscal years (FY2005-FY2024), even though life sciences remains the single largest field. Life sciences' share of total federal research obligations fell from 52.3% in FY2005 to 40.8% in FY2024, an 11.5-percentage-point drop, and in inflation-adjusted (2024) dollars its funding actually declined about 10.9% over the period. Meanwhile psychology, social science, math/computer science, and physical sciences all grew their shares and grew substantially in real dollars (psychology +64% real, physical sciences +47% real, math/computer science +32% real). A large 'Other fields' category also grew sharply, partly reflecting NCSES's 2021 field-of-R&D taxonomy revision rather than new spending alone. Overall federal research obligations grew 83.6% in nominal dollars over 20 years but only 14.3% in real (CPI-U-adjusted) terms — most of the apparent 20-year growth in the federal research budget is inflation, not an expansion of real research capacity.

Data and method

The askengine connector's own field-of-R&D table, research.nsf_rd_by_field, is built from the same NCSES Survey of Federal Funds for R&D but is only loaded for FY2016-FY2025 (and FY2025 is only partially loaded — 10 of ~49 field rows). That is well short of the 'last twenty years' the question asks about, so per this connector's own escalation guidance for a confirmed coverage gap, I fetched the primary compiled series directly: AAAS's Historical R&D Data workbook (Historical Data.xlsx, linked from its Federal R&D Budget Dashboard page, itself sourced from NCSES's Federal Funds for R&D survey), which carries a 'Field' sheet with annual federal research obligations by field of R&D from 1970 through FY2024 (current dollars, USD millions). I read that sheet directly (8 fields: Life Science, Physical Science, Environmental Science, Math/Computer Science, Engineering, Social Science, Psychology, Other) and computed FY2005 vs FY2024 shares and, via the connector's adjust_inflation tool (BLS CPI-U annual averages), real 20-year growth by field.

Cross-check: I also queried research.nsf_rd_by_field directly for the years it does hold (2016 and 2024, broad-field level). It shows the same directional story over its shorter window: Life sciences up only 20.5% nominal (32,045 -> 38,622 $M) while Physical sciences nearly doubled (6,706 -> 12,759 $M) and Psychology more than doubled (2,021 -> 5,286 $M) — consistent with, and corroborating, the AAAS-sourced 20-year finding above. The connector table uses NCSES's post-2021 field labels (e.g. 'Computer and information sciences' rather than the older 'Math/Computer Science' rollup), so the two sources are not a line-for-line match, but they agree on direction and magnitude.

The shares track a mostly monotonic trend across the window (see the dashboard line chart at 2005/2008/2011/2014/2017/2020/2024 checkpoints): life sciences drifted down from the low-50s% through most of the period before a sharper drop into 2024; physical sciences and 'Other' both stepped up notably in the final years shown, partly coincident with the post-2021 field-of-R&D taxonomy revision, which changed how some sub-fields are bucketed and can shift a few points of share mechanically rather than through new appropriations.

Caveats

  • This is federal research obligations (basic + applied), not development — NCSES does not publish a comparable field-of-R&D breakdown for development spending, so this excludes a large share of total federal R&D (e.g. most DOD RDT&E is development, not research, and is not represented in these field totals).
  • The 'Other' category's outsized growth (+201% real) partly reflects NCSES's 2021 field taxonomy revision reclassifying some sub-fields rather than a genuine 3x real increase in spending on a stable set of activities; treat that figure as a mix of real growth and classification change, not pure growth.
  • All figures are current-year obligations as compiled by AAAS from the same underlying NCSES survey the connector's own research.nsf_rd_by_field table draws on; AAAS's workbook was the only way to reach the full 20-year window since the connector's own table is loaded only for FY2016-2025.
  • CPI-U (the general urban consumer price index) is used as the deflator; it is not R&D-specific and may understate true research cost inflation (e.g. NIH's Biomedical Research and Development Price Index has historically outpaced CPI-U), which would make the real decline in life sciences funding somewhat understated here, if anything.
