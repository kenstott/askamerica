Federal research funding has grown only modestly since 2005, but the mix has shifted sharply from life sciences toward physical sciences, psychology, and social sciences

NSF NCSES Survey of Federal Funds for R&D, FY2005 vs FY2025, real 2024 dollars; NCSES National Patterns for economy-wide context

Summary

Between FY2005 and FY2025, total federal obligations for research (basic+applied, all agencies, all performers) rose from about $53.7 billion to about $95.2 billion in nominal dollars — but in constant 2024 dollars that is only a +7.5% real increase over twenty years ($86.3B to $92.8B), because most of the nominal growth was inflation. What moved far more than the total is the mix across fields. Life sciences (still funded mostly through NIH) fell from 52% to 39% of the federal research portfolio — the single largest compositional shift of any field. That share migrated mainly toward physical sciences (10% to 13%), Psychology (3.5% to 5.5%), Social sciences (2.0% to 3.2%), and a residual 'other fields' category (3.9% to 9.3%, partly a taxonomy-revision artifact — see caveats). Engineering held roughly steady (16% to 17%) and environmental/geosciences slipped slightly (6.5% to 6.2%). Separately, and arguably the larger story: the federal government's share of ALL U.S. research funding (not just its own portfolio mix) fell from 45% in 2010 to 34% in 2022, as business R&D funding grew faster than federal funding economy-wide (NCSES National Patterns of R&D Resources).

Data and method

The askamerica connector's research.nsf_rd_by_field table carries NCSES's all-agency, all-performer field-of-R&D obligations for research only, but its declared/observed coverage only reaches back to FY2016 — a 10-year window, not 20. To reach a genuine 20-year (FY2005-FY2025) comparison, this report pulled NCSES's own historical Table 133 ('Federal obligations for research, by detailed field of science and engineering: FYs 1995-2005') directly from NCSES's archived data-table site (ncsesdata.nsf.gov, preserved via the Internet Archive after NCSES retired the old table-numbering scheme) for the FY2005 baseline, and used the connector's FY2025 rows for the endpoint. Both are the same underlying NCSES Survey of Federal Funds for Research and Development, so the two years are methodologically comparable, though NCSES revised its field-of-R&D taxonomy in 2021: the FY2005 'Computer sciences and mathematics' and 'Environmental sciences' categories were relabeled 'Computer and information sciences' (split out from mathematics and statistics) and 'Geosciences, atmospheric, and ocean sciences' respectively. This report recombines the post-2021 Computer/Math split back into one bucket and treats Environmental sciences and Geo/atmo/ocean sciences as the same field for a like-for-like comparison. Dollar figures were converted to constant 2024 dollars using BLS CPI-U annual averages (195.3 for 2005; 313.69 for 2024) since the connector's own CPI table (econ.inflation_metrics) only loads 2009 onward and could not deflate the 2005 figure itself — the 2005 CPI-U value was sourced externally from BLS's own published historical CPI-U series.

Life sciences is the only field to see an absolute real-dollar decline over the twenty years, despite still being nearly 40% of the portfolio. Every other field grew in real terms, with physical sciences, psychology, and social sciences all growing 38-69% in real dollars, and the residual 'other fields nec' bucket growing fastest of all — though that category's size and definition shifted materially in the 2021 NCSES taxonomy revision, so part of its apparent growth is a reclassification artifact rather than a real funding shift, and should be treated as the least reliable single number in this table.

Why life sciences fell as a share even as its dollar total is still huge

Life sciences' share was cited by NCSES's own S&E Indicators series as 'about half the federal research portfolio since 2001,' consistent with the FY2005 finding here (52%). The subsequent decline to 39% by FY2025 lines up with two things visible in the wider literature: (1) NIH's early-2000s budget doubling (completed 2003) had already pushed life sciences to a historic peak by FY2005, so reversion toward the mean was mechanically likely; and (2) NCSES's own commentary on the most recent decade specifically flags psychology and the social sciences (from a low base) as the fastest-growing shares, and environmental sciences as a field that saw comparative decline — both directionally corroborated by the figures in this report, though this analysis finds environmental/geosciences roughly flat rather than sharply declining over the full 20-year window (the decline NCSES describes appears concentrated in the most recent decade, not the full 2005-2025 span).

The bigger context: federal government is a shrinking share of ALL U.S. research funding

A shift in which fields the federal government funds is only part of the story. NCSES National Patterns of R&D Resources data show that the federal government's share of total U.S. research funding — across all funders, not just within its own portfolio — fell from 45% in 2010 to 34% in 2022, because business-sector research funding grew faster (38% to 50% of the total) than federal funding did. In other words, even fields where federal dollars grew in real terms (physical sciences, engineering, computer science) may be seeing the federal government supply a shrinking share of that field's total research funding if business R&D in that field grew even faster — a question this report's field-level federal data alone cannot answer, since it does not include non-federal funding by field.

Caveats

  • The FY2025 figures from NCSES's most recent Survey of Federal Funds cycle are described by NCSES as preliminary/estimated for the latest year(s); revisions are typical.
  • The 'Other fields (nec)' category's 158% real growth is the number in this analysis least trustworthy at face value: NCSES's 2021 field-of-R&D taxonomy revision changed what falls into residual/other buckets, so some of this growth is definitional rather than a real dollar shift.
  • This is federal RESEARCH obligations only (basic+applied), not federal R&D as a whole — experimental development obligations are not broken out by field in NCSES's own field-of-R&D crosscut and are excluded from every figure in this report.
  • Figures are federal-agency obligations by field of R&D, not by performing sector; they do not show whether the money went to universities, federal labs, or contractors.
