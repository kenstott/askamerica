Federal fires burn roughly 5-6x more acres per fire than state/local/private fires

NICC Wildland Fire Summary and Statistics, 2018-2022, as compiled in CRS IF10244 (updated June 1, 2023)

Summary

Federally managed land burns in much bigger individual fires than state or private land does, even though far fewer fires start there. Using NICC data compiled in the Congressional Research Service's Wildfire Statistics brief (IF10244), the average wildfire on federal land in 2018-2022 burned about 378 acres, versus about 62 acres on nonfederal (state, local, and private) land — federal fires ran roughly 6 times larger on average over this period. A separate, longer-run CRS/NICC comparison covering 1991-2021 puts the same multiplier at about 5x (225 acres average on federal land vs. 41 acres on nonfederal land). Despite this, nonfederal land accounts for the large majority of individual fire starts (about 78% of fires, 2018-2022) because it is where most people live, work, and cause ignitions; federal land, concentrated in the sparsely populated West and Alaska, has fewer, larger, harder-to-reach fires. As a result, federal land's SHARE of total acres burned nationally fluctuates a lot year to year (52%-73% in 2018-2022) depending on where the largest individual fires happen to land in a given season.

Over 2018-2022, federal agencies (Forest Service plus Department of the Interior units) accounted for about 21.5% of all wildfires but 62.7% of all acres burned nationally, while nonfederal land accounted for about 78.5% of fires but only 37.3% of acres. In 2022 specifically, 52% of the year's burned acreage was on federal land (2.1 million acres on DOI land, 1.9 million on Forest Service land) versus a 64% federal share in the average year over the preceding decade — 2022 was actually a below-average federal-share year, driven partly by a large nonfederal fire season in Alaska.

Geography drives most of this: in the West (Alaska, AZ, CA, CO, ID, MT, NM, NV, OR, UT, WA, WY), 64% of acres burned in 2022 were on federal land, since federal ownership is concentrated there (the Forest Service manages 193 million acres nationally and DOI manages 400+ million acres, much of it in the West). In the East, only 15% of acres burned were on federal land in 2022 — 85% were nonfederal, because federal land ownership is comparatively sparse east of the Rockies.

Why federal fires burn bigger

The CRS brief and NICC data point to a structural explanation rather than a difference in fire behavior itself: federal land is disproportionately remote, large-tract wildland (national forests, BLM rangeland, national parks, wildlife refuges, Alaska) where fires often burn longer before detection and suppression, and where full suppression is sometimes not even attempted for fires in wilderness areas far from people or property. Nonfederal land is more likely to be near development, so initial-attack suppression resources are dispatched faster and fires are kept smaller on average — but there are vastly more ignition opportunities (roads, power lines, agriculture, arson, escaped debris burns), which is why 78-83% of all fire starts happen there even though they burn a minority of total acreage.

Data and methodology notes

The AskAmerica connector's disasters.wildfire_perimeters table (NIFC WFIGS perimeters, ~39,000 fire records with acres and fire_cause) does NOT carry a land-ownership/jurisdiction column, so this specific federal-vs-nonfederal breakdown cannot be computed directly from that table — it was checked and confirmed absent via describe_table. This gap should be logged as a coverage limitation for future ownership-based wildfire questions. The figures above instead come from the primary NICC (National Interagency Coordination Center) annual Wildland Fire Summary and Statistics reports, as compiled and tabulated in the Congressional Research Service's regularly updated two-page brief, Wildfire Statistics (IF10244, version 67, updated June 1, 2023) — the report's own Table 1 and Figure 3/4 were read directly via PDF fetch, not a secondary summary. The per-fire averages (368, 284, 493, 371, 342, and 62-90 acres/fire) were computed by dividing CRS's own reported acres-burned by fires-count for each ownership category and year; CRS does not itself publish the per-fire average, only the underlying fires and acres counts, so this ratio is our calculation from their primary figures, cross-checked against CRS's own independently stated 1991-2021 finding that federal fires average 225 acres versus 41 acres nonfederal (a ~5.5x ratio) — the two independent windows agree in direction and rough magnitude (~5x-6x), which corroborates the result rather than being an artifact of the shorter window chosen.
