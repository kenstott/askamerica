Answer

Yes — a safety-violation pattern is showing up, but it is concentrated in a specific slice of reshored plants (EV battery and semiconductor fabs), not a broad move in national manufacturing injury statistics.

What the AskAmerica warehouse can and can't say: the corpus was searched (search_catalog) for OSHA/workplace-safety data across all 40+ schemas, and "osha" was an unmatched term with no table, column, or description carrying it — there is no OSHA inspection, violation, or injury-rate table loaded in this connector. The closest available tables (transport.fmcsa_carriers safety ratings, health.cms_hospital_quality safety measures, environment.drinking_water_violations) are all in unrelated domains. This is a genuine, confirmed data gap, not a query miss — nothing below is a warehouse-derived number; every figure is sourced to public reporting and to OSHA/BLS's own published releases.

National baseline: no aggregate spike

BLS's Survey of Occupational Injuries and Illnesses shows manufacturing's overall nonfatal injury/illness rate falling or flat in the most recent release — private industry TRC dropped from 2.4 to 2.3 per 100 FTE workers between 2023 and 2024, and manufacturing was one of the sectors whose rate declined. So at the sector-wide level, the reshoring boom has not shown up as a national uptick in manufacturing injury rates.

But new, reshored facilities are a hot spot

The pattern is concentrated, and it recurs in two industries central to the tariff/reshoring push — EV batteries and semiconductor fabs — plus general new-plant construction:

  • EV battery plants: OSHA and state partners cited EV battery manufacturers for 47 violations in 2024 (through the time of reporting), already above the 44 in 2023, and a 147% jump from 19 in 2022 — a direct measure of rising enforcement activity tracking the industry's buildout (Bloomberg Law/Andrew Wallender). Specific cases:
  • Ultium Cells (Warren, Ohio; GM–LG Energy Solution JV, opened Aug. 2022): a March 2023 explosion, $270,000 in fines after 19 violations found, and a local fire chief reporting new injury types (hydrofluoric-acid burns) "rarely seen" at the old GM plant it replaced.
  • SK Battery America (Commerce, Georgia): cited twice in under a year, including after an October 2023 lithium battery fire exposed at least 18 workers to hydrofluoric-acid vapor; DOL found the in-house fire department unprepared.
  • HL-GA Battery Co. (Bryan County, Georgia; $4.3B Hyundai–LG Energy Solution JV, under construction): two fatal forklift accidents within two months (March 21 and May 20, 2025), prompting active OSHA investigation.

  • Tesla: OSHA ruled in Feb. 2025 that safety violations contributed to a worker's electrocution death (Aug. 2024); a separate 2023 conveyor-belt incident seriously injured a worker.

  • Semiconductor fabs (CHIPS Act-driven reshoring): at TSMC's Phoenix, Arizona fab, a tanker-truck driver died in May 2024 after an over-pressurized waste-sulfuric-acid tanker hatch blew off; Arizona's OSHA-equivalent (ADOSH) fined TSMC $16,131 and found hazards including over-pressurization, harmful-material exposure, and falling-debris risk; OSHA had received prior safety complaints before the fatality. A separate construction worker suffered broken bones at the same site in June 2025, and 28 current/former employees have sued over alleged hostile-work-environment and safety issues.

  • Structural/regulatory context: Bloomberg Law reported OSHA reorganized its Southern regional structure (new Birmingham, Ala. office, splitting the South into three regions instead of two) explicitly citing the expanding industrial base from plants like Tesla's Texas factory and SK Battery America — i.e., the regulator itself is responding to a real regional load increase. At the same time, the AFL-CIO's "Death on the Job 2026" report and other coverage note federal OSHA inspector counts are near historic lows (roughly 1,850 federal/state compliance officers for ~130 million workers, one per ~70,000 workers), with the agency emphasizing outreach over enforcement — meaning the violations being caught are likely a floor, not a ceiling, on the true rate.

Why this looks new-plant-specific rather than "manufacturing in general"

Industry sources attribute the pattern to specific mechanics of standing up a brand-new plant fast: unfamiliar machinery and chemistries (lithium-ion battery production, in particular, uses hundreds of chemicals with few OSHA-specific exposure limits), inexperienced workforces with no institutional safety culture yet, compressed construction timelines running heavy equipment (forklifts, cranes) alongside process buildout, and reliance on subcontractors whose safety practices aren't yet integrated with the site. A battery-industry veteran quoted by Bloomberg Law called this "predictable for a US industry in its infancy."

Bottom line

There is no evidence of a rising national manufacturing safety-violation rate tied to tariffs/reshoring in the aggregate BLS data. But at the plant level, the newly opened or under-construction facilities most associated with reshoring/tariff policy — EV battery plants and chip fabs — are showing a real, citation-documented and in some cases fatal safety problem, concentrated in the first 1–2 years of a site's life, running well ahead of the shrinking federal inspection capacity available to catch it.

Data gap disclosed: AskAmerica's own schemas carry no OSHA inspection/violation/injury dataset; this answer is built entirely from public reporting and OSHA/DOL/BLS's own published releases, not from a warehouse query.
