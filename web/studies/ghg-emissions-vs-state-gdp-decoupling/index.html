41 of 50 states cut CO2 emissions while growing GDP, 2005-2016 — the last year the U.S. published a full state-level carbon-intensity series

EIA State Energy Data System emissions (2005-2016, last published Feb 2019) vs. BEA real state GDP (chained 2017$); EPA GHGRP large-facility data used only as a 2016-2023 directional check

Summary

Between 2005 and 2016 — the last year for which the U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA) published a full state-by-state carbon-intensity series before discontinuing it — the average U.S. state cut CO2 emitted per dollar of economic output by about a quarter (national average carbon intensity fell from 414 to 309 metric tons of CO2 per million dollars of real GDP, a 25.4% decline). Every one of the 50 states reduced carbon intensity over that period. But intensity can fall even while total emissions rise, if GDP grows fast enough — so a sharper question is which states actually cut absolute emissions while the economy kept growing. Combining EIA's own state emissions series with BEA's state real-GDP series, 41 of the 50 states genuinely decoupled: emissions fell in absolute terms while real GDP rose. The steepest carbon-intensity cuts were in New Hampshire (-41%), North Dakota (-43%), Maryland (-39%), Alaska (-37%), and Massachusetts (-36%). Nine states did not decouple: Texas, Washington, North Dakota, Nebraska, South Dakota, Idaho, Arkansas, and Mississippi all had rising absolute emissions (mostly energy-producing/agricultural states with fast-growing extraction, manufacturing, or data-center-adjacent activity), and Louisiana's emissions rose while its real GDP fell (oil-price-driven, 2014-16 downturn). A directional check using EPA's facility-level GHGRP data for 2016-2023 (large industrial/power facilities only, not full economy-wide emissions) shows the broad pattern continuing — emissions fell at 39 of 50 states' large facilities — though Texas's large-facility emissions rose sharply (+37%) on LNG and petrochemical buildout.

Why 2005-2016, and what the number is

Window chosen because that is what exists. EIA's State-Level Energy-Related Carbon Dioxide Emissions series is the only U.S. government product that computes economy-wide (not just large-facility) state CO2 emissions and pairs them with state GDP into a carbon-intensity figure. EIA published this analysis in five-ish-year snapshots and discontinued it after the February 2019 release covering 2005-2016 (its own release note reads 'Next release date: Discontinued'). No newer vintage of this specific series exists publicly. Reporting the change over any other window would mean fabricating data EIA never published.

Carbon intensity of the economy = metric tons of energy-related CO2 divided by a state's real GDP (EIA's own table used chained-2009 dollars; BEA's current vintage uses chained-2017 dollars after rebasing). It combines two effects: how much energy an economy uses per dollar of output (energy intensity) and how carbon-heavy that energy supply is (carbon intensity of the fuel mix). Energy-related CO2 covers direct fuel combustion in residential, commercial, industrial, and transportation use plus fuel burned for electricity generation, attributed to the state where combustion happens (not where the electricity is consumed) — this differs from EPA GHGRP, which covers only large individual facilities (mostly power plants and heavy industry) required to report to the Greenhouse Gas Reporting Program, and misses transportation, homes, and small businesses entirely.

Method: how the decoupling classification was built

EIA's own Table 9 (carbon intensity by state, 2005-2016) was read directly from its published PDF. EIA's Table 1 (absolute state emissions, million metric tons CO2, 2005-2016) was read the same way. Real state GDP for 2005 and 2016 was pulled from BEA's SAGDP1 (real GDP, chained 2017 dollars) via this connector's econ.state_gdp table. A state was classified 'decoupled' when absolute emissions fell AND real GDP rose over 2005-2016 — the only classification that actually answers 'cut emissions while still growing,' since carbon-intensity decline alone is compatible with emissions rising (e.g., Texas, Washington, North Dakota: emissions rose but GDP rose even faster, so intensity still fell).

Vintage mismatch, disclosed rather than papered over: EIA's own published intensity figures use chained-2009-dollar GDP as of their 2019 analysis; BEA has since rebased its real-GDP series to chained-2017 dollars and revised the historical figures. Recomputing intensity change from (EIA emissions) / (current BEA real GDP) gives numbers close to but not identical to EIA's own published percentages — for most states within 1-3 percentage points, but for states with near-zero real GDP growth over the window (Connecticut, Michigan, Nevada) the sign of the *implied* GDP change extracted algebraically from EIA's own rounded intensity table flipped versus the actual (now-revised) BEA figure. That is why the decoupling classification above uses BEA's real GDP directly (not backed out from EIA's rounded intensity ratios) — it is the more reliable of the two paths, and the two disagree only on a handful of near-zero-growth states, not on the headline count.

Post-2016 sanity check (large facilities only, not the full picture)

EIA's state carbon-intensity series was discontinued after 2016 and no equivalent federal replacement exists. As a partial, imperfect check on whether the pattern continued, EPA's Greenhouse Gas Reporting Program (GHGRP) — which captures only large individual facilities (power plants, refineries, major industrial sites above an emissions threshold), not transportation, homes, or small businesses — shows total large-facility CO2e emissions falling in 39 of 50 states between 2016 and 2023, consistent with continued decline in the power-generation and heavy-industry sectors that dominate the facility-level data (mostly coal-to-gas switching and renewables growth). The conspicuous exception is Texas, whose large-facility emissions rose 36.8% from 2016 to 2023 — LNG export terminal and petrochemical buildout — while Rhode Island (+21.5%) and Idaho (+21.1%) also rose. This is not a substitute for a true economy-wide, GDP-normalized figure; it only shows that the industrial/power-sector piece of the emissions picture has broadly kept declining post-2016, which is directionally consistent with (but does not prove) continued decoupling economy-wide.

Caveats

  • No U.S. government agency currently publishes a maintained, economy-wide, state-level CO2-per-dollar-of-GDP series past 2016. EIA discontinued the series that produced these numbers.
  • EIA's methodology attributes electricity-generation emissions to the state where the fuel is burned, not the state where the electricity is consumed — a state that imports a lot of coal-fired power (e.g., some Northeastern states) looks cleaner on this measure than its actual electricity consumption footprint would suggest, and a state that exports power (e.g., Wyoming) looks dirtier.
  • Biomass combustion is treated as carbon-neutral in EIA's methodology, which may understate emissions in states with heavy biomass use.
  • The GHGRP post-2016 check covers a different, narrower population (large reporting facilities only) and is not comparable in scope to the 2005-2016 EIA figures; it is included only as a directional cross-check, not as an update to the headline numbers.
  • The 2005-2016 window spans the 2008-09 financial crisis and the 2014-16 oil-price collapse, both of which independently suppressed both energy demand and GDP in some states (most visibly Louisiana) — the observed 'decoupling' in the recession years for hard-hit states is partly a business-cycle artifact, not solely a structural or policy-driven trend.
