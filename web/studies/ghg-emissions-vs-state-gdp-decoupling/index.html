Most states cut GHGRP-reported emissions per dollar of output 2013-2023; Texas, Connecticut and Rhode Island bucked the trend

EPA Greenhouse Gas Reporting Program (large facilities) vs. BEA real state GDP, 2013 to 2023

Summary

Comparing EPA Greenhouse Gas Reporting Program (GHGRP) large-facility CO2-equivalent emissions to BEA real (inflation-adjusted, chained-dollar) state GDP for 2013 and 2023, 41 of 51 states-plus-DC cut their reported emissions per dollar of output — and 40 states plus DC actually reduced absolute reported emissions while their real GDP grew ('decoupled growth'). Nationally, GHGRP-covered emissions rose a modest 3.8% while real GDP rose 27.2%, so covered-sector carbon intensity fell about 18.4%. Maine, New Hampshire, Arizona, New Mexico, and Florida posted the largest intensity declines (all over 40%). Only three jurisdictions moved the wrong way — Texas, Connecticut, and Rhode Island — with reported emissions intensity rising 11-32%; Texas is the standout, with reported emissions up 57% (still up 37% even measured from the post-reform 2016 baseline), driven by its LNG-export and petrochemical buildout outpacing its (also large) 41% GDP growth.

Important scope caveat: GHGRP covers only large stationary sources (facilities emitting over ~25,000 tons CO2e/year — power plants, refineries, large industrial and oil/gas facilities). It is not a comprehensive state greenhouse-gas inventory: it excludes transportation (about 28% of total U.S. GHG emissions), agriculture, and most small/commercial/residential sources. A state's true economy-wide emissions trend, including its transportation sector, could differ from what is reported here.

Method

Emissions: environment.ghg_emissions joined to environment.ghg_facilities on (facility_id, year), summed to state totals of co2e_emission (metric tons CO2e), for 2013 and 2023 — the earliest year with a stable reporting universe (2010-2012 had visibly fewer reporting facilities as the program ramped up) through the latest year loaded (observed coverage 2010-2023, EPA publishes with a ~2-year lag).

Economic output: econ.state_gdp, BEA table SAGDP9 line 1 ('Real GDP by state: All industry total'), which is already expressed in chained (inflation-adjusted) dollars — no separate inflation adjustment was needed since the two years are already on a common real-dollar basis.

Intensity = state CO2e tons ÷ real GDP ($ millions). 'Decoupled' states are those where covered-sector emissions fell AND real GDP rose over the same window — the strict test of the question's second clause.

Sensitivity check on Texas: EPA's Subpart W (petroleum and natural gas systems) reporting requirements changed materially in 2016, so a straight 2013→2023 comparison risks conflating real growth with a reporting-methodology change for oil/gas-heavy states. Re-running the comparison from 2016 (the first year EPA itself calls 'comparable' for Subpart W) to 2023 still shows Texas emissions up 36.8% against 30.8% real GDP growth over that shorter window — the direction and rough scale of Texas's outlier status holds even on the cleaner baseline. Other oil/gas states (Colorado, North Dakota, New Mexico, Oklahoma, Wyoming, Louisiana) show emissions declines in both the 2013-2023 and 2016-2023 windows, so the national pattern is not an artifact of the Subpart W change.
