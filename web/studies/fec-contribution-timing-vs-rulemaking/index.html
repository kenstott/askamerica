Campaign money clusters tightly around elections and FEC filing deadlines — not around when federal rules get finalized

National weekly/monthly analysis, 2017-2025 (through mid-2026 where available). fedregister.fr_documents + fec.individual_contributions.

Summary

At the national, all-agency level, campaign money does not meaningfully cluster around when federal rules get finalized. Comparing the weekly count of final rules published in the Federal Register (2017-2026, n=491 weeks) against weekly itemized federal campaign contributions, the raw correlation is weak (r=0.14, r=0.03 in log terms) and it disappears entirely once the two real drivers of contribution timing are controlled for: proximity to a general election (contributions run roughly 5x higher in Oct-Nov of even years, p<0.001) and FEC quarterly-reporting deadlines (roughly +30% in the weeks around each filing deadline, p=0.03). With those two controls in the model, an extra rule published in a given week moves that week's log contribution total by essentially nothing (+0.0003, p=0.93), and a leave-one-year-out check shows this coefficient flips sign depending on which year is dropped — it is not a stable effect, just noise. A distributed-lag version (rules 2 weeks before through 2 weeks after the contribution week) finds no lead or lag structure either; the only marginally significant lag is the exact same week (p=0.033, effect size trivial: about 0.5% more money per additional rule that week).

In short: the timing of campaign money in the U.S. is overwhelmingly explained by the election calendar and FEC filing deadlines, not by the federal rulemaking calendar. This is a genuine, if modest, empirical finding — not a well-trodden one, since a literature search turned up no prior study that directly measures this relationship (see Research context below). This national aggregate test cannot rule out industry-specific clustering (e.g., a single sector's PAC money spiking around one sector-specific rule) that would wash out in an economy-wide total; that would require a narrower, agency-by-industry follow-up.

Research context: what the literature says (and doesn't)

Two rounds of search (the question directly, then the closest adjacent literature) turned up substantial work on the political economy of rulemaking but nothing that directly tests campaign-contribution timing against rule-finalization dates:

  • Susan Webb Yackee's body of work (e.g. "Sweet-Talking the Fourth Branch", and her review in the Annual Review of Political Science, "The Politics of Rulemaking in the United States") and the related "A Bias Towards Business?" establish that industry commenters are disproportionately influential during the notice-and-comment period and during OMB review, and that lobbying during OMB review is associated with rule change — but that literature is about comment-letter and lobbying timing, not campaign-contribution timing.
  • "Influence and the Administrative Process: Lobbying the U.S. President's Office of Management and Budget" (APSR) studies lobbying, again not campaign dollars, aimed at OMB review specifically.
  • On the lobbying-vs-PAC-money side, "Systemic and Sequential Links Between Campaign Donations and Lobbying" (Journal of Politics) and "Conflating Lobbying and PACs" both document that organizations that lobby and organizations that give via PAC overlap only partially (and that overlap has been declining, ~65% in 1998 to ~30% by 2018), which matters here: it means the entities lobbying hardest around a specific rule are frequently not the same entities whose PAC checks would show up in a campaign-finance timing test. A Frontiers piece on lobbying timing around budget cycles shows lobbying itself does cluster around a known recurring calendar event (the budget), which is the closest documented analogue to what this question asks about rules.

Net: this appears to be a genuinely open empirical question that this connector can partially answer with primary FEC/Federal Register data, rather than one where a published number could simply be looked up.

Method

Finalization-date series: weekly and monthly count of fedregister.fr_documents rows with doc_type='RULE' (all federal agencies), 2017 through mid-2026. Data-quality finding, reported to the connector's issue log: the effective_on column — the rule's actual effective date — is populated for only 81 of 29,505 RULE rows in this window (0.3%), so it cannot be used; publication_date (100% populated) is used instead as the finalization-event date, which is the standard convention anyway (a final rule's effective date is normally publication date + 30-60 days, a fixed administrative lag, not a substantively different economic event).

Money series: weekly/monthly sum of positive amount in fec.individual_contributions (itemized federal contributions >$200, all transaction types, i.e. both capped 15/15E giving and uncapped code-10 Super PAC/Hybrid-PAC giving). Coverage check: this table's year column is a 2-year-cycle partition key, observed loaded only for the 2018/2020/2022/2024/2026 partitions — but each partition's transaction_date column does span both years of its cycle (e.g. the "2018" partition holds real 2017 and 2018 dates), so filtering on those five partition values and grouping by the actual transaction_date gives complete date coverage for 2017-2026 with no interior gap, which was verified before use.

Model: ols_regression of weekly log(contribution $) on weekly rule count, controlling for (a) a pre-election indicator (weeks 40-45 of even years, i.e. the ~6 weeks before a November general election) and (b) an FEC-quarterly-filing-deadline indicator (weeks around the mid-April/mid-July/mid-October/year-end reporting deadlines). A second specification added rule-count at lags/leads of 1 and 2 weeks to check for anticipatory or delayed clustering. sensitivity_analysis (leave-one-year-out) was run on the rule-count coefficient to check whether it is a stable effect or an artifact of one year.

The leave-one-out check on the plain (no-lag) specification is decisive: the rule-count coefficient's sign and significance are not stable — omitting 2018, 2019, 2020, or 2022 flips its sign, and the coefficient's own range across the ten single-year exclusions (-0.0017 to +0.0052) straddles zero. That is the signature of noise, not a real relationship.

Caveats and what this does not show

  • This is a national, all-agency aggregate test. It is well suited to answering "does the pace of rulemaking overall move the total flow of campaign money" (answer: no, once you control for the election calendar). It is not powered to detect a narrower claim like "does the finance industry's PAC money spike around SEC/CFPB rule finalizations" or "does the energy industry's PAC money spike around EPA rules" — those effects could exist and cancel out against other sectors in a whole-economy total. That would need a follow-up joining fedregister.fr_documents.cfr_references (CFR title → regulated industry) to committee industry classification, which was out of scope for this pass.
  • effective_on in fedregister.fr_documents is a broken column for this purpose (0.3% populated) — logged to the connector's issue queue. publication_date was used as a substitute and is the more standard "finalization" marker in any case.
  • fec.committee_contributions (direct PAC-to-candidate 24K checks, arguably the more "access-motivated" money channel) was checked and found to have an even worse gap for this purpose: it is loaded only for the even-year partitions with almost no odd-year transaction-date coverage inside them (unlike individual_contributions), so it could not support a continuous weekly time series and was not used.
  • The most recent two months in the raw monthly series (2026-05, 2026-06) show contribution totals near zero, consistent with reporting lag rather than a real collapse in giving; those months are shown in the underlying data but the analysis window and regressions were built on full weeks, and the near-zero tail months do not drive any reported coefficient.

What This Report Does Not Answer

  • Industry-specific / agency-specific clustering (e.g. does a specific sector's money cluster around that sector's own rules): Explicitly named as out of scope for this pass and flagged as the natural follow-up; the national aggregate null result cannot rule out offsetting sector-level clustering.
