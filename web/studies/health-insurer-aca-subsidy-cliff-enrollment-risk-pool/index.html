ACA marketplace enrollment is down roughly 12-18% since enhanced subsidies lapsed; who left is unmeasured directly, and the public data show only a slight tilt older, with sicker-pool evidence coming from insurer pricing, not claims

CMS effectuated and open-enrollment files (HealthCare.gov states only for the mix), KFF, insurer 2027 filings; as of 2026-11-18

Answer

Enrollment fell sharply. Paid (effectuated) marketplace enrollment was 19.17M in February 2026 versus 21.78M in February 2025, down 12% (CMS; the warehouse table ends at February 2026). KFF puts individual-market enrollment (on and off exchange) down 18% from June 2025 to June 2026 (25.2M to 20.5M), and CBO had projected about 25%. Plan sign-ups fell about 1.2M to roughly 23.1M nationally, so the larger loss shows up in people who did not pay or dropped after enrolling.

Whether leavers are healthier or sicker than stayers cannot be measured from public data: no claims, diagnosis or risk-score data for leavers exists. The observable proxies show only a slight tilt older among those remaining, and insurers are pricing for a sicker pool. My judgment is that leavers skew somewhat healthier on average (the usual adverse-selection pattern), but the size is unproven and the group that lost the most is mainly higher-income people priced out of subsidies, not a health-selected group.

Enrollment shift (computed)

  • Effectuated, National, platform All: Jan 2025 22.22M, Jan 2026 20.19M (-9.1%); Feb 2025 21.78M, Feb 2026 19.17M (-12.0%). Source: health.aca_effectuated_enrollment_monthly.
  • Open-enrollment selections in the 30 HealthCare.gov states: 17.13M (2025) to 15.77M (2026), -7.9%. This footprint excludes state-based marketplaces and Illinois moved to its own exchange in 2026, so it overstates the decline slightly. New consumers fell from 2.84M to 2.52M (-11%); returning fell 14.28M to 13.25M (-7%).
  • KFF: enrollment fell in nearly every state; states that backfilled subsidies did better.

Who left: proxies for health status (computed, selections not effectuated)

  • Age: 45+ share of selections 41.8% to 41.2%; 18-34 share 27.4% to 26.2%. Young adults fell 11-13%, 55-64 fell 10%, children rose 4%. That is a small tilt older, not a collapse of the young.
  • Income: above 400% FPL (no subsidy cliff protection) fell 39%, the largest loss; under 100% FPL fell 45% (small group). The 100-150% FPL group, most of the base, fell only 5%. Leavers are largely people who lost the most subsidy, not a health-sorted group.
  • Plan choice: Bronze share rose from 31.6% to 42.7% and Silver fell from 55.7% to 39.1%. Trading down to high-deductible plans is typically done by those expecting less care, while those expecting more care are pushed toward Gold (12.6% to 17.9%). This is a within-pool selection signal and is consistent with some sorting, but it is not a measure of illness.
  • Insurers: KFF's review of 276 insurers finds a median 15% proposed 2027 increase, with roughly 4 points added on average for further enrollment declines and a sicker pool. Filings (for example one Pennsylvania filing) state healthier, price-sensitive people leave. These are expectations, not observed claims.
  • Counter-evidence: Manatt notes increases are similar whether or not states backfilled subsidies, so medical trend matters too. KFF's survey shows 22% of 2025 enrollees moved to other coverage and 9% became uninsured, with no health data.

Caveats

Selections are not paid enrollment; mix data cover only HealthCare.gov states; age and income are weak proxies for morbidity. Warehouse effectuated data stop at February 2026, so later attrition (KFF notes continued falls through June) is cited from KFF, not computed. Not tested for single-state influence. A definitive answer needs 2026 risk-adjustment transfer reports (CMS, expected mid-2027) or insurer claims data.
