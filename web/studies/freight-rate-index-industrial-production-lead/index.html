Freight volumes are essentially coincident with, not leading, official industrial production — despite trade-press claims of a 3-4 month lead

FRED TRUCKD11 (ATA Truck Tonnage), FRGSHPUSM649NCIS (Cass Freight Shipments) vs INDPRO, cross-correlation by lag, fetched and computed directly 2026-09-26

Summary

Not by much, and maybe not at all — in a direct test, truck tonnage and the Cass Freight Shipments Index move essentially in lockstep with industrial production, with correlation peaking at zero lag rather than at a multi-month lead. Trade-press and industry commentary (AAR-linked analysts, Cass Information Systems) commonly claim rail carloads lead industrial production by 3-4 months and GDP by 3-6 months, with truck tonnage able to predict rail carload direction on a further 2-3 month lag. Testing the truck/freight-index side of that directly against 26 years of ATA Truck Tonnage Index data and 10 years of Cass Freight Shipments data against the Federal Reserve's INDPRO, the correlation is highest when the two series are compared in the same month (r=0.69 for tonnage, r=0.39 for Cass), and falls off steadily and roughly symmetrically as freight is pushed forward relative to IP — the opposite of what a genuine multi-month statistical lead would look like. The 'lead' claimed in industry commentary is better explained by a reporting-timeliness lead — rail carloads are reported weekly and near-real-time, while truck tonnage's final figure isn't released until roughly the 5th of the following month and IP is released with a similar delay — rather than a lead in the underlying economic activity itself.

What the corpus has, and what it doesn't

AskAmerica's transport schema was checked first. transport.rail_service_performance (STB EP 724 weekly Class I railroad filings, including carloads originated by commodity) is the closest match to a rail freight-volume series, but a coverage check confirmed it is loaded for 2026 only — a single year, useless for estimating a lead-lag relationship. The catalog also surfaces a Commodity Flow Survey freight-flows view, but that is a five-yearly (2017) snapshot, not a monthly time series, so it was ruled out by description alone and never queried. econ.fred_indicators (this corpus's curated FRED mirror, 50 series) does not include any truck-tonnage, rail-carload, or freight-index series among its curated set — confirmed by querying it directly (zero rows for TRUCK/RAIL/FRGSHP/FREIGHT patterns). There is a genuine data gap here: no source inside AskAmerica supports a lead-lag computation between freight volumes and industrial production.

To answer the question at all, the real long-run series were pulled directly from FRED (external to this corpus): the ATA Truck Tonnage Index (TRUCKD11, monthly since 2000), the Cass Freight Index — Shipments (FRGSHPUSM649NCIS, monthly since 2016), and the Federal Reserve's Industrial Production Index (INDPRO, monthly since 1919). No comparably clean, freely downloadable long-run rail carload time series was found (AAR's carload data is not distributed as a bulk downloadable CSV), so the rail leg of the answer rests on secondary literature rather than an own computation — flagged explicitly here, not silently treated as equivalent to the truck result.

What the trade press and industry sources claim

  • Rail carloads: Heavy Duty Trucking's Jeff Kauffman (an economic analyst writing with AAR/ATA data) states plainly: "Rail carloads lead GDP movements by an average of three to six months and industrial production by three to four months. And rail carloads are reported weekly by the Association of American Railroads, versus a two-month lag for truck tonnage information." He also notes adjusted rail carloads (ex-coal and grain, with intermodal added back) "track well with truck tonnage in terms of direction and magnitude" and have "consistent predictive value with industrial production."
  • Truck tonnage / ATA: ATA's own press releases describe the tonnage index as preliminary at first release, with the final figure issued around the 5th of the following month — a real reporting lag baked into the series, separate from any economic lead-lag claim.
  • Cass Freight Index: Cass's own site describes the index as "one of the timeliest sources of freight market data available," typically released within two weeks of month-end — again a timeliness claim about publication schedule, not a stated number of months of economic lead over industrial production.
  • A March 2026 FreightWaves roundup (Craig Fuller) cross-references AAR rail carloads, ATA tonnage, SONAR spot-market indices and the ISM Manufacturing PMI as converging, same-period signals of the industrial cycle, explicitly framing freight and manufacturing data as moving together ("getting confirmation from multiple freight and supply chain datasets that the industrial recovery is real") rather than freight leading manufacturing by a fixed number of months.

These are narrative claims, several from parties with an interest in promoting their own index's forecasting value — reason enough to test the quantitative claim directly rather than repeat it as fact.

What a direct empirical test finds

Two independent cross-correlation tests were run on real FRED data fetched via curl and cross-correlated in Python in this session (not simulated, not sourced from a published study):

1. ATA Truck Tonnage Index vs INDPRO, year-over-year percent change, 3-month moving-average smoothed, monthly 2000-2026 (n≈300-317 per lag): correlation is 0.68 at lag -1 month, peaks at 0.687 at lag 0 (perfectly contemporaneous), and falls steadily to 0.10 by lag +8 and negative by lag +10. There is no secondary peak at 3-4 or 6 months out — the relationship decays roughly symmetrically around zero, the signature of a coincident indicator, not a leading one.

2. Cass Freight Shipments Index vs INDPRO, month-over-month percent change, 2016-2026 (n≈104-127 per lag): the only clear correlation spike is at lag 0 (r=0.39); every other lag from -3 to +12 months is under 0.18 in magnitude, several of them negative. A year-over-year version of the same test shows the same pattern: r=0.75 at lag 0, declining monotonically to negative by lag 12.

Both series therefore behave as coincident, not leading, indicators of industrial production in this direct test — a materially different conclusion from the 3-6 month leads claimed in the trade press.

Reconciling the two: why 'coincident' and 'leading' can both be defensible

The gap is most plausibly explained by two different senses of 'lead':

  • Statistical lead in the underlying economic activity — does a move in freight volume this month predict a move in industrial output several months later? The cross-correlation test above answers this directly for truck/Cass data, and the answer is no: the two move together in the same month, not with freight several months ahead.
  • Reporting-timeliness lead — does freight data become available to analysts before industrial production data for the same reference period? Rail carload data is reported weekly by AAR, essentially in real time; ATA's tonnage index is preliminary at first release and finalized around the 5th of the following month; the Federal Reserve's industrial production index for a given month is published roughly two to three weeks after month-end and is revised for months afterward. On this dimension freight data can genuinely arrive first, letting an analyst tracking weekly rail carloads or the ATA's early tonnage read get an early signal on the current month's industrial output before the official IP figure for that same reference month is finalized — a very different claim from freight predicting IP's future direction several months out.

AAR- and Cass-linked commentary is not necessarily wrong on its own terms; much of it (the FreightWaves piece especially) treats freight and manufacturing data as converging, same-period signals rather than a strict multi-month lead. But the specific numeric claims (3-4 months for rail, 1-2 quarters for freight indices generally, as reported by financial-media aggregators) do not survive a direct cross-correlation test on the truck/Cass side. The empirical test is the more literal answer to 'do freight volumes move ahead of official industrial-production numbers, and by how much' when read as a lead in the economic cycle itself rather than in publication schedule.

Caveats and scope

  • No rail-specific cross-correlation was computed directly — no clean, freely downloadable long-run rail carload series was located. The rail lead claim rests entirely on secondary AAR/FreightWaves-linked commentary and has not been independently verified the way the truck and Cass results were; it should be read as substantially less certain than the truck/Cass finding.
  • The truck tonnage sample (2000-2026) spans two recessions and a pandemic-driven freight/production disruption (2020-2022), both of which can distort a simple linear cross-correlation; a regime-specific analysis (excluding 2020-2022) was not run here.
  • Cass Freight Index only has about 10 years of FRED history (2016-present), a much shorter and noisier sample than the truck tonnage series.
  • A linear correlation does not rule out a genuine but weak or nonlinear lead relationship (e.g., a lead that shows up only around business-cycle turning points/recessions rather than throughout the whole cycle) that a simple full-sample cross-correlation could miss.
