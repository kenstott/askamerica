SNAP benefits surged past grocery inflation during 2021-22, then fell behind as pandemic boosts ended

USDA SNAP national average benefit per person vs BLS CPI-U Food-at-Home, federal fiscal years 2020-2024

Summary

Averaged over the full FY2020-FY2024 window, SNAP's nominal average benefit per person rose +20.4% ($155 to $187/month) while grocery prices (BLS Food-at-Home CPI) rose +23.2% — a near wash, with benefits ending about 2% behind cumulative grocery inflation in real terms. But that net figure hides two very different episodes: (1) FY2020-FY2022, when pandemic emergency allotments plus the October 2021 Thrifty Food Plan re-evaluation pushed nominal benefits up 48.6% against only 13.1% grocery inflation — benefits far outran prices; and (2) FY2022-FY2024, when emergency allotments ended (Feb/March 2023) and nominal benefits fell 19.0% even as grocery prices kept climbing +8.9% — real purchasing power collapsed roughly 25% in two years. The lived experience for a SNAP household was a benefit that comfortably beat food inflation during the pandemic boost and then fell sharply behind it once that boost was withdrawn.

FY2020-FY2022, expressing the SNAP benefit in constant FY2020 grocery-purchasing-power terms, the benefit's real value rose from $155 to about $204 — a genuine gain in what a SNAP dollar could buy at the grocery store. By FY2024, that same nominal $186.76 was worth only about $151.61 in FY2020 grocery dollars — slightly below where the program started, despite four years of rising nominal benefits.

Methodology and data quality note

SNAP figure: a persons-weighted national average of each state's federal-fiscal-year (Oct-Sep) 'avg_monthly_benefit_per_person_usd', from USDA FNS's published SNAP data (fiscal.snap_benefits_by_geography). Grocery prices: BLS CPI-U Food-at-Home (series CUUR0000SAF11, U.S. city average, not seasonally adjusted), averaged across the same Oct-Sep fiscal-year window to match SNAP's reporting calendar (econ.food_cpi).

Important data-quality finding: the same national-average calculation applied to FY2015-FY2019 rows in fiscal.snap_benefits_by_geography returns implausibly high per-person figures (~$252-260/month), roughly double the true historical value (USDA's actual published FY2018-2019 national average was closer to $125-130/person/month) — and the parallel per-household calculation for those years returns nonsensical multi-billion-dollar figures, indicating a units/scaling defect in the households_participating or per-person/per-household fields for pre-FY2020 rows specifically. The FY2020 onward figures used here were cross-checked (total_benefits_usd ÷ persons_participating reproduces the published avg_monthly_benefit_per_person_usd exactly) and are consistent with independently published USDA/CBPP figures for those years, so the analysis is confined to FY2020-FY2024 rather than extending back to a pre-pandemic baseline. This defect was logged via report_issue.

food_cpi's declared coverage window ends at calendar year 2024 (BLS publication lag), so FY2025 is not included even though a small number of 2025/2026 rows are present in the raw table; SNAP's own FY2025 total-row scaling was not independently validated against a published cross-check, so it was also excluded from the headline comparison for consistency.

What outside research says

This matches the qualitative pattern reported in independent coverage: the Center on Budget and Policy Priorities and USDA's Economic Research Service both describe the October 2021 Thrifty Food Plan re-evaluation (a 21% benefit increase, the first real increase in SNAP purchasing power in 45 years) as raising benefits faster than inflation at the time, while later reporting (Yahoo Finance, Food Research & Action Center) on the FY2024 3.5% cost-of-living adjustment noted it ran about 1.4 percentage points behind year-over-year food inflation for that adjustment alone. Cato Institute and Urban Institute commentary similarly frame the 2021 TFP change as a one-time step up in benefit generosity beyond straight inflation-indexing, which is consistent with the FY2020-FY2022 gap this analysis finds, and with that gap closing again once emergency allotments (a separate, temporary pandemic measure, not the TFP change) were phased out in 2023.
