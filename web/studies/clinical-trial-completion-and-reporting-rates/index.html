Only about half of registered clinical trials post results on time — and industry sponsors report 1.5-3x more often than academic/non-industry sponsors

Synthesis of peer-reviewed FDAAA-compliance studies (NEJM, Lancet, BMJ EBM) and the FDAAA TrialsTracker, 2015-2025

Summary

Somewhere between one-third and a bit over half of trials that are legally required to report results to ClinicalTrials.gov actually do so on time, and the gap is enormous by sponsor type. In the largest cross-sponsor study (TrialsTracker data, 2006-2015 completions), the overall disclosure rate was 55%, but that blends 74% for industry sponsors against only 46% for non-industry sponsors (universities, government agencies, individual PIs) — industry reports roughly 1.6x as often. The gap is even starker for the legal 12-month deadline specifically: in a 2008-2013 cohort, only 13.4% of trials met the 12-month deadline, rising to about 41% in a 2018-2019 cohort studied after the 2017 FDAAA Final Rule added real enforcement teeth (fines, public tracking). Even after that improvement, 1 in 3 trials in the 2018-2019 study still had not reported results at all by the time the study closed. Top pharmaceutical companies with public transparency commitments do best (top-50 companies: 76%; industry-association members: 77%), while academic/government sponsors lag well behind and, in some sub-analyses, worse than NIH itself.

What 'finishing and reporting' means, and why the numbers move around

Under Section 801 of the FDA Amendments Act (FDAAA, 2007) and its 2017 Final Rule, a defined subset of trials — mainly phase 2-4 interventional drug/device/biologic trials with FDA oversight — must post summary results to ClinicalTrials.gov within 12 months of their primary completion date, unless a certification or extension delays that deadline. 'Reporting' in the studies below means at least one primary outcome was actually posted to the registry, not merely that the trial is marked complete and not a journal publication (publication rates are a separate, generally even lower, statistic). Because different studies define the eligible population (all trials vs. only FDAAA-"applicable" trials), the completion window, and the reporting window (12 months vs. 5 years vs. "ever, as of the analysis date") differently, the headline percentage moves a lot depending on which of those three choices a given study makes — this is why the figures below range from 13% to 78% rather than converging on one number.

The core numbers

  • Anderson et al. 2015, NEJM (PMC4508873) — 13,327 "highly likely applicable" trials completed/terminated 2008-2013: only 13.4% reported within the legal 12-month window; 38.3% reported at any point within 5 years. A parallel check of trials NOT flagged as legally applicable found even lower rates (5.0% / 9.6%), confirming the legal requirement itself is doing real work, just not enough.
  • DeVito, Bacon & Goldacre 2020, The Lancet — 4,209 trials required to report between March 2018 and September 2019, i.e. after the tougher 2017 Final Rule took effect: about 41% reported within the 12-month deadline ("fewer than half"), and roughly one in three trials in the cohort had reported no results at all by the time the study closed. This is a large, real improvement over the 13.4% pre-rule figure — evidence that enforcement mechanics (public tracking, threat of fines) move sponsor behavior — but compliance is still a minority outcome even post-reform.
  • Baronikova, Purvis, Southam et al. 2019, BMJ Evidence-Based Medicine (PMC6837245) — the most detailed sponsor breakdown found: using FDAAA TrialsTracker data on 29,377 eligible trials completed 2006-2015 (69 industry sponsors, 254 non-industry institutions), the mean disclosure rate was 55% overall, split 74% for industry vs. 46% for non-industry sponsors. Restricting to the world's top-50 biopharmaceutical companies by sales, the average rose to 76% (77% among companies that are EFPIA/PhRMA members with public transparency pledges, vs. 67% among non-members). Of all undisclosed trials in this dataset, 81% belonged to non-industry sponsors even though non-industry sponsors ran a larger share of trials overall — the non-reporting problem is disproportionately an academic/institutional one, not a Big Pharma one.
  • FDAAA TrialsTracker (live tool, Bennett Institute, Oxford) — the tool that produces the above sponsor-level figures went offline for over a year (Feb 2024-March 2025) due to a ClinicalTrials.gov platform migration, and resumed regular updates in March 2025. A search-engine summary of the tracker (not independently verified by direct fetch, since the live page is JavaScript-rendered and did not return tabular data to a static fetch) cited roughly 78% (20,039 of 25,691 trials) reporting within one year of primary completion as of April 2025 — notably higher than the 2018-2019 Lancet figure, consistent with continued improvement, but this specific number should be treated as provisional pending direct verification.

Why sponsor type drives so much of the variation

The literature converges on a consistent explanation, not just a consistent number: large, EFPIA/PhRMA-member pharmaceutical companies face concentrated legal exposure (FDA can levy fines up to $12,000/day per overdue trial, though in practice it almost never has), reputational risk from public leaderboards like TrialsTracker, and since 2014 an industry-wide public pledge (the EFPIA/PhRMA joint principles) that 26 of the top 50 companies had adopted as of 2018. Non-industry sponsors — individual academic PIs, universities, and some government programs — are far more numerous (254 distinct sponsors vs. 69 industry sponsors in the Baronikova sample), each running fewer trials, with weaker institutional compliance infrastructure and no equivalent public pledge or brand-reputation incentive. Anderson et al. found NIH-funded and other-government/academic trials had far lower rates of certified reporting delay eligibility (i.e., aren't even exempted) yet still reported at much lower rates than industry. The FDA itself has been reported (Science/STAT, Jan 2020, not independently re-fetched here) as inconsistently enforcing the law even against its own NIH-funded trials.

What this connector could and could not verify directly

AskAmerica's own health.clinical_trials table (ClinicalTrials.gov studies, restricted to a last-update-post-date on/after 2020-01-01) carries overall_status, funder_type, and completion dates, but no results-reported / has-results field — confirmed by describing the table and by inspecting its columns directly. That means this specific question (reporting compliance) cannot be computed live from this connector's data; the volumes below are for context on how large each sponsor population is today, not a reporting rate: as of the current snapshot, industry sponsors have 33,958 completed interventional trials on file, non-industry/'OTHER' sponsors have 91,588, NIH has 1,580, and federal agencies have 1,200. This gap is disclosed here rather than papered over with a computed-looking number; the FDAAA-compliance percentages above all come from the peer-reviewed literature and the TrialsTracker project, not from an AskAmerica query.
