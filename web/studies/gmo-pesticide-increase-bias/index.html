Total pesticide use rose about a quarter, not "dramatically" — and the increase is almost entirely herbicides, not insecticides

USGS NAWQA agricultural pesticide-use data, 1992-2018, cross-checked against EPA and Benbrook (2012, 2016)

Summary

The premise needs correcting before it can be answered as stated. Total US agricultural pesticide use has NOT "dramatically" increased since GMO crops were introduced in 1996 — it rose by roughly a quarter, and that increase is concentrated almost entirely in herbicides, not pesticides broadly. Directly from USGS's own national pesticide-use survey data, the four years before commercialization (1992-95) averaged about 839 million lbs/year applied nationally; the most recent reliably-loaded three years (2016-18) averaged about 1,074 million lbs/year — an increase of about 28% (19% using USGS's high-estimate method). That is a real increase, but it is not the uniform, dramatic escalation the question's premise implies, and it did not happen evenly across pesticide types: insecticide use on the specific crop acres planted to Bt (insect-resistant) GMO traits actually FELL, while herbicide use rose sharply, driven overwhelmingly by one compound — glyphosate — and, more recently, by the additional herbicides (dicamba, 2,4-D) farmers added once weeds evolved glyphosate resistance.

What the primary data shows

I queried USGS's National Water-Quality Assessment (NAWQA) Pesticide National Synthesis Project directly — the same county-level agricultural pesticide-use estimates independent researchers (including Benbrook) build on. This dataset carries low and high estimate methods (epest_low_kg / epest_high_kg) for every compound, county and year, 1992 onward.

Why the total understates the real story: herbicides up, insecticides down

The NAWQA totals above lump herbicides, insecticides and fungicides together across hundreds of compound names, which this session could not cleanly reclassify at scale. For that breakdown, and to isolate the effect specifically attributable to genetically engineered traits (rather than the many other things that changed on US farms over the same 25+ years — no-till adoption, weather, disease pressure, cropland mix), the standard reference is Charles Benbrook's peer-reviewed analysis (Environmental Sciences Europe, 2012), which compares actual 1996-2011 pesticide use on GE crop acres against a modeled counterfactual of what would have been used absent the GE traits:

  • Herbicides: +527 million lbs (herbicide-tolerant, i.e. Roundup Ready, traits led farmers to substitute a single broad-spectrum herbicide for multiple pre-emergent products, then to add MORE herbicides as glyphosate-resistant "superweeds" spread)
  • Insecticides: -123 million lbs (Bt insect-resistant traits reduced the need for sprayed insecticides)
  • Net change: +404 million lbs, roughly +7% relative to the no-GE-trait counterfactual over that 16-year span

Benbrook's 2016 follow-up study (Environmental Sciences Europe) tracked glyphosate specifically through 2014: US farm use rose from about 12.5 million lbs in 1995 (the year before commercial introduction) to about 250 million lbs in 2014 — roughly a 20-fold increase in that single active ingredient, which is the number usually behind claims of a "dramatic" pesticide increase. But glyphosate is one compound substituting for others, not a proxy for total pesticide load, and it is markedly less acutely toxic per pound than many of the herbicides and insecticides it displaced — a point the pounds-applied metric alone does not capture.

Caveats and what this analysis could not verify

  • Correlation vs. causation: the NAWQA national total mixes GMO and non-GMO crop acres and reflects everything that changed on US farms 1992-2018 (weather, no-till adoption, resistant-weed spread, disease-driven fungicide use, crop mix) — it is not a controlled estimate of the GMO-attributable effect. Benbrook's counterfactual method above is the closest available attempt at that isolation, and even that has been publicly disputed by industry-funded reviewers who argue his counterfactual assumptions overstate the effect; both sides agree on the underlying herbicide-up/insecticide-down direction.
  • Seed-treatment insecticides are excluded from NAWQA from 2015 onward (the source discontinued those estimates), so the "recent" total in this analysis undercounts a real and growing insecticide application pathway (neonicotinoid seed coatings) that shifted OFF spray records during the comparison window — meaning the true recent insecticide picture is not fully captured by this data.
  • No clean 2019+ figure: 2019 is only partially loaded and no year after that is in this dataset, so "today" in this analysis means 2016-2018, not the present year.
  • This session did not have a code-execution tool that could parse the compound-level herbicide/insecticide/fungicide classification directly from the ~9.5 million NAWQA rows, so that specific breakdown is sourced from Benbrook's published analysis rather than recomputed here — a genuine methodology gap, disclosed rather than silently filled with the nearest available column.

What This Report Does Not Answer

  • Attribute the change specifically to GMO crops (causal claim in premise): The NAWQA total is a national all-crop, all-pesticide figure, not a controlled GMO-attributable estimate. Used Benbrook's published counterfactual analysis (herbicide +527M lbs, insecticide -123M lbs, net +404M lbs/+7% on GE crop acres, 1996-2011) as the best available attempt to isolate the GE-trait-specific effect, since this session lacked a tool to reclassify ~9.5M compound-level rows into herbicide/insecticide/fungicide categories itself.
