Counties with a heavier blue-collar occupation mix grew jobs more slowly 2010-2023 — but the gap is mostly county size, not automation itself

County-level proxy analysis using ACS occupation mix (2010) and BLS QCEW employment (2010-2023); literature review of Acemoglu-Restrepo robot-exposure and Frey-Osborne automation-risk studies

Summary

Comparing US counties by how automation-exposed their 2010 workforce was (share of workers in production, transportation, material-moving, construction, and maintenance occupations — the occupations the automation literature flags as most substitutable), the counties in the least-exposed quintile (~10% of workers in those occupations) saw total employment grow a mean of 13.5% from 2010 to 2023, versus 7.6% in the most-exposed quintile (~21% of workers) — a raw gap of roughly 6 percentage points over 13 years. But this gap is not mainly about automation: high-exposure counties are also systematically smaller and more rural (correlation of exposure with 2010 county size, r=-0.56), and smaller counties grew slower for reasons unrelated to automation (urban agglomeration, domestic migration to metros). Once county size is held fixed in a regression, the coefficient on automation exposure flips sign (slightly positive) and is not statistically distinguishable from zero (p=0.22, robust to dropping any one state). The peer-reviewed literature, using a sharper measure — actual industry robot adoption weighted by each area's occupation mix (Acemoglu & Restrepo 2020) — does find a real, robust negative local effect (about -0.2 percentage points of employment-to-population ratio per additional robot per thousand workers), concentrated in routine manual and blue-collar occupations. This corpus's occupation-mix proxy is cruder and, once the obvious county-size confound is controlled, does not independently confirm that effect.

What the peer-reviewed literature finds

Acemoglu and Restrepo (NBER w23285; JPE 2020) construct a commuting-zone "robot exposure" measure from national industry-level robot adoption weighted by each zone's 1990 industry employment mix, and find that one more robot per thousand workers reduces the employment-to-population ratio by about 0.2 percentage points and wages by about 0.42%. The effect is concentrated in routine manual, blue-collar, and assembly occupations and among workers without a college degree, and the authors show the exposed commuting zones had no pre-1990 differential trend and that the robot effect is distinct from Chinese/Mexican import competition, offshoring, and other capital deepening.

Frey and Osborne's (2013/2017) occupation-level automation-probability estimates — the basis for the occupation-share proxy used here — found about 47% of 2010 US jobs at high risk of computerization. Follow-on county-level work (e.g. the ScienceDirect county-health study) aggregated these occupation probabilities to counties and found substantial geographic variation, but critics (OECD 2016 task-based reanalysis; later empirical retrospectives) note only a modest empirical correlation (~0.26) between predicted automation risk and actual subsequent job loss, and argue the Frey-Osborne method overstates exposure because it does not allow occupations to be redefined around the automatable tasks rather than eliminated outright.

What this corpus can and cannot measure directly

This connector does not carry an industry robot-adoption series (the input Acemoglu-Restrepo's exposure measure requires) or a pre-built Frey-Osborne occupation-risk crosswalk. The best available proxy is census.acs_occupation, which gives county-level worker counts by broad occupation category from the ACS 5-year survey, including "production, transportation, and material moving" and "natural resources, construction, and maintenance" — the two categories that overlap most with the automation literature's high-risk occupations. This is a coarse proxy: it captures occupation composition, not actual automation adoption, and cannot see within-category variation (e.g., a robotically automated auto-assembly line versus a skilled, hard-to-automate machinist).

Method: exposure share = (production/transportation/material-moving + construction/extraction/maintenance workers) / total employed workers, from the 2010 ACS 5-year estimate (2006-2010), used as a pre-period measure to avoid reverse causality. Employment change is total county employment (all industries, all ownership) from BLS QCEW, 2010 to 2023 (the latest full year available; 2024-2025 QCEW rows exist but were excluded to avoid partial-year effects). Counties with fewer than 500 ACS-reported employed workers were dropped to reduce noise from tiny counties. n=3,189 counties.

The raw gap, and why it mostly disappears with a control

Sorting counties into exposure quintiles and taking the unweighted mean growth rate per quintile: Q1 (least exposed) grew 13.5%; Q2 9.7%; Q3 7.4%; Q4 7.0%; Q5 (most exposed) 7.6% (quantile_binning_test: trend slope -0.0145/bin, p=0.07, not fully monotonic — most of the drop happens between Q1 and the rest, not a smooth gradient). A population-weighted (sum of employment) comparison shows the same pattern: the least-exposed quintile's total employment grew 18.4% vs. 10.8% in the most-exposed quintile, but that comparison is dominated by a handful of very large metro counties in Q1 (Los Angeles, Cook, Harris, etc.).

The key diagnostic: exposure share correlates strongly and negatively with 2010 county employment size (r=-0.56, p<1e-260) — high-exposure counties are disproportionately small, rural counties. Smaller counties independently grew more slowly over this window (r=+0.20 between log county size and growth — larger places grew faster, consistent with well-documented urban agglomeration and continued rural-to-metro migration). Running growth_pct ~ exposure_share + log(2010 employment) gives a coefficient on exposure_share of +0.139 (std. error 0.114, p=0.22) — the sign flips positive and the effect is statistically indistinguishable from zero once county size is held fixed. A leave-one-state-out sensitivity check confirms this null result is not being driven by any single state: the coefficient never flips sign or crosses p=0.05 when any one of the 52 jurisdictions is dropped (coefficient range 0.082 to 0.223 across all 52 leave-one-out refits).

Interpretation and limits

  • The raw pattern is real but confounded. Places with more blue-collar/production workers did see slower total job growth 2010-2023, but that is largely a restatement of "rural and small-town counties grew more slowly than metros," a well-documented pattern with many drivers (migration, industry diversification, agglomeration economies) that predates and is broader than automation specifically.
  • This is not a test of the Acemoglu-Restrepo finding. Their measure isolates robot adoption within industries, net of the area's general economic trajectory, using pre-1990 exposure and controls for import competition and other capital deepening — precisely the kind of control this corpus's cruder occupation-share proxy cannot replicate. Their result should not be considered contradicted by this analysis; this analysis simply cannot speak to it with the tools available here.
  • Correlational only. No causal claim is warranted from either the quintile comparison or the regression control.
  • Coverage: ACS occupation data is declared through 2023; QCEW county data measured coverage 2008-2025, though 2024-2025 were excluded here as likely partial. County-level exposure and employment figures are subject to ACS margins of error at the individual-county level (not applied here since the analysis pools thousands of counties).
