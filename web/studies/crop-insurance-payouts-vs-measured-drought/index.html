Crop insurance payouts track drought closely at the national level (r=0.79) but only loosely state-by-state (r=0.48) — California's persistent drought years are the biggest divergence

USDA RMA Summary of Business &amp; Cause of Loss vs. NOAA/UNL U.S. Drought Monitor, 2010-2024

Summary

At the national, year-to-year level, federal crop insurance payouts track measured drought closely: total U.S. indemnities and the U.S. Drought Monitor's average national severity index (DSCI) move together with a correlation of r=0.79 over 2010-2024 (n=15 years) — both peaked in the historic 2012 Plains/Midwest drought ($17.4B in indemnities) and again in 2022-23 (Texas/Oklahoma drought, $19.4B and $17.8B).

But that headline number hides a much weaker relationship underneath. Broken out to the state-year level (the grain at which drought actually strikes), the payout rate (indemnity as a share of insured liability) correlates with local drought severity at only r=0.48 (n=750 state-years), and drought severity explains just 23% of the variance in payout rates (R²=0.227). The relationship survives controlling for state and year fixed effects (two-way panel regression, coefficient still significant at p<0.0001), so it is real — just far from a tight match.

The two starkest divergences are the biggest finding here. California's 2013-2016 and 2021 droughts registered near-maximum severity on the Drought Monitor (DSCI 313-432 out of a 500 ceiling) yet crop insurance paid out only 2-7% of insured liability those years — 15 to 22 percentage points below what the drought-severity relationship predicts for that level of dryness, the single largest and most persistent gap in the data. In the opposite direction, South Carolina in 2015 paid out 35% of liability despite drought that was almost entirely absent (DSCI of 48, near the low end of the scale) — that payout was driven by the catastrophic October 2015 South Carolina floods (Excess Moisture/Precipitation was the RMA-coded cause of $69.7M of the $148M indemnified, versus $44.8M coded to drought).

Data and method

Payouts: USDA RMA Summary of Business (ag.rma_crop_insurance), summed indemnity and liability by state and year, 2010-2024 (fully loaded, sanity-checked: our 2012 total of $17.45B matches the widely-reported $17.4B figure for that drought year). Peril-level attribution comes from RMA's Cause of Loss file (ag.rma_cause_of_loss), which is only loaded for 2013-2017 and 2025 — 2018-2024 are an interior gap in that specific table, so cause-of-loss attribution below is limited to years where the data exists; it is not available for the 2022-23 divergence cases.

Drought: NOAA/UNL U.S. Drought Monitor weekly county data (weather.drought_monitor_weekly), averaged (unweighted across county-weeks) to a state-year DSCI (Drought Severity and Coverage Index, 0=no drought to 500=all D4/exceptional). Fully loaded 2010-2025.

Payout rate = indemnity / liability, restricted to state-years with liability over $100M to avoid noise from small state insurance programs. This normalizes out the confound that raw indemnity dollars have grown for reasons unrelated to drought (rising commodity prices, expanding insured acreage and coverage levels over the 15-year window) — a payout-rate measure isolates how much of what was actually insured got paid out.

Divergence measure: residual from a pooled OLS of payout_rate on avg_DSCI (payout_rate = 0.0598 + 0.00050 x DSCI, R²=0.227, n=750). A large positive residual means a state paid out far more than its drought severity alone would predict; a large negative residual means it paid out far less.

Where the match breaks down, and why

California's drought years systematically under-trigger crop insurance. Breaking out RMA's cause-of-loss codes for California 2013-2015 (the only years available in that table), the dominant causes of indemnified loss were not filed as "Drought" at all but as "Failure of Irrigation Supply" ($24.4M in 2013, $170.9M in 2014, $178.6M in 2015) and "Heat" ($26.3M, $99.7M, $273.7M) — RMA's own "Drought" code captured only $10.7M, $9.6M, and $1.7M those same years. Even summed together, drought-adjacent causes fall well short of what a 300-430 DSCI would predict for a state with California's insured liability (roughly $6-10B/year). This is consistent with California's insured crop mix: high-value irrigated perennials (almonds, grapes, citrus) where growers buffer yield loss with groundwater pumping and other adaptation rather than letting insurable yield losses occur, and where some acreage/practices carry different or thinner insurance coverage than Midwest row crops.

Non-drought perils can drive large payouts with no drought present at all. South Carolina 2015 is the clearest case in the loaded cause-of-loss years: a payout rate of 35% against essentially no measured drought, because the October 2015 SC flood event dominated indemnities. At the national level, 2019 shows the same pattern at scale — a $10.6B indemnity year (the third-highest since 2010) coincided with the lowest average DSCI in the 2010-2024 series, reflecting the historic 2019 Midwest "prevented planting" floods rather than drought.

Texas, Oklahoma, Montana, and Nevada drought years show the model under-predicting, not diverging in substance. States like TX (2022, 2011, 2024) and OK (2022, 2011, 2023) top the list of payouts exceeding the linear prediction, but these are still drought-driven — the relationship between DSCI and payout rate is likely convex (payouts accelerate faster than DSCI in the most severe droughts) rather than genuinely mismatched, since these states pair very high DSCI with very high payout rates simultaneously. This is a modeling-form caveat, not a sign that insurance failed to respond to drought in these states.

Caveats

  • DSCI is averaged unweighted across county-weeks per state; it is not weighted by insured cropland acreage, so it can overstate or understate the drought exposure actually relevant to a state's insured crop mix.
  • Cause-of-loss attribution (the mechanism behind the CA and SC findings) is only available for 2013-2017 and 2025 in this corpus — the 2018-2024 interior gap means the TX/OK/MT/NV 2018-2024 divergence cases above are explained only by the aggregate indemnity/liability relationship, not by peril-level attribution.
  • The R²=0.23 state-year relationship is a real but modest fit; roughly three-quarters of state-year variation in payout rates is driven by something other than measured drought severity — crop mix, insurance product/coverage-level choices, and other perils (hail, excess moisture, freeze, price decline under revenue protection) all matter, and none are controlled for individually here.
