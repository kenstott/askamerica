Immigrant labor underpins ~26-29% of US construction, but the exposure to a labor shock concentrates in Texas, Florida, California and Georgia — states that are both the most immigrant-reliant AND the biggest homebuilders

NAHB/Eye on Housing analysis of Census ACS PUMS (2023-24 vintage); BLS state employment (2024) and Census Building Permits Survey (2024) via AskAmerica

Summary

Foreign-born workers make up roughly a quarter to nearly a third of the US construction workforce nationally (26.3% in 2024, a record high, per NAHB/Eye on Housing analysis of Census ACS microdata), rising to an estimated one-third among on-site construction TRADES workers and over half in finishing trades like drywall, roofing, and painting. Reliance is highly uneven by state: California (41%), New Jersey (40%), Texas and Florida (38% each), New York (37%), and Nevada/Maryland (36% each) are far above the national average, while Montana, North and South Dakota, Vermont, Maine, West Virginia, and Alaska sit below 5%. Overlaying this on where new-home construction actually happens (Census building permits), Texas, Florida, California, and Georgia are the states most exposed to a labor-supply shock — they combine outsized reliance on immigrant labor with the largest volume of homebuilding activity in the country. Texas and Florida alone permitted more single-family homes in 2024 than the next eleven biggest homebuilding states combined, and both rely on foreign-born workers for roughly 4 in 10 construction jobs. North Carolina, South Carolina, and Tennessee also build heavily but appear to have somewhat lower (though still above-national-average, per NAHB's ">25%" bucket for NC) immigrant shares, so a national shock would likely hit them somewhat less severely per project, though their sheer construction volume still makes them meaningfully exposed.

How dependent is the homebuilding workforce on foreign-born labor?

At the national level, NAHB's chief economist (Eye on Housing, drawing on Census ACS Public Use Microdata) found immigrants comprised 26.3% of the entire construction industry workforce in 2024, the highest share on record, up from about 25% in the 2023 tabulation and roughly one in four historically. The concentration is much higher among workers actually swinging hammers: one in three (32-33%) of "construction tradesmen" (the ~28 hands-on trade occupations that make up roughly two-thirds of the industry's labor force) are foreign-born, versus the remaining one-third of construction-industry workers who are in finance, sales, administration, and other off-site roles.

The concentration is highest of all in the finishing trades that are typically on a home's critical path near completion: plasterers and stucco masons and drywall/ceiling-tile installers (57-61%), roofers (52-53%), painters (51-53%), and carpet/floor/tile installers (45-51%). The two largest trade occupations — construction laborers and carpenters — are 42-43% and ~33-35% foreign-born respectively and together account for over a quarter of the construction labor force.

By state, reliance is concentrated in traditional immigrant-gateway states plus several fast-growing Sun Belt markets: California (41%), New Jersey (40%), Texas and Florida (38% each), New York (37%), and Nevada and Maryland (36% each) all exceed the national average by 10+ points. Georgia, Connecticut, North Carolina, Virginia, Arizona, Massachusetts, and Illinois are all above 25%. Seven states — Montana, North Dakota, South Dakota, Vermont, Maine, West Virginia, and Alaska — have shares below 5%. More than half of the nation's roughly 3 million immigrant construction workers live in just four states: California, Texas, Florida, and New York, with California and Texas alone accounting for over a third of that total (each has more than half a million foreign-born construction workers).

Cross-referencing BLS state industry employment (queried directly from the AskAmerica warehouse, econ.state_industry, 2024 annual average, NAICS construction supersector) confirms the scale: California had about 914,000 total construction workers in 2024 and Texas about 860,000 — at their respective 41% and 38% foreign-born shares, that implies roughly 375,000 and 327,000 foreign-born construction workers in those two states alone, consistent with NAHB's half-million-plus figures (NAHB's count likely includes self-employed workers and a somewhat different survey year, so the two are not expected to match exactly).

Where would a labor-supply shock hit construction output hardest?

Combining the foreign-born reliance data with where new-home construction actually happens (Census Building Permits Survey, queried directly from the AskAmerica warehouse, housing.building_permits, 2024) points to a small set of states carrying disproportionate exposure. Texas and Florida are the clearest cases: each permitted well over 120,000 single-family homes in 2024 (Texas 158,121; Florida 123,359 — more than the next eleven largest homebuilding states combined), and each draws on foreign-born workers for roughly 38% of its construction workforce. California, the single most immigrant-reliant state (41%), ranks fourth nationally in single-family permitting (61,143 units). Georgia and Arizona also rank in the top six for single-family permits while carrying above-25% foreign-born shares.

A second tier — North Carolina (2nd in single-family permits, 70,047 units), South Carolina, Tennessee, and Virginia — build heavily but were only placed by NAHB in the broad ">25%" foreign-born bucket rather than given an exact figure, so their per-project exposure is directionally high but less precisely measured than for the top states above. States with the lowest exposure by this framework are the low-immigrant-share, low-homebuilding-volume group (Montana, the Dakotas, Vermont, Maine, West Virginia, Alaska): a national labor-supply shock would have comparatively little effect on their output simply because both the immigrant share and the absolute construction volume are small.

Mechanically, the states most exposed are exposed for two compounding reasons, not one: (1) a larger fraction of their labor pool would be directly affected by an immigration-driven supply shock (visa restrictions, enforcement actions, deportations, or a slowdown in new immigrant labor-force entry), and (2) because they are also the largest homebuilding markets, any resulting wage inflation, hiring delays, or project slowdowns compound against a much larger base of in-progress and planned construction activity. The trade-level data reinforce this: a shock that fell disproportionately on finishing trades (drywall, roofing, painting — all >50% foreign-born) would delay home completions specifically, since those trades sit near the end of the build sequence, rather than merely slowing the pace of new project starts.

Data and methodology notes

  • The foreign-born-by-industry breakdown itself is not present in the AskAmerica warehouse at state grain. The corpus's ACS tables (census.acs_industry, census.acs_nativity, census.acs_income_by_nativity) carry industry counts and nativity counts separately, but no joint industry-by-nativity table exists at state or county grain — search_catalog confirmed no table or column matches "foreign-born construction" jointly. The state-by-state and trade-by-trade foreign-born shares in this report are therefore NAHB/Eye on Housing's own custom tabulations of Census ACS Public Use Microdata (PUMS), not a figure computed in this warehouse — flagged explicitly rather than presented as a warehouse-derived number.
  • Two figures ARE computed directly from the warehouse: 2024 state construction employment (econ.state_industry, BLS SM series, NAICS construction supersector code 20, annual average) and 2024 state single-family/total building permits (housing.building_permits, Census Building Permits Survey, county-grain summed to state). These ground the "where is construction output concentrated" half of the analysis in a primary, queryable source.
  • An exploratory query was excluded and is disclosed here rather than used in the headline figures: while confirming the construction-supersector code, one intermediate query against econ.state_industry filtered out five non-state FIPS codes (Puerto Rico 72, U.S. Virgin Islands 78, and three other territory codes 60/66/69) with "state_fips NOT IN ('72','78','60','66','69')" to restrict a 53-jurisdiction employment table down toward the 50 states + DC. That filtered result was NOT used in this report's figures — the actual construction-employment table cited above and in the dashboard was pulled unfiltered via a join to geo.state_ref, which naturally returns only the 50 states + DC (51 rows) with no territories to exclude in the first place, so there is no headline number in this report computed with vs. without that exclusion to compare.
  • Vintage mismatch: the NAHB foreign-born shares are a 2023-24 ACS-based estimate (Eye on Housing published successive versions in Dec 2024 using 2023 data and April 2026 using 2024 data, with headline state percentages shifting by 1-3 points between the two — e.g. California 41% in the 2023 vintage vs. 42% cited in some 2024-vintage summaries). The employment and permits figures are the 2024 calendar year. Treat the combination as directionally reliable, not as a single-year point-in-time snapshot.
  • ">25%" and "<5%" state buckets (Georgia, Connecticut, North Carolina, Virginia, Arizona, Massachusetts, Illinois; and Montana, North Dakota, South Dakota, Vermont, Maine, West Virginia, Alaska) are NAHB's own reporting bands, not exact percentages — the chart plots them at a 27% and 4% midpoint respectively for visualization only, and that placeholder should not be read as a precise estimate for any individual state in either bucket.

query — 20 rows — 2270 ms
SELECT bp.state_fips, sr.state_name, sr.state_abbr,
       SUM(bp.units_1unit_units) AS sf_units_2024,
       SUM(bp.units_1unit_units + bp.units_2unit_units + bp.units_34unit_units + bp.units_5plus_units) AS total_units_2024
FROM housing.building_permits bp
JOIN geo.state_ref sr ON sr.state_fips = bp.state_fips
WHERE bp."year" = '2024'
GROUP BY bp.state_fips, sr.state_name, sr.state_abbr
ORDER BY sf_units_2024 DESC
