Veterans have a lower unemployment rate than nonveterans, not a higher one — the exception is disability status and short-term volatility in young/Post-9/11 subgroups

BLS Current Population Survey, veteran-status series (LNU04049526/601), fetched directly from api.bls.gov; IVMF/Syracuse "Employment Situation of Veterans," November 2025 release

Summary

By the standard yardstick — the unemployment rate — veterans do not have a harder time finding work than everyone else; they consistently have an easier time by this measure. In 2024 (full-year average), the veteran unemployment rate was 3.0% versus 3.9% for nonveterans — veterans about a quarter lower. Through the first 11 months of 2025 the gap held (3.5% vs 4.2%), and it was still open in the latest available month (August 2026: 3.4% vs 4.2%). This pattern holds even among disabled veterans, where the veteran rate (5.7%) is well below the disabled-nonveteran rate (8.1%), though disability roughly doubles the rate for both groups. The one place the story flips is a different metric: 62% of veterans report being underemployed — in a job below their skill level — six and a half years after leaving service, a job-quality problem the unemployment rate does not capture at all.

2025's annual figure is an 11-month average because BLS could not collect October 2025 CPS data during the federal government shutdown — a genuine collection gap, not a rounding choice, and it applies equally to both series so it does not bias the comparison between them.

Does the advantage survive controlling for disability?

The most obvious confound is that veterans as a group skew male, older, and more likely to carry a service-connected disability — all factors that could cut either way. Splitting by disability status (BLS/IVMF, September 2024–August 2025 average) shows the veteran advantage is not an artifact of who is disabled: veterans with a disability, 5.7%; veterans with no disability, 3.0%; nonveterans with a disability, 8.1%; nonveterans with no disability, 3.8%. Disability roughly doubles the unemployment rate within both populations, but veterans fare better than nonveterans at every disability level — the disabled-veteran rate (5.7%) is well below the disabled-nonveteran rate (8.1%), an even wider gap in relative terms than the headline comparison.

Where it does get harder: age, short-run volatility, and job fit

Three qualifications matter and are worth stating plainly rather than folding into the headline:

  • Young veterans are volatile month to month. IVMF's November 2025 release shows the 18–24 veteran unemployment rate jumping from 1.1% in September to 11.6% in November 2025, versus 9.2% for nonveteran 18–24-year-olds that month — a single month where young veterans look worse off. But on the annual-average basis IVMF itself recommends for this age group (2024: veterans 6.4% vs nonveterans 8.4%), veterans are still lower. IVMF explicitly cautions that subgroup monthly figures "can vary widely from month to month" because of small CPS sample sizes for veteran subpopulations — the 11.6% single-month spike is not a stable finding.
  • Post-9/11 veterans track close to the overall rate but can diverge sharply in a given month (2.4% in September 2025 to 4.3% in November 2025), again a small-sample effect IVMF flags rather than a structural trend.
  • Underemployment, not unemployment, is where veterans plausibly fare worse. IVMF's April 2025 research brief "Reimagining Military-to-Civilian Transition" found 62% of veterans report being underemployed — working below their skill or education level — 6.5 years after leaving the military. That is a different, harder-to-measure outcome (job fit, wage-appropriate placement, translating military skills into civilian credentials) than the unemployment rate, and no matched nonveteran underemployment figure from the same source was located to benchmark it against, so this is reported as a veteran-side finding only, not a veteran-vs-nonveteran comparison on that metric.

Bottom line

"How much harder is it for veterans to find work" presumes a premise that the primary BLS data does not support: on the standard unemployment-rate measure, it is not harder — it is consistently somewhat easier, by roughly 0.7–0.9 percentage points (about a fifth to a quarter lower), a gap that persists even after splitting by disability status. The real risk to the framing is a different metric entirely: whether the job a veteran lands actually uses their skills and pays accordingly, where IVMF's 62% underemployment figure suggests transition friction that the unemployment rate is structurally unable to see.

Veteran-status BLS series are not seasonally adjusted and ages 18+ (vs 16+ for the general population rate), so they are not perfectly comparable to the headline U-3 rate, but the veteran/nonveteran pair used throughout this report is internally consistent (same age floor, same seasonal-adjustment status) for both groups.
