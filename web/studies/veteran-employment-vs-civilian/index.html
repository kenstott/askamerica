Overall, veterans are not worse off than nonveterans in unemployment rate — but young and recently separated veterans face a meaningfully harder job market

BLS Current Population Survey, veteran status, ages 18+, not seasonally adjusted

Summary

Averaged across the whole veteran population, veterans are not harder-off in unemployment terms than nonveterans — the reverse, slightly. Over January-August 2026, the U.S. veteran unemployment rate averaged 3.9% versus 4.2% for nonveterans (BLS CPS, not seasonally adjusted). In BLS's 2024 annual averages, veterans were also lower: 3.0% vs. 3.9% for nonveterans. This has been the typical pattern for at least a decade: veterans, on average, have an unemployment rate at or below the national nonveteran rate, mainly because the veteran population skews older, more male, and more attached to steady employment than the general working-age population.

But the aggregate number hides where veterans genuinely do have it harder: young veterans and the recently separated. In the November 2025 BLS release, veterans aged 18-24 had an 11.6% unemployment rate versus 9.2% for nonveteran 18-24 year olds — 2.4 points harder. Post-9/11 veterans overall ran higher than the general population in that same release (4.3% vs. 4.2%), and their rate is also far more volatile month to month than the all-veteran or nonveteran rates, swinging by several points in a single month for smaller demographic cells. So the honest answer has two parts: for the typical (often older, established) veteran, the labor market is not harder — it is comparable or a bit easier. For someone in their first few years out of the military, especially age 18-24, it measurably is harder, and by several percentage points at times.

What was measured, and its limits

The measure used is the BLS Current Population Survey unemployment rate by veteran status (series LNU04049526 for veterans, LNU04049601 for nonveterans, and the Gulf War-era II/post-9/11 veteran series LNU04066408), for ages 18+, not seasonally adjusted — BLS publishes no seasonally adjusted veteran breakout, so month-to-month veteran figures carry more noise than the headline seasonally adjusted national rate, and small subgroup cells (e.g., veterans 18-24, roughly the smallest slice of the veteran population) are especially volatile from sampling variance alone; IVMF's own release explicitly cautions that veteran subgroup data "can vary widely from month to month."

This is a comparison of unemployment RATES (share of the labor force that is jobless and looking), not a measure of how long it takes to find a job, how many applications it takes, or wage/quality of the job found once hired — those would need different data (e.g., median weeks unemployed by veteran status, which BLS does not break out) and are not available in this exercise.

Data used and vintages

  • askamerica econ.employment_statistics (BLS CPS, veteran-status series LNU04049526 veterans / LNU04049601 nonveterans): monthly Jan-Aug 2026, not seasonally adjusted. This connector table's declared coverage is 2020-2026 but only 2026 months are actually loaded (checked live) — the connector cannot supply the veteran-status series before 2026, so the multi-year annual-average comparison (2024: 3.0% vs 3.9%) and the November 2025 age breakdown came from a direct BLS-sourced document via IVMF, not from this table.
  • BLS "Employment Situation of Veterans" release, November 2025 data (published Dec 12, 2025), read via the D'Aniello Institute for Veterans and Military Families (IVMF) summary, which reproduces BLS's own tables verbatim including the age, sex, and race/ethnicity breakdowns and the 2024 annual averages column.
