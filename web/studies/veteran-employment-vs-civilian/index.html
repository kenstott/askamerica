Overall, veterans are not harder off — but young, female, and post-9/11 veterans face a real, measurable disadvantage

BLS Current Population Survey veteran-status series, 2025-2026, plus qualitative research on transition barriers

Summary

On the single headline number, veterans as a whole are not harder off: in 2026 year-to-date (Jan-Jul), the average unemployment rate for veterans was 3.9% versus 4.2% for nonveterans (BLS CPS, not seasonally adjusted) — veterans have run at or below the nonveteran rate for most of the last five years. But that headline conceals sharp, consistent splits within the veteran population: post-9/11 (Gulf War-era II) veterans run noticeably higher than the all-veteran average and about even with or above nonveterans (4.7% vs 4.2% in July 2026); female veterans have higher unemployment than nonveteran women (4.6% vs 4.0% in the 2025 annual average, while veteran men beat nonveteran men, 3.3% vs 4.3%); and younger veterans (25-34) run well above same-age nonveterans (7.3% vs 5.0% in March 2026). Beyond the unemployment rate itself, veterans by most accounts find a first civilian job about as easily as anyone else but have a harder time keeping it and matching it to their skill level — a retention and underemployment problem the unemployment rate does not capture at all.

1. The headline rate: veterans are not, on average, harder off

BLS publishes a not-seasonally-adjusted veteran-status breakout of the Current Population Survey (series LNU04049526 for veterans 18+, LNU04049601 for nonveterans 18+, LNU04066408 for post-9/11/Gulf War-era II veterans). Pulling this directly:

  • 2026 monthly veteran rate ranged 3.2%-4.5%, averaging 3.9% Jan-Jul.
  • 2026 monthly nonveteran rate ranged 3.9%-4.6%, averaging 4.2% over the same months.
  • Annual averages for recent years (BLS): veterans 4.4% (2021), 2.8% (2022), 2.8% (2023), 3.0% (2024), 3.5% (2025) — a run of years at or below the general labor market's unemployment rate.

So on the plain question 'is it harder for a veteran to find work than for anyone else,' the government's own survey data says no, not on average — if anything slightly easier, likely reflecting that veterans skew older, male, and more attached to the labor force than the general population, all of which independently correlate with lower unemployment.

2. But averages hide who is actually struggling

The 'veterans' aggregate blends World War II-era retirees, Vietnam-era veterans mostly past working age, and post-9/11 veterans still actively job-hunting. Breaking it out changes the picture:

  • Post-9/11 veterans (Gulf War-era II) ran 4.7-5.9% through 2026 — consistently 0.5-1.5 points above the all-veteran rate, and at or above the nonveteran rate in most months. Their 2025 annual average was 3.6% vs. 3.5% for all veterans and roughly comparable to nonveterans.
  • Female veterans had a 2025 annual unemployment rate of 4.6% versus 4.0% for nonveteran women — a 0.6-point disadvantage — while male veterans (3.3%) did clearly better than nonveteran men (4.3%). Female veteran unemployment also rose faster over 2025 (+1.1 points) than male veteran unemployment (+0.4 points).
  • Younger veterans fare worse than same-age nonveterans: in March 2026, veterans 25-34 had a 7.3% unemployment rate versus 5.0% for nonveterans the same age — a 2.3-point gap, among the largest of any group compared here. Veterans 18-24 were roughly even with nonveteran 18-24-year-olds (7.9% vs 7.6%). Older veterans (55-64) did much better than older nonveterans (1.6% vs 3.2%), which is a major reason the blended 'all veterans' number looks good — the population is simply older on average.

So the honest answer to 'how much harder' depends entirely on which veterans: a middle-aged or older veteran, especially male, generally has an easier time than a demographically matched nonveteran; a young, recently separated, or female veteran faces a real and measurable disadvantage of 1-2+ percentage points.

3. What the unemployment rate does not capture: retention and underemployment

Multiple veteran-employment studies (Journal of Veterans Studies; USC Center for Innovation and Research on Veterans; veteran-service organizations) describe a pattern where veterans are not primarily blocked from getting a first civilian job, but struggle to keep it or to find one that matches their skill level and pay grade. Commonly cited figures put first-year attrition from a veteran's first civilian job at roughly 40% or higher, well above typical civilian turnover, driven by: (a) difficulty translating military skills and job titles into civilian resume language — one survey found only about 38% of employers believe military skills transfer easily to the private sector; (b) employer unfamiliarity with military occupational specialties and, in some cases, stigma around presumed mental-health issues; (c) genuine transition stress, even short of diagnosable PTSD, that affects interview performance and early job fit; and (d) a cultural adjustment gap between military structure/hierarchy and civilian workplace norms. None of this shows up in the unemployment rate, which only measures whether someone currently has or is actively seeking a job — a veteran who is employed but underemployed relative to their training, or who has cycled through several short-tenure jobs, counts the same as anyone else with a job.

Data and method notes

Veteran-status unemployment series (LNU04049526/601/408) are drawn from the BLS Current Population Survey and are not seasonally adjusted, unlike the headline U-3 rate (LNS14000000); month-to-month comparisons carry more noise than the seasonally adjusted rate, which is why this report leans on annual averages and same-month/same-quarter comparisons rather than a single monthly snapshot. The AskAmerica connector's econ.employment_statistics table currently holds only 2026 rows for the veteran-status series (its declared coverage window is 2020-2026, but only 2026 is actually loaded) — a genuine connector coverage gap, reported separately. Multi-year annual averages (2021-2025) and the 2025 by-sex breakdown in this report were therefore pulled directly from BLS's own published 'Employment Situation of Veterans' news releases via search, not from the connector table, since the connector could not supply them for those years.

What This Report Does Not Answer

  • Multi-year trend: The connector's employment_statistics table declares 2020-2026 coverage for the veteran-status series but only actually holds 2026 rows; 2021-2025 annual averages were sourced externally from BLS's own published news releases instead, and are reported as such.
