Op-ed's "3.8% record-low poverty" and "income more than doubled" claims are both false

Census Bureau CPS ASEC and ACS data show the actual 2023 poverty rate was 11.1% and real median household income rose about 8%, not 100%+, since 2000

Summary

Both headline claims in the op-ed are false. The actual 2023 U.S. poverty rate was 11.1% (Census Bureau official CPS ASEC measure) -- not 3.8%, and not a record low; the historic low on any recognized U.S. poverty measure is roughly 7.8-10.5%, still nowhere near 3.8%. Separately, real (inflation-adjusted) median household income rose only about 8% between 2000 and 2023, not "more than doubled." Even in unadjusted nominal dollars, income grew 91.3% ($42,148 to $80,610) -- close to but short of doubling, and the relevant comparison for "financial security" is real, inflation-adjusted purchasing power, which grew only about a twelfth as much as claimed. The op-ed's concluding claim that families are "twice as financially secure as their parents' generation" does not survive contact with either underlying statistic.

Poverty rate: 3.8% does not match any published measure

The U.S. Census Bureau's official poverty rate (Current Population Survey Annual Social and Economic Supplement, CPS ASEC) for 2023 was 11.1%, a 0.4 percentage point decline from 2022's 11.5% -- a real but modest improvement, not a plunge to a record low (Census, P60-283, "Poverty in the United States: 2023"). The Supplemental Poverty Measure (SPM), which accounts for taxes and non-cash benefits, was 12.9% in 2023 -- actually up 0.5 points from 2022, the opposite of the op-ed's direction. AskAmerica's own ACS 1-year poverty data (census.acs_poverty) independently computes a national 2023 rate of 12.73%, corroborating the official figure rather than the op-ed's. No vintage of any recognized U.S. poverty statistic -- official, SPM, or ACS -- has ever come close to 3.8%; the lowest rate ever recorded on the official measure is about 10.5% (2019), and on the SPM about 7.8% (2021, a pandemic-relief-driven anomaly). The 3.8% figure appears to be simply invented.

Household income: real growth was about 8%, not "more than doubled"

Census Bureau's own historical report states nominal median household income in 2000 was $42,148 (P60-213, "Money Income in the United States: 2000"). Census's 2024 report states real median household income in 2023 was $80,610 (P60-282, "Income in the United States: 2023"). The straightforward nominal ratio is 80,610 / 42,148 = 1.913, i.e. +91.3% nominal growth -- already short of "more than doubled" before even considering inflation.

Financial security, however, is a real-purchasing-power question, not a nominal-dollar one. Using AskAmerica's adjust_inflation tool (BLS CPI-U, series CUUR0000SA0), $42,148 in year-2000 dollars equals $74,579 in 2023 dollars. Comparing that to the actual 2023 figure of $80,610 gives real growth of only (80,610 - 74,579) / 74,579 = 8.1% over 23 years -- a modest, single-digit real gain, not a doubling. The op-ed's claim that this proves families are "twice as financially secure" would require the real growth figure to be roughly 100%; the actual figure is about a twelfth of that.

Methodology note

Census expresses its own historical income series in "real" (inflation-adjusted) dollars using a blended CPI-U-RS / chained-CPI methodology that can diverge slightly from a single-index CPI-U recalculation like the one used here (see AskAmerica's own reconcile-diverging-real-dollar-adjustments finding, logged from a prior run on this same question). Using CPI-U throughout, as done here, keeps the poverty-rate and income comparisons on one consistent, checkable footing; the qualitative conclusion -- real growth in the single digits, not a doubling -- is not sensitive to that methodological choice.
