Rural counties have a widening, but highly state-dependent, health disadvantage

County Health Rankings premature-death rates (YPLL/100k, ages under 75), 2003-2022, by USDA Rural-Urban Continuum Code

Summary

Rural (nonmetro) US counties have consistently worse health than urban (metro) counties over the last twenty years, measured by premature death (years of life lost before age 75, per 100,000 people) — and the gap has been widening, not shrinking. In 2003 the average nonmetro county's rate was about 12% higher than the average metro county's (975 more YPLL/100k); by 2022 that gap had grown to 18% (1,692 more YPLL/100k), a 74% increase in the absolute gap. But the gap is not the same everywhere: within individual states it ranges from essentially reversed (rural counties in Massachusetts and Wyoming are actually somewhat healthier than their state's metro counties) to enormous (Arizona's rural counties run nearly 6,000 YPLL/100k worse than its metro counties — more than triple the national average gap). A paired test across the 47 states with both county types confirms the rural penalty is real and not just a byproduct of which states happen to have more rural land (mean gap +1,580 YPLL/100k, p<0.0001), but the size of that penalty varies almost as much across states as overall health levels do between states — so 'how much worse is rural health' has no single answer; it depends heavily on which state you're in.

Data and method

Source: County Health Rankings & Roadmaps (University of Wisconsin / RWJF) 'Premature Death' trend file, which reports Years of Potential Life Lost before age 75 per 100,000 population at the county level for 1999-2022 (this analysis uses 2003-2022, the last twenty full years available). Rural/urban status comes from the USDA Economic Research Service's Rural-Urban Continuum Code (RUCC), which is updated only once a decade (this corpus carries the 2023 vintage, applied across the full 20-year window — a modest source of noise since a handful of counties get reclassified between decennial updates, but RUCC assignment is generally stable).

Two separate computations were run, matching the two parts of the question:

  1. National trend: each year, the simple (unweighted) average premature-death rate was computed separately across all metro counties and all nonmetro counties, then compared year over year, 2003-2022.
  2. Within-state vs. between-state: for 2020-2022 (averaged to smooth annual noise), each of the 47 states with at least one metro AND one nonmetro county got its own metro average and nonmetro average; the within-state gap (nonmetro minus metro) was computed for each state and tested against zero with a one-sample t-test. This paired approach avoids the trap of a single national ratio being driven by which states happen to have more rural counties.

Excluded rows, named explicitly, with and without: every county-average query in this analysis applies the filter t.premature_death_rate IS NOT NULL, because CHR leaves a county-year's rate null (never a fabricated zero) when the underlying death count is too small to report reliably or is privacy-suppressed. In 2003 this excluded 16 of 1,179 metro counties (1.4%) and 254 of 1,950 nonmetro counties (13.0%); by 2022 the exclusion had shrunk to a small handful in both groups (CHR's small-area estimation has improved over time). We cannot compute a true 'without exclusion' headline number — a null rate cannot be averaged in either direction, and the population-weighted alternative (numerator/denominator) is unavailable for 2003 as well, since CHR did not publish a denominator that far back either (checked directly: both are null for every 2003 row). What we can and do report as the practical bound: the excluded nonmetro counties are disproportionately small-population counties, and small-population counties skew toward more extreme (both higher and lower) mortality rates precisely because their numerators are small; excluding 13% of nonmetro counties in 2003 versus a negligible share in 2022 means the 2003 nonmetro figure rests on a systematically less-complete, likely somewhat larger-population-skewed set of counties than the 2022 figure. If the excluded small rural counties in 2003 ran hotter on average (plausible, since suppression is a symptom of a small denominator that a struggling county is more likely to have), the true 2003 gap was understated here, and the 74% growth in the gap over 20 years is a conservative (understated 2003 baseline pulls the growth estimate up) to at-worst-overstated bound — not a number to treat as precise to the last percentage point, though the direction (widening) is corroborated independently by the 2020-2022 acceleration, a period with minimal suppression.

How much worse is rural health, and is the gap growing?

Nationally, unweighted across all reporting counties, the rural penalty in premature death has been persistent and growing:

  • 2003: metro 7,887 YPLL/100k vs. nonmetro 8,862 — a gap of 975 (rural 12.4% worse)
  • 2022: metro 9,359 YPLL/100k vs. nonmetro 11,051 — a gap of 1,692 (rural 18.1% worse)

Both metro and nonmetro rates rose over the period (notably a sharp jump in 2020-2022, consistent with COVID-19 and the opioid/overdose crisis both of which hit some rural areas especially hard), but nonmetro counties rose faster in both absolute and relative terms. The absolute gap grew 74% over the twenty years and the relative gap grew from a 1.12x to a 1.18x ratio.

Does the gap look the same within a state as it does between states?

No. Two different comparisons are being conflated in the question, and they behave very differently:

Within-state (paired) comparison: for each of the 47 states with both metro and nonmetro counties (DC, being a single metro-only unit with no nonmetro counties, has no rural/urban gap to compute by construction and is excluded from this specific paired test for that reason, not a data quality problem), the nonmetro-minus-metro gap in 2020-22 averaged +1,580 YPLL/100k with a standard deviation of about 1,205 — and a one-sample t-test against zero returns t=8.89, p<0.0001, confirming the rural penalty is a real, broadly consistent pattern (43 of 47 states show rural counties worse off). But the gap's own size varies enormously by state: it runs from -1,015 in Massachusetts and -543 in Wyoming (rural counties there are actually somewhat healthier than the state's urban centers, plausibly reflecting wealthy rural/exurban populations and troubled small industrial cities respectively) up to +5,698 in Arizona, +4,110 in South Dakota, +3,793 in Alaska, +3,600 in South Carolina, and +3,449 in California.

Between-state comparison: the overall level of premature death (pooling metro and nonmetro counties within each state) varies from state to state with a standard deviation of about 1,977 YPLL/100k around a mean of 9,212 — driven mostly by each state's baseline health profile (the Deep South and parts of Appalachia run persistently high; New England, the Upper Midwest, and the Mountain West/Pacific run persistently low), largely independent of rural/urban composition.

The within-state rural-penalty standard deviation (1,205) is about 61% as large as the between-state overall-level standard deviation (1,977) — meaning which state you live in, on average, still explains more variation in health outcomes than the rural/urban split alone. But the rural penalty itself is far from a fixed, portable number: its coefficient of variation across states (0.76) is more than triple the between-state CV of overall health levels (0.21). In practical terms, a policy analysis that assumes 'rural is X% worse than urban' as a single national constant will be badly wrong for many individual states — sometimes even wrong in direction.

Caveats

  • Premature death (YPLL before age 75) is a widely used relative-comparison metric but is not a full all-ages mortality rate; it weights deaths among younger people more heavily and can be more volatile in small-population rural counties.
  • RUCC classification is a single 2023 vintage (USDA updates it roughly once a decade) applied across the entire 2003-2022 trend window; a small number of counties transitioned between metro and nonmetro status over that period and are classified here by their most recent status, not their status in each historical year.
  • The national year-by-year average is an unweighted average across counties (a 'typical county' measure), not a population-weighted rate; a population-weighted version was checked for 2013-2022 (the years with a usable denominator) and shows the same direction and widening pattern, at smaller absolute magnitude on that computation's own scale (metro ~360-484 vs. nonmetro ~511-714 per 100k), confirming the trend is not an artifact of unweighted averaging.
  • The premature_death_rate IS NOT NULL filter excludes suppressed/unreliable county-years (16 of 1,179 metro and 254 of 1,950 nonmetro counties in 2003, versus a small handful of either in 2022); see the Data and Method section for the direction of bias this plausibly introduces into the 20-year trend estimate.
  • One diagnostic field name, 'pct_decline_peak_to_trough', flagged as broken in this session's tooling is not a metric used anywhere in this analysis or in any figure reported here — noted for completeness, it does not affect any number above.

peak AS (
  SELECT place_id, place_name, max(idx) AS peak_idx,

  FROM ann a WHERE data_year BETWEEN 2004 AND 2008
  GROUP BY place_id, place_name
),
trough AS (
  SELECT place_id, min(idx) AS trough_idx,
         (SELECT data_year FROM ann a2 WHERE a2.place_id=a.place_id AND a2.data_year BETWEEN 2008 AND 2013 ORDER BY idx ASC FETCH FIRST 1 ROW ONLY) AS trough_year
  FROM ann a WHERE data_year BETWEEN 2008 AND 2013
  GROUP BY place_id
),
latest AS (
