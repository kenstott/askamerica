Rural US counties have persistently worse health than urban ones -- roughly 20-47% higher premature-death rates -- but that gap is more state-specific than state-independent

CDC/NCHS + County Health Rankings + USDA Rural-Urban Continuum, joined at county grain

Summary

Rural (nonmetro) US counties have meaningfully worse health than urban (metro) counties, and the gap has been present and roughly stable-to-widening for at least the last two decades. Nationally, the population-weighted premature-death rate (years of life lost before age 75, per 100,000, current County Health Rankings release) is about 47% higher in nonmetro counties (713.8) than metro counties (484.3); the simple across-county average puts it at 18-20% higher. Published NCHS research (not this corpus's own computation) shows this gap has been widening for 20 years: rural all-cause age-adjusted mortality was 7% above urban in 1999 and 20% above by 2019, with the working-age (25-54) gap widening even more sharply, from 6% to 43%.

The gap does not look the same within a state as it does between states. Computed directly here across 47 states with both metro and nonmetro counties: the average within-state rural-minus-urban gap is +1,784 YPLL/100k (rural worse in 44 of 47 states, one-sample t-test vs. zero, p<0.0001) -- real and highly consistent in direction. But the state-to-state spread in overall mortality level (SD of a state's average urban rate = 2,086; SD of its average rural rate = 2,617) is actually LARGER than the average within-state urban-rural gap itself (1,784), and urban and rural rates within the same state are strongly correlated (r=0.83). In plain terms: which STATE a county sits in predicts its health outcome about as much as, or more than, whether that county is rural or urban.

What the literature already establishes (20-year trend)

NCHS's own analysis of 1999-2019 national vital statistics (Curtin, published on the CDC/NCHS blog) found: the overall age-adjusted death rate in rural counties rose from 7% higher than urban in 1999 to 20% higher by 2019; the gap held for all 10 leading causes of death; and the working-age (25-54) natural-cause mortality gap widened from 6% to 43% over the same period. A separate NCHS study (1969-2009) found essentially no rural-urban life-expectancy gap in 1969 but a statistically significant 2-year gap by 2009 (78.8 urban vs. 76.8 rural years). These figures are cited from the published sources, not recomputed here, because askamerica's own county-grain historical series for this measure (health.chr_premature_death_trends) is currently broken -- every query against it fails with an S3 404 -- and has been reported as a defect.

Exclusions made in this analysis, disclosed with before/after figures

Query 1 (national cross-section). c.premature_death_rate IS NOT NULL removed 64 of 3,144 joined county rows (2.0%) in the health.chr_premature_death x geo.rural_urban_continuum join -- counties where CHR suppresses the rate for a small numerator, disproportionately small rural counties. Headline statistic WITH c.premature_death_rate IS NOT NULL applied: population-weighted 484.3 (metro) vs. 713.8 (nonmetro) per 100k, unweighted average 9,359 (metro) vs. 11,051 (nonmetro). Headline statistic WITHOUT c.premature_death_rate IS NOT NULL (re-running the identical SELECT with that clause deleted) is numerically IDENTICAL, because SQL's AVG() and SUM() already ignore NULL values on their own -- removing the explicit filter does not add any NULL rate into the average, it only changes which rows COUNT(*) can see. The one real effect of the 64-row loss is a 2.0% reduction in county coverage skewed toward the smallest rural counties, which if anything understates rather than overstates the true nonmetro rate.

Query 2 (2019-2024 overdose trend). m.rate IS NOT NULL removed 689 of 18,813 matching county-year rows (3.7%) in the health.cdc_county_injury_mortality x geo.rural_urban_continuum join (intent='Drug_OD', 2019-2024) -- counties where CDC suppresses the crude rate for 1-9 deaths. Headline statistic WITH m.rate IS NOT NULL applied: metro rates 18.85-30.40/100k and nonmetro rates 12.03-22.26/100k across 2019-2024 (the chart above). Headline statistic WITHOUT m.rate IS NOT NULL is again numerically IDENTICAL for the same reason -- AVG() already skips NULL rates whether or not the filter is written explicitly. The real effect of the 689-row loss is coverage loss concentrated in small rural counties, meaning the reported nonmetro overdose rate is a floor on the true rate, not a ceiling.

What was computed directly from this corpus

Recent multi-year trend (proxy): This corpus's county-level drug-overdose mortality series is not the broad 'health' measure the question asks about, but it is this corpus's best available multi-year county-grain series and stands in as a directly-computed recent trend, not a replacement for the broken 20-year table. Metro counties averaged higher overdose rates than nonmetro throughout 2019-2024, and the metro-nonmetro overdose GAP narrowed somewhat by 2023-24 as fentanyl-driven overdose deaths, historically more urban, spread further into rural areas. This cuts against a simple 'rural always worse, always widening' story for this one specific cause; the broader all-cause CHR premature-death measure (and the cited NCHS literature) is the more decisive evidence for the overall rural-urban health penalty.

Within-state vs. between-state gap: Grouping the CHR/RUCC join by state (47 states have both metro and nonmetro counties -- 3 states/territories are excluded because they lack one category) and averaging premature-death rate by metro_nonmetro within each state: the average within-state rural-minus-urban gap is +1,784 YPLL/100k, with rural higher in 44 of 47 states (one-sample t-test against zero: t=8.40, p=7.6e-11, n=47). But this within-state gap has a standard deviation across states of 1,456 -- nearly as large as its own mean -- ranging from -1,260 (Delaware and Wyoming, where rural is essentially even with or slightly better than urban) to +7,131 (Arizona). Compare that to the between-state spread in the LEVEL of health itself: the standard deviation of a state's own average urban rate is 2,086 and of its average rural rate is 2,617 -- both larger than the average within-state gap -- and urban and rural rates within the same state correlate at r=0.83. That correlation is the key finding for the second half of the question: most of a county's health outcome is explained by which state it is in (Mississippi, West Virginia, and Arizona have both high urban AND high rural rates; Idaho, Utah, and Massachusetts have both low urban and low rural rates), and only secondarily by whether it is classified rural or urban. The rural penalty is real and statistically robust, but its SIZE is itself state-dependent -- it is not one fixed number that applies uniformly everywhere.

Other caveats and data-quality notes

  • health.chr_premature_death_trends -- the intended 20-year county-grain historical series -- is currently broken in this corpus (S3 404 on every describe_table/query call). This was reported as a data-quality issue.
  • health.chr_premature_death itself carries only a single release_year (2025) live in this corpus, so it supports a cross-sectional urban/rural comparison, not a trend.
  • geo.rural_urban_continuum is a single 2023 vintage (RUCC updates only once per decade), applied as a stable classification across all years used here.
  • Premature Death (YPLL before age 75) is a truncated mortality measure, not identical to all-ages age-adjusted mortality or life expectancy; the NCHS-cited figures use different measures, so percentages are not directly additive across sources.
  • The state-level analysis (n=47) is a small-n comparison; a few state gap estimates (Alaska n=3 urban counties, Hawaii n=2, Wyoming n=2 urban) rest on very few counties and are noisy.

What This Report Does Not Answer

  • Cover the last twenty years specifically: This corpus's only county-grain 20-year trend table (health.chr_premature_death_trends, declared 1997-2023) is broken (S3 404 on every call) and was reported as a defect. The 20-year trend is therefore sourced from published NCHS literature rather than recomputed directly here.
