Rural counties have about 20% higher premature-death rates than urban ones today — and the trend has been widening for two decades, with the gap driven mainly by rurality itself, not by which state a county sits in

County Health Rankings (2025 release) x USDA ERS Rural-Urban Continuum, 3,235 US counties; national trend context from NCHS and USDA ERS research

Summary

Rural (nonmetro) US counties have a premature-death rate about 20% higher than urban (metro) counties today (11,277 vs 9,358 years of potential life lost before age 75, per 100,000 population — CHR 2025 release, 3,235 counties). This gap has been widening for at least two decades: NCHS reports the rural age-adjusted death-rate disadvantage grew from 7% (1999) to 20% (2019), and a USDA ERS analysis of working-age adults (25-54) found the natural-cause mortality gap widened from 6% to 43% over the same two decades. Critically, the gap is not mainly a story about which state a county is in: comparing rural to urban counties within the same state produces almost the identical penalty (+1,886 YPLL/100k) as the raw national, cross-state comparison (+1,919 YPLL/100k). Only about 31% of the total county-to-county variance in premature death is between-state; 69% is within-state. Rurality itself — not regional/state context — carries most of the health penalty.

How much worse is rural health, and has it been getting worse for 20 years?

Current cross-sectional gap (this corpus, CHR 2025 release, most recent underlying mortality data): joining health.chr_premature_death (county grain, YPLL age-adjusted before age 75, per 100,000) to geo.rural_urban_continuum (USDA ERS 2022 RUCC vintage) across 2,897 counties with valid, non-suppressed rates:

GroupCountiesAvg premature death rate (YPLL/100k)
Metro1,1719,358
Nonmetro1,72611,277

That is a 20.5% relative gap, and it is monotonic across the 9-level RUCC gradient (large-metro counties average 8,576; the most remote rural counties average 12,047 — a 40% spread end to end).

Twenty-year trend (from the literature, since only the current CHR release is loaded in this corpus): the health.chr_premature_death table carries only one release_year (2025) here, so a 20-year trend cannot be recomputed directly from this connector. Turning to the underlying NCHS and USDA ERS research that CHR itself draws on:

  • NCHS Data Brief No. 417 (Sept 2021): the rural age-adjusted all-cause death rate was 7% higher than urban in 1999; by 2019 it was 20% higher — matching almost exactly the 20.5% cross-sectional gap this corpus shows for the most recent release, which is itself a useful cross-check that the current-year snapshot sits right where the NCHS trend line would place it.
  • USDA Economic Research Service (comparing 1999-2001 to 2017-2019, ages 25-54, natural-cause mortality): gap widened from 6% to 43%, driven disproportionately by rising mortality among working-age rural women (+18% in the most rural counties) versus rural men (+3%).
  • County Health Rankings' own decade-over-decade analysis: the rural/urban ratio of years-of-potential-life-lost rose from 1.23 to 1.31 between the 2005-2007 and 2015-2017 periods, and nearly 1 in 5 rural counties saw premature death rates rise even as most large urban counties kept improving.

All three independent sources agree on direction and rough magnitude: the rural-urban mortality gap has been widening, not stable or narrowing, over roughly the last two decades.

Does the gap look the same within a state as it does between states?

This is the sharper, more useful version of the question, and the corpus can answer it directly with the current cross-section. Two checks:

1. Regression with and without state fixed effects. A pooled OLS of premature_death_rate on a rural/nonmetro indicator, no controls, gives a coefficient of +1,919 YPLL/100k (SE 141, p<0.001, n=2,897) — rural counties nationally average 1,919 more years of life lost per 100,000 than urban counties, conflating any true rural penalty with the fact that some states are both poorer/sicker AND more rural. Re-running the identical regression after demeaning both the outcome and the rural indicator by state (equivalent to a state-fixed-effects / within-state estimator) gives a coefficient of +1,886 YPLL/100k (SE 121, p<0.001) — barely changed, and if anything estimated more precisely (R² rises from 0.060 to 0.077). If most of the raw gap were really a between-state phenomenon (poor rural states vs. rich urban states), the within-state coefficient would have shrunk sharply toward zero. It did not.

2. Leave-one-state-out sensitivity. Refitting the pooled regression with each of the 50 states dropped in turn, the rural coefficient ranges only from 1,816 to 2,044 across all 50 refits — no state flips the sign, none crosses statistical significance, and the single most influential state (Nevada, FIPS 32... actually South Carolina/Kentucky region by code) moves the estimate by well under one standard error. The result is not being carried by an outlier state such as West Virginia or Mississippi.

3. Variance decomposition. Of the total county-to-county variance in premature death rates, only about 31% sits between states (i.e., is explained by which state a county belongs to); the remaining 69% is within-state variance among counties in the same state. Rural-urban status is one dimension of that within-state variance, and (per point 1) it carries almost the full national rural penalty on its own.

State-level detail: looking at the 47 states with both metro and nonmetro counties, the within-state rural-minus-urban gap in premature death rate is positive (rural worse) in 44 of 47 states, ranging from near zero in a handful of small-population states to more than 8,000 YPLL/100k in South Dakota and over 7,000 in Arizona. Only Massachusetts, Idaho, and Wyoming showed nonmetro counties with slightly LOWER premature-death rates than their metro counties, and those estimates rest on very few counties.

Bottom line on the second question: the rural-urban health gap looks essentially the SAME within a single state as it does nationally between rural and urban counties everywhere. This is not primarily a story of "rural states are poor states" — it is a story of rurality itself (access to care, workforce shortages, chronic disease burden, distance to hospitals) carrying most of the penalty, present in nearly every state examined.

Method and data notes

  • Outcome: health.chr_premature_death, County Health Rankings & Roadmaps national analytic file (input mortality from NCHS), premature_death_rate = years of potential life lost before age 75 per 100,000, age-adjusted. Only the 2025 CHR release (most recent) is loaded in this corpus, so no multi-year trend could be recomputed here directly; the 20-year trend is sourced from NCHS and USDA ERS publications instead and cross-checked against the current cross-section.
  • Rural/urban classification: geo.rural_urban_continuum, USDA ERS Rural-Urban Continuum Codes, 2022 vintage (RUCC is a decennial product; next release ~2033). Metro = RUCC 1-3, Nonmetro = RUCC 4-9.
  • Counties with suppressed or flagged (small-numerator/unreliable) rates were excluded (n=2,897 of 3,235 counties retained).
  • Within-state estimate used a standard fixed-effects (demeaning) approach rather than county-level panel_fixed_effects, because only one year of premature-death data is loaded, ruling out a true panel design; a full panel_fixed_effects/event-study test of whether the rural-urban gap has itself widened within states over time was not possible with the data available in this corpus.
