A small slice of NFIP properties — repeat flooders — account for a wildly disproportionate share of federal flood claims

FEMA National Flood Insurance Program (NFIP) data, as compiled by NRDC (Dec. 2022 FEMA SRLP dataset) and Pew Charitable Trusts (2016, citing FEMA/CRS)

Summary

Federal flood claims are heavily concentrated in a small number of properties that flood again and again. Historically, Repetitive Loss (RL) properties - those with 2+ paid claims of $1,000+ within any 10-year window since 1978 - have made up only about 1% of NFIP policies but 25-30% of all flood claims paid (Pew Charitable Trusts, 2016, citing FEMA and CRS data). Using FEMA's stricter Severe Repetitive Loss (SRL) definition (4+ claims over $5,000 each, or 2+ building claims whose total exceeds the property's value), FEMA's most recent compiled dataset (as of December 2022, obtained and analyzed by NRDC) shows roughly 44,000 SRL properties - under 1% of NFIP policies - accounted for more than 10% of all program claims paid. Under either definition, claims concentration is extreme: a sliver of properties drives a share of total losses ten to thirty times larger than their share of the policy book.

Two overlapping but distinct definitions

NFIP tracks two nested categories of repeat flooders, and it matters which one a given statistic uses:

  • Repetitive Loss (RL): 2 or more separate claims of more than $1,000 each (building and/or contents) within any rolling 10-year period since 1978. This is the broader category.
  • Severe Repetitive Loss (SRL): a subset of RL - either (a) 4+ separate claims over $5,000 each with cumulative payments over $20,000, or (b) 2+ building-only claims whose total exceeds the building's fair market value. As of the December 2022 FEMA dataset, there were approximately 44,000 SRL properties nationwide (NRDC, 'Losing Ground,' Dec. 2023 fact sheet).

Because SRL is a subset of RL, the SRL numbers (44,000 properties, >10% of claims) are not additive with the broader RL numbers (roughly 150,000+ properties historically, 25-30% of claims per Pew's 2016 infographic, itself citing a 2012 CRS report and FEMA drilldown data). Both point in the same direction - concentration is severe - but they are two different cuts, from different vintages, and should not be summed or averaged together.

What this has cost the program

Pew's 2016 analysis found that repeatedly flooded properties had cost the NFIP more than $12.5 billion cumulatively - equivalent to roughly half of the program's $23 billion debt to the U.S. Treasury at that time. Pew also found the number of repeatedly-flooded properties (RL) was growing by an estimated 5,000 per year, and that about 1 in 10 repeatedly-flooded properties had already received payments exceeding the property's own value. NRDC's more recent SRL analysis (Dec. 2022 data) found that only about a quarter of SRL properties (10,601 of 44,616) had ever been mitigated (elevated, demolished/bought out, relocated, or replaced) - meaning the large majority of the highest-risk repeat flooders remain in place and insurable today.

What this connector's own NFIP claims table can and cannot show

This corpus's disasters.nfip_claims table (FEMA's public OpenFEMA claims file, 2,755,527 claim records, 1978-2026, totaling $95.4 billion in paid claims across all categories) does NOT carry a property or building identifier - FEMA redacts street addresses and any unique structure ID from the public claims file for privacy, and even latitude/longitude are rounded to one decimal degree (roughly 11 km), far too coarse to distinguish individual properties. This is a documented, structural limitation of the public dataset, not an ingestion gap: the Congressional Research Service's own March 26, 2026 testimony on this exact topic states plainly that 'no information is available online with which to calculate total claims from multiple loss structures' from the public OpenFEMA claims file alone. FEMA's separate, non-public 'NFIP Multiple Loss Properties' extract (which NRDC obtained directly from FEMA under a data-sharing arrangement, not from the public API) is the actual source underlying the RL/SRL claims-share statistics cited above. Because of this, the concentration figures in this report come from primary analyses that had privileged access to FEMA's redacted-but-linked property-level extract (NRDC, Pew/CRS), not from a computation run directly against this corpus's public claims table.
