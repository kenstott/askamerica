A tiny share of NFIP-insured properties (~1%) has historically accounted for 25-30% of all flood claims dollars

FEMA/GAO/Pew figures on repetitive-loss and severe-repetitive-loss properties, corroborated geographically against AskAmerica's NFIP claims data

Summary

Federal flood claims are extremely concentrated in a small number of repeat-flooding properties. Historically, properties that have flooded and been paid at least twice under the National Flood Insurance Program (NFIP) - FEMA's official "repetitive loss" (RL) definition - make up only about 1% of NFIP-insured properties but have accounted for roughly 25-30% of all NFIP flood-claim dollars paid since the program's 1978 inception. A narrower, more extreme subset - "severe repetitive loss" (SRL) properties, generally those with 4+ paid claims over $5,000, or 2+ building-only claims exceeding the property's value - is smaller still (roughly 1-2.5% of policies in various FEMA/OIG estimates) and by some FEMA figures accounts for close to half of total claims dollars. As of Pew's 2016 accounting, more than 150,000 U.S. properties had flooded repeatedly, costing the NFIP over $12.5 billion - equivalent to about half of the program's $23 billion debt at the time, and the number of such properties was growing by an estimated 5,000/year. FEMA's own public per-claim data (used in this corpus) confirms that flood losses are heavily geographically concentrated (county-level Gini = 0.96), consistent with - but not a re-derivation of - the property-level figures, because the public claims file has no property identifier.

The headline numbers, and where they come from

  • 1% of properties, 25-30% of claims - The Pew Charitable Trusts (2016), drawing on FEMA and a 2012 Congressional Research Service report: "repeatedly flooded properties have accounted for just 1 percent of properties with National Flood Insurance Program policies but about 25-30 percent of flood claims." Pew put the cumulative cost at more than $12.5 billion, roughly half of the NFIP's $23 billion debt at the time, spread across more than 150,000 repeatedly-flooded properties nationwide, with the count growing by about 5,000/year.
  • 1% of policies, 38% of claims (1978-2004) - An earlier GAO accounting (cited widely, e.g. by NRDC and FEMA program guidance) found repetitive-loss properties made up 1% of policies but 38% of claims dollars over 1978-2004, an even sharper concentration than the later Pew figure, reflecting how the estimate moves somewhat by window and by whose claims file is being summarized.
  • Severe repetitive loss (SRL): ~1-2.5% of policies, up to ~50% of claims by value - FEMA's SRL definition (4+ paid claims over $5,000, or 2+ building-only payments exceeding the property's value) targets the most extreme repeat-flooders. Estimates of the SRL share of total claims dollars vary by source and period (roughly 10% to nearly 50% depending on the study and window), but every source agrees SRL properties are a small fraction of policies carrying a share of claims many multiples larger than their share of the book of business.
  • About 1 in 10 repeatedly-flooded properties have received cumulative payments worth more than the property's own value (DHS Office of Inspector General, 2009), and 21 states have 1,000+ repeatedly-flooded properties.

Why this can't be recomputed property-by-property from AskAmerica's own NFIP data

AskAmerica's disasters.nfip_claims table is FEMA's public per-claim extract (OpenFEMA FimaNfipClaims), and FEMA deliberately strips any property-level identifier from that public file for privacy - it truncates latitude/longitude to one decimal place (roughly 11 km resolution). We confirmed this directly: in 2022 alone, 60,209 individual paid claims resolve to only 2,756 distinct lat/lon pairs and 3,289 distinct ZIP codes - nowhere near enough resolution to link two claims to the same physical address. FEMA does track true repetitive-loss status internally and publishes an aggregated, privacy-preserving "NFIP Multiple Loss Properties" summary dataset for exactly this question, but that aggregate is a separate FEMA product outside this corpus, not a table we can query here. This is a genuine corpus coverage gap, not an oversight: the property-level figures above are the right way to answer this question, sourced directly from FEMA/GAO/Pew rather than recomputed.

What the corpus data can corroborate: extreme geographic concentration

What disasters.nfip_claims can support is a coarser, county-grain check on the same underlying phenomenon - that flood losses cluster heavily rather than spreading evenly. Across all loaded years (2010-2026, 966,722 paid claims totaling $55.76 billion; note 2018 is an interior data gap in this corpus), the distribution of total dollars paid across the 2,656 counties with paid claims has a Gini coefficient of 0.96 (0 = perfectly even, 1 = one unit holds everything) - the bottom 90% of counties by dollars paid account for only about 5.3% of total claims dollars, while the top decile of counties carries essentially all the rest. This is directionally consistent with a small number of places (and, per FEMA/GAO/Pew, a small number of properties within those places) carrying most of the claims burden, but it is a geography-level statistic, not a re-derivation of the 1%-of-properties / 25-30%-of-claims figure, and should not be quoted as if it were.
