FDA review time has been roughly flat to modestly faster over 20 years, but total drug development time has lengthened

FDA CDER median review times, 2005-2022, with context on total development timelines. Prepared 2026-09-10.

Summary

It depends which clock you mean. The FDA's own review clock (time from application submission to an approval decision) has been essentially flat to modestly improved over the past 20 years: standard-review drugs took a median of about 11.8 months in 2005 and about 10.1 months in 2016 (roughly -1.7 months), while priority-review drugs actually got slightly slower on FDA's own scorecard, from 6.0 months in 2005 to 8.0 months in 2016 (+2.0 months), mainly because a 2012 program change (the 'Program for Enhanced Review Transparency') added review-clock time for novel drugs. Averaged across all approvals and reported in FDA-year eras, the broader academic literature puts overall median review time at about 12.9 months for 1992-2012 versus 9.9 months for 2012-2022 -- a modest further improvement, not a big swing.

What HAS changed a lot over 20 years is the volume and pathway mix, not the review clock itself: CDER approved roughly 25 novel drugs a year in the 2000s versus roughly 50 a year since 2018 (about double), driven by a big rise in use of expedited pathways (fast track, breakthrough therapy, priority review, accelerated approval) -- from about 42% of drug-indication pairs in 2008 to about 75% in 2021. Separately, the total time to bring a new drug from first-in-human trials to approval (which the FDA review clock is only the last, smallest piece of) has if anything lengthened somewhat over this period, with recent industry analyses putting average clinical development (Phase 1 to approval) at roughly 10.5 years versus a slightly shorter figure two decades ago -- so the part regulators control has held steady or improved a bit while the part sponsors control (running trials) has not gotten shorter.

What changed in the FDA's own review clock, 2005-2025

FDA publishes its own year-by-year median review-time table for CDER's NDA/BLA approvals (Priority vs. Standard). The table was last updated by FDA through calendar year 2016; its final decade of data (2005-2016) is charted above.

  • Standard review (the majority of novel drugs, roughly 10-month PDUFA goal): median FDA review time went from 11.8 months (2005) to 10.1 months (2016), a decrease of about 1.7 months, with a bumpy path in between (peaking at 13.0 months in 2008).
  • Priority review (significant therapeutic improvement, 6-month PDUFA goal): median FDA review time went from 6.0 months (2005) to 8.0 months (2016), an INCREASE of about 2 months. FDA's own footnote attributes part of this to a 2012 process change (PDUFA V's 'Program for Enhanced Review Transparency and Communication') that added review-clock time for novel-drug applications, starting the clock only after a 60-day filing period -- a methodological change, not necessarily slower underlying agency work.

A peer-reviewed 2024 analysis in Scientific Reports of every FDA new-drug/biologic approval from 1980-2022 (1,355 products) corroborates the plateau: median review time fell sharply after PDUFA's 1992 enactment (from 26.6 months pre-PDUFA) and then kept falling only modestly across the last 20 years of that window -- 12.9 months for the PDUFA-to-FDASIA era (1992-2012) versus 9.9 months for the FDASIA-to-2022 era (2012-2022). A separate study of 2015-2022 approvals found priority-review drugs averaged 9.4 months (median 8.0) versus 18.6 months (median 12.0) for standard-review-only drugs -- consistent with FDA's own recent figures and showing the priority/standard gap has remained wide and stable in the last decade.

What changed a lot: approval volume and expedited-pathway use

The most dramatic 20-year change is not the review clock but the pipeline feeding it. CDER approved an average of about 25 novel drugs a year in the 2000s, rising to about 34/year in 2011-2017 and roughly 50/year from 2018 through 2024-2025. Over the same window the share of approvals using at least one FDA expedited program (fast track, breakthrough therapy, priority review, accelerated approval, orphan designation) rose from about 42% (2008) to about 75% (2021), and in the 1980-2022 Scientific Reports dataset, the FDASIA-2022 era (2012-2022) used at least one expedited pathway on 74% of approvals versus 49% in both earlier eras. So the practical experience of 'getting a new drug approved' has shifted heavily toward faster, expedited tracks becoming the norm rather than the exception -- even though the underlying review-time goals (6 months priority / 10 months standard) have not changed since the 1990s.

The bigger, and less favorable, trend: total development time

The FDA review period is only the final stage of getting a drug approved; most of the calendar time is the preceding clinical-trial program, which the FDA does not control and which has not shortened. Industry-standard estimates (PhRMA, Tufts CSDD-style analyses, and a BIO study of ~9,700 development programs from 2011-2020) put total time from Phase 1 trials to approval at roughly 10-15 years, with the BIO analysis specifically finding an average of about 10.5 years for the 2011-2020 cohort. Earlier retrospective studies found clinical-trial time prior to filing rose from a mean of about 5.7 years for drugs approved 1980-1984 to about 6.4 years for drugs approved 2005-2009, and a UK antiviral-drug study found total clinical development time roughly doubled between the 1980s and the 2004-2014 period. Taken together with the roughly flat FDA review clock, this means the OVERALL time from starting human trials to getting a new drug approved has not shortened over the last 20 years, and by some measures has modestly lengthened -- even though the specific, FDA-controlled review-decision step has stayed about the same or improved slightly.

Data and methodology notes

AskAmerica's own health.fda_drug_approvals table (openFDA NDC directory) was checked first and does NOT carry an original-submission date or a review-decision date -- only a single 'latest submission date/status' field with no year-coverage dimension -- so it cannot compute review-time-to-approval directly; this is a genuine gap in that table for this specific question. Given that, this answer is built entirely from primary government and peer-reviewed sources fetched directly: FDA's own official CDER approval-times table (fda.gov, covering calendar years 1993-2016, its last published update) and a peer-reviewed reanalysis of the FDA's full 1980-2022 approval record (Scientific Reports, 2024), supplemented by a 2015-2022 review-time study and industry drug-development-timeline analyses (BIO, PhRMA) for the 'total development time' context. No figure here was estimated or extrapolated by the analyst; every number is quoted directly from the cited source's own reported statistic.
