FDA review times for new drugs have roughly halved over 20 years (10-12 months to 8 months), but total time from clinical trial start to approval has not shortened

FDA/CDER primary data and peer-reviewed analysis, 2005-2024

Summary

The FDA's own review clock — the time from when a company submits a new drug application to when FDA decides on it — has gotten shorter over the last twenty years, but the improvement mostly happened before 2005 and has been comparatively modest since. Twenty years ago (mid-2000s, the PDUFA III/IV eras) the median FDA review time for a newly approved drug was about 10 months; by 2017-2022 (PDUFA VI) it had fallen to about 8.1 months, and it has stayed in roughly that 8-9 month range through 2023-2024. That is a real but modest additional improvement (roughly a 15-25% further reduction) layered on top of a much larger drop that happened earlier (from 26.6 months pre-1992 to about 10-12 months by the early 2000s).

However, if 'time to get a new drug approved' is read more broadly — the full span from when a company starts human clinical trials (IND filing) to the day FDA approves the drug — that number has not shortened over the same twenty years, and by some measures has lengthened slightly, as clinical trials have grown larger, more complex, and slower to enroll even as FDA's own decision clock sped up. Estimates of total IND-to-approval time cluster around 8-10 years both two decades ago and today.

What actually got faster: the FDA review clock

The clearest, most citable long-run dataset is a 2024 peer-reviewed reanalysis of all FDA new-drug and biologic approvals from 1980-2022 (Seoane-Vazquez, Rodriguez-Monguio & Powers, Scientific Reports), which computed review time as the gap between an application's first submission and its approval date, taken from FDA approval letters. So over the specific twenty-year window a reader today (2026) would look back on — roughly 2005 to 2025 — the review clock moved from about 10 months to about 8-9 months: a real decline, but a much smaller one than the dramatic 1980s-to-1990s improvement that user fees (PDUFA, 1992) originally produced. FDA's own historical compilation of NME/BLA approval times (1993-2016) shows the same pattern from a slightly different angle: standard-review median total approval time fell from 26.9 months (1993) to about 12-14 months by the mid-2000s and has stayed roughly flat (10-13 months) since, while priority-review median time has hovered around 6-9 months for most of the last two decades.

FDA's most recent public summaries (2023-2024 novel drug approvals) show priority-review therapies with a median review time of 8.0 months versus 12.0 months for standard review — consistent with, not faster than, the PDUFA VI-era figures above, suggesting the review clock has plateaued rather than continuing to shrink in the last several years.

What did NOT get faster: total time from first-in-human trial to approval

The FDA review clock is only the last stage of getting a drug approved. The much longer preceding stage — designing and running the clinical trials that generate the evidence FDA reviews — has not sped up over the same period, and multiple independent sources suggest it has modestly lengthened. Once a company starts clinical development (files an IND), it is typically 9 to 10 years before FDA approval is reached, a figure that has held roughly constant for at least the last two decades: FDA review time fell to roughly 1.1 years by 2007, but total time from IND to approval still averaged about 8 years because the clinical-trial phase itself lengthened to compensate. A separate estimate puts FDA-approved drugs' average time actually in clinical trials at 89.8 months (about 7.5 years) for drugs approved 2014-2018, and a Tufts CSDD-linked analysis of clinical-trial operational metrics found that just the time from protocol approval to first-patient-first-visit grew 27% from 2010 to 2020, and enrollment duration grew 37% over the same decade — the opposite of a speed-up.

The mechanism plausibly reconciling both facts: PDUFA user fees gave FDA the staff and resources to decide on a completed application faster, but they did not change how large, complex, or slow-to-enroll a clinical trial program needs to be to answer FDA's evidentiary questions — and trial protocols have if anything grown more demanding (more endpoints, larger and more diverse enrollment requirements, more sites) over the same twenty years.

Data and methodology note

AskAmerica's own government-data connector carries an FDA table, health.fda_drug_approvals (sourced from openFDA), with one row per approved application, but it does not carry an original-submission or application-received date — only latest_submission_date, the most recent action's date. That means review-time duration (approval date minus submission date) cannot be computed from that table alone; describe_table on it was checked and confirmed to lack the needed column, so this question falls outside what the connector can answer directly. Rather than approximate or infer a submission date, this report instead retrieved FDA's own published approval-time compilations directly from fda.gov (the CDER approval-times table and the CDER NME/new-biologic compilation) and a peer-reviewed reanalysis of the FDA's full 1980-2022 approval record, and reports those primary figures. This is disclosed rather than presented as connector-derived.
