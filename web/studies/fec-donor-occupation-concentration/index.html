Federal Campaigns Are Funded Mainly by Corporate Executives, Investors, and the Retired Wealthy — Not the Public at Large

FEC individual-contribution data, 2023-2024 election cycle, cross-checked against OpenSecrets, USAFacts, and Visual Capitalist reporting

Summary

Federal campaigns are funded overwhelmingly by a narrow slice of wealthy professionals, not by a representative cross-section of working Americans. Of the $9.58 billion in itemized individual contributions to candidates, parties, PACs and Super PACs in the 2023-2024 cycle, over a third ($3.26B, 34%) came from single checks larger than $100,000 — almost entirely uncapped money to Super PACs. By self-reported occupation, the single largest category is "Retired" ($1.63B), followed by "Not Employed" ($1.07B, which FEC codes also apply to homemakers and students) — both dominated in dollar terms by wealthy retirees, not typical retirees on fixed incomes. Among people who report a working occupation, the money is led by CEOs ($744M), attorneys ($293M), company presidents ($270M), physicians ($252M), executives ($246M), and investors ($214M). At the very top, the ten largest individual donors of the cycle — led by Elon Musk ($207.7M, SpaceX/Tesla CEO), Timothy Mellon ($197M, banking-family heir/investor), Miriam Adelson ($126M, casino fortune, self-reported occupation "physician"), Richard Uihlein ($105.7M, Uline CEO), and Jeffrey Yass ($83.1M, trading-firm executive) — together gave more than $1.2 billion, almost all of it from finance, real estate, energy and tech executives.

Step 1 — What the literature already says

OpenSecrets, the standard outside tracker of this question, reports the same top-donor roster this analysis found independently: Musk led all 2024 donors with roughly $291M in total political spending (a broader figure than the FEC-itemized-only number here, since it includes non-itemized categories and later-reported amounts), followed by Mellon (~$197M), Adelson (~$148M), the Uihleins (~$143M), Kenneth Griffin (~$108M), and Jeffrey and Janine Yass (~$101M) — all Republican-aligned. The two largest Democratic donors were Michael Bloomberg (~$64M) and Dustin Moskovitz (~$51M). OpenSecrets and USN&WR both frame this as a story of billionaire concentration: eight of the top ten donors leaned Republican, and the top ten collectively contributed over $1.2 billion. Separately, OpenSecrets' sector data (not independently re-derived here, cited as reported) finds Finance/Insurance/Real Estate is the largest-giving economic sector, with Securities/Investment the single largest industry, when PAC and party contributions are included alongside individual money. USAFacts notes that PACs and party committees, not individuals, account for the majority (over 75%) of all federal election fundraising by dollar volume — the individual-donor picture in this report is specifically about the itemized-individual-contribution channel, one important piece of a larger financing system that also includes PAC and party money and unlimited independent expenditures.

This dataset's per-donor totals sit close to but somewhat below OpenSecrets' figures for the same names (e.g. Musk $207.7M here vs. ~$291M reported by OpenSecrets, Adelson $126M vs. ~$148M) — the gap is expected and explainable: OpenSecrets' totals include additional non-itemized and later-amended filings, joint-fundraising-committee allocations, and giving that flows through entities/LLCs not captured under an individual's own name in the raw FEC record. The rank ordering and occupational makeup, however, match published reporting closely, which is a useful cross-check that the underlying FEC data and this query are sound.

Two caveats on reading this table literally. First, "Retired" and "Not Employed" are the FEC's catch-all codes for anyone without a current employer, which captures both a modest-income retiree giving $25 and a retired billionaire (e.g. Jan Koum, Fred Eychaner) giving $20M — the size of the "Retired" total is driven overwhelmingly by the latter, since a handful of nine-figure gifts from wealthy retired executives can outweigh millions of small transactions. Second, occupation is self-reported and not standardized: Miriam Adelson (widely known for her family's casino fortune) lists "Physician"; Timothy Mellon (widely known as a banking-family heir) lists "Investments." A donor's FEC occupation field describes what they told the treasurer, not necessarily their primary public identity or source of wealth.

Step 4 — How concentrated the money is

Of the $9.58B total, $3.26B (34%) arrived in single checks larger than $100,000. Nearly all of this large-check money uses FEC transaction code 10 — contributions to Super PACs and other nonfederal, uncapped accounts created after Citizens United (2010) and SpeechNow.org v. FEC (2010) — which have no per-donor contribution limit, unlike the $3,300-per-election cap on ordinary candidate-committee giving (code 15). By contrast, at the candidate-committee level specifically (fec.candidate_summaries, which excludes Super PAC and party money), itemized (>$200) contributions totaled $2.53B versus $1.21B unitemized (<=$200) for the 2024 filing year — itemized, larger-dollar giving is roughly twice unitemized small-dollar giving even within candidate committees alone, before Super PAC money is added at all.

Methodology and data caveats

  • Channel measured: This report covers itemized individual contributions (FEC transaction types 15, 15E, and 10; entity_type='IND') for the 2023-2024 cycle. It does not include PAC-to-candidate money, party committee money, or independent expenditures reported without a named individual donor — those are separate FEC tables/channels with different leaders (e.g. labor unions and trade associations dominate direct PAC giving, which is a different question from who the underlying individual donors are).
  • Occupation field: Self-reported at the time of contribution, not standardized or verified by FEC, and can diverge from a donor's better-known public role.
  • Cross-check: Top-donor names, rank order, and rough magnitudes were verified against OpenSecrets/Visual Capitalist/USAFacts reporting before being presented; the FEC-derived per-donor totals here run slightly below OpenSecrets' broader totals for the reasons noted in Step 2.
  • Small-dollar giving: ActBlue and WinRed, the two dominant small-dollar aggregator PACs, raised over $1.7B combined in this cycle (per USAFacts/FEC), but that money is reported as a PAC-level receipt, not attributed to the underlying small donor's occupation in this table — so the occupation breakdown above is a picture of itemized, individually-attributed giving, not of the full universe of small online donors.

  2. FEC individual contributions, 2023-2024 cycle (top donors)
    Show SQL
    SELECT contributor_name, occupation, employer, SUM(amount) as total_amt, COUNT(*) as n_tx FROM fec.individual_contributions WHERE "year" IN ('2023','2024') AND transaction_type IN ('15','15E','10') AND entity_type = 'IND' AND (memo_cd <> 'X' OR memo_cd IS NULL) AND amount > 0 GROUP BY contributor_name, occupation, employer ORDER BY total_amt DESC
  3. FEC individual contributions, 2023-2024 cycle (total and check-size breakdown)
    Show SQL
