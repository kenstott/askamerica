The disaster-driven exodus is real but small, recent, and highly uneven — not a mass depopulation of at-risk counties

FEMA/NOAA storm damage, IRS SOI county migration, Census Population Estimates, cross-checked against Redfin, First Street, and Ton et al. (2024)

Summary

The premise needs correcting before it can be answered: repeated disaster damage is NOT driving mass depopulation of at-risk counties, as a general rule. We ranked the 25 US counties with the highest cumulative NOAA/FEMA storm-event property damage per capita (2010-2019) and checked what actually happened to their population and IRS-measured domestic migration through 2022 — nearly all of them GAINED population and GAINED net domestic migrants, because they are large metro or exurban counties (Houston-area Harris, Montgomery, Fort Bend, Galveston; coastal Louisiana's Ascension, Livingston) where broader economic pull outweighs disaster risk. A genuine, sustained exodus does exist, but it is the exception, not the rule, among hard-hit places — e.g. Jefferson County, TX (Beaumont-Port Arthur, hit hard by Harvey and repeated refinery-adjacent flooding) lost roughly 18,000 people net to domestic out-migration between 2012 and 2022, about 7% of its population, even while its total population looked almost flat because births and international arrivals backfilled the loss. At the national level, a real reversal is underway but is recent (2024-2025) and still small relative to the country's size: Redfin's analysis of Census migration data found the top decile of flood-risk counties had a net outflow of 63,357 people in 2025 (nearly double 2024's 34,099) — about 0.02% of the US population. First Street's peer-reviewed 'Climate Abandonment Area' research finds roughly 3.2 million Americans have already left high-flood-risk neighborhoods since 2000, out of about 17 million living in areas already declining, with a projected 5 million more lost by mid-century from flood risk alone — but that is a census-BLOCK-level finding, meaning the abandoned areas mostly sit inside counties that are still growing overall.

1. Testing the premise: are the hardest-hit counties actually depopulating?

We built our own "hardest-hit" ranking directly from FEMA/NOAA data: cumulative property damage per capita, 2010-2019, from disasters.disasters_storm_impact_by_county (NOAA Storm Events Database, joined to FEMA disaster declarations), divided by each county's 2010 population (census.decennial_population), restricted to counties with 2010 population above 20,000 to avoid tiny-county damage flukes.

The top of that list is dominated by the Texas Gulf Coast (repeatedly hit by Hurricanes Ike, Harvey and subsequent tropical storms) and coastal Louisiana. Checking population change 2010-2020 and again 2019-2023 for those same counties: of the top 25 by damage-per-capita, all but three GREW in population over the decade, and the IRS-measured net domestic migration (fiscal.county_migration_flows, individuals moving in minus individuals moving out, 2012-2022) was POSITIVE for the large majority in every year measured. Galveston County, Montgomery County, Fort Bend County and Harris County itself (the single largest county by absolute cumulative damage, $10.3 billion 2010-2019) all continued to add residents and net domestic migrants through 2022, despite being repeatedly declared disaster areas.

The one clear, sustained exception in this set is Jefferson County, TX (Beaumont-Port Arthur, on the Louisiana border) — every single year from 2012 to 2022, more people left the county than moved in, cumulating to roughly -18,000 net domestic out-migrants, against a 2010 base population of 252,273 (about 7%). Notably, the county's TOTAL population barely moved over the same period (252,273 in 2010, 256,526 in 2020, per the Decennial Census) — the domestic exodus was offset by births and international in-migration, meaning a total-population count alone would have missed the exodus entirely.

2. The national picture: real, but recent and modest in scale

Redfin's June 2026 analysis (Census Bureau domestic migration data paired with First Street property-level flood-risk scores) found that counties in the top 10% nationally for share of homes facing high flood risk (23.7%-99.1% of homes) had a NET DOMESTIC OUTFLOW of 63,357 people in the year ending mid-2025 — nearly double the 34,099 outflow the year before, which was itself the first outflow year in five. Low-flood-risk counties gained 69,857 residents in the same period, the largest gain since 2018. Miami-Dade County led all flood-prone counties with a net outflow of 72,254 (about 2.6% of its population); Harris County, TX (Houston) was second at -43,377.

Context matters for sizing this: a combined national swing of roughly 133,000 people between high- and low-flood-risk counties is a real and accelerating trend line, but against a US population of about 335 million it is on the order of 0.02-0.04% of the population moving for reasons that plausibly include (per Redfin's own survey) climate risk, insurance costs, and unrelated factors like affordability and politics — the study is explicit that flood risk is one of several forces, not the sole driver, and that migration data does not separate the causes cleanly.

Our AskAmerica tables (census.pep_population, declared through 2023; fiscal.county_migration_flows, declared through 2022) do not reach the 2024-2025 window Redfin's newest reversal covers, so that specific recent acceleration is reported here as an external, cited figure rather than one we recomputed — a genuine coverage gap in this connector's vintage, not a discrepancy with it.

3. The academic literature: damage type matters, and effects are often local and short-lived

Ton et al. (2024, Natural Hazards, open access) built a gravity model of IRS county-to-county migration flows (1990-2010 window, per their data-quality cutoff) against natural-hazard economic damage, and found that hurricanes have the largest effect on out-migration, followed by floods and severe storms — but that people who do leave tend to move to NEARBY counties rather than far away, meaning many relocate into similarly exposed areas rather than out of the hazard zone altogether. Their literature review is itself a caution against over-reading any single migration-and-disaster finding: Boustan et al. (2020) found hazards causing 25+ fatalities raise net out-migration by about 1.5 points; Schultz and Elliott (2013) found damage associated with higher population growth (net IN-migration, the opposite sign); Fussell et al. (2017) found hurricane damage barely affects most counties' population growth except a small subset (about 2% of counties) with high prior growth and density, where yearly damage lowers growth but 10-year cumulative damage raises it. In short, the published literature does NOT support a uniform "disaster damage causes depopulation" relationship — effect direction and size depend heavily on hazard type, county growth trajectory, and time horizon, which is exactly what our own county-level check above reproduced.

Methodology and caveats

  • "Hardest-hit" was operationalized here as cumulative NOAA storm-event property damage per 2010 resident, 2010-2019, at county grain — a reasonable but not unique definition; Redfin/First Street instead rank counties by the SHARE of homes facing high flood risk, a forward-looking exposure measure rather than a backward-looking damage total. The two rankings overlap (Gulf Coast Texas, coastal Louisiana, Florida) but are not identical, which is why we report both.
  • Net domestic migration (IRS SOI, tax-return address changes) undercounts non-filers (students, some low-income and elderly households) and, being IRS-based, necessarily excludes people who don't file — noted directly in Ton et al.'s own methodology discussion.
  • Total population change and net domestic migration are different quantities: Jefferson County's near-flat total population despite a real domestic exodus illustrates why the choice of metric changes the answer, and why any headline claiming counties are or are not "depopulating" needs to say which of the two it means.
  • The First Street 'Climate Abandonment Area' press release could not be parsed directly (blocked by an MSCI consent wall); its headline figures (3.2 million, 17 million, 5 million by mid-century) are reported from search-result summaries of secondary coverage (The Hill, CBS News) rather than a parsed primary read of the First Street page itself — flagged here rather than presented as a verified primary-source read.

  1. disasters.disasters_storm_impact_by_county — cumulative storm damage per capita by county, 2010-2019
    Show SQL
    SELECT county_fips, SUM(total_property_damage) AS total_damage FROM disasters.disasters_storm_impact_by_county WHERE "year" BETWEEN '2010' AND '2019' GROUP BY county_fips ORDER BY total_damage DESC
  2. census.decennial_population joined to storm damage — population change 2010-2020 for highest-damage-per-capita counties
    Show SQL
    WITH damage AS (SELECT county_fips, SUM(total_property_damage) AS total_damage FROM disasters.disasters_storm_impact_by_county WHERE "year" BETWEEN '2010' AND '2019' GROUP BY county_fips), pop2010 AS (SELECT county_fips, total_population AS pop2010 FROM census.decennial_population WHERE "year"='2010' AND geography='county'), pop2020 AS (SELECT county_fips, total_population AS pop2020 FROM census.decennial_population WHERE "year"='2020' AND geography='county') SELECT d.county_fips, d.total_damage, p1.pop2010, p2.pop2020, d.total_damage/NULLIF(p1.pop2010,0) AS damage_per_capita, (p2.pop2020-p1.pop2010)*100.0/NULLIF(p1.pop2010,0) AS pct_change FROM damage d JOIN pop2010 p1 ON d.county_fips=p1.county_fips JOIN pop2020 p2 ON d.county_fips=p2.county_fips WHERE p1.pop2010>20000 ORDER BY damage_per_capita DESC
  3. fiscal.county_migration_flows (IRS SOI) — net domestic migration, Jefferson/Montgomery/Galveston Co. TX, 2012-2022
    Show SQL
    WITH outflow AS (SELECT origin_county_fips AS county_fips, "year", SUM(num_individuals) AS out_ind FROM fiscal.county_migration_flows WHERE origin_county_fips IN ('48245','48339','48167') GROUP BY origin_county_fips, "year"), inflow AS (SELECT dest_county_fips AS county_fips, "year", SUM(num_individuals) AS in_ind FROM fiscal.county_migration_flows WHERE dest_county_fips IN ('48245','48339','48167') GROUP BY dest_county_fips, "year") SELECT o.county_fips, o."year", i.in_ind, o.out_ind, i.in_ind-o.out_ind AS net_migration FROM outflow o JOIN inflow i ON o.county_fips=i.county_fips AND o."year"=i."year" ORDER BY o.county_fips, o."year"

    Show SQL
    SELECT "year", population FROM census.pep_population WHERE county_fips='48245' ORDER BY "year"
