Counting rules understates concentration in the wrong agencies — weighting by significance shifts burden toward HHS/DOE/SEC/DOL

Federal Register 2024 final rules (AskAmerica connector, cross-checked against CEI) vs. Spring 2025 Unified Agenda economically-significant actions (GWU Regulatory Studies Center)

Summary

By raw count, five agencies (Transportation, Homeland Security, EPA, Commerce, HHS) issued 61% of all 3,248 federal final rules in 2024. But re-rank agencies by how many economically significant actions (annual effect ≥$100M) they are working on — the Spring 2025 Unified Agenda's proxy for regulatory weight — and the picture both concentrates further AND shifts to different agencies: the top 10 significance-ranked agencies account for 85% of all 148 active economically-significant actions, but that top-10 list swaps out high-volume, low-weight agencies (Commerce, DHS) for low-volume, high-weight ones (DOE, SEC, DOL). HHS alone accounts for 30 of 148 (20%) of significant actions despite issuing well under a tenth of all final rules. Counting rules and weighing them by significance are not just different magnitudes of the same story — they identify substantially different sets of 'biggest' agencies.

Step 1 — What 'weighing by significance' means and where the data comes from

Under Executive Order 12866 (and its 2023 update, EO 14094), OMB's Office of Information and Regulatory Affairs (OIRA) designates a rule '(economically) significant' if it is likely to have an annual effect on the economy of $100 million or more (temporarily $200 million under Biden's EO 14094; restored to $100 million by the Trump administration in 2025). This is the standard operationalization of 'weighing rules by how significant they are' in regulatory-policy research — it is not a page count or a dollar-cost model of every rule, but it is the field's own metric, tracked twice a year in the Unified Agenda of Federal Regulatory and Deregulatory Actions and reported by both the George Washington University Regulatory Studies Center and the Competitive Enterprise Institute's annual 'Ten Thousand Commandments' report.

The Spring 2025 Unified Agenda lists 3,816 total agency actions, of which 243 are economically significant (148 active, 55 long-term, 40 completed). This is a snapshot of the pipeline, not a rate of Federal-Register final rules; it is the most current, publicly available significance-weighted breakdown by agency (GWU Regulatory Studies Center, Sept. 2025).

Step 2 — Unweighted: which agencies write the most rules

Querying the AskAmerica connector's Federal Register table directly for all 2024 final rules (doc_type='RULE') returns 3,248 total rules — this matches CEI's independently compiled Federal Register/National Archives count for 2024 exactly, which is a useful cross-check that the underlying rule inventory is sound. By raw count, the top agencies are Transportation (678), Homeland Security (414), EPA (370), Commerce (344), and HHS (169) — the top 5 alone account for 1,975 of 3,248 rules, or 61%. This lines up with the independently reported Unified-Agenda-pipeline figure (a slightly different base: all pending actions, not final rules only) that the five most active rule-writing departments — Transportation, Interior, Treasury, Commerce, and EPA — account for 46% of the 3,816-action pipeline (GWU/CEI). Either way the unweighted picture says the same thing: rule-writing volume is dominated by a handful of large regulatory departments, heavily weighted toward agencies that issue large numbers of routine actions (e.g., FAA airworthiness directives at DOT, Federal Register notices tied to routine EPA/DHS/Commerce administrative actions).

Step 3 — Weighted by significance: a different top tier

Re-ranking by active economically-significant actions in the Spring 2025 Unified Agenda produces a materially different leaderboard: HHS (30), DOE (17), SEC (15), DOL (15), EPA (12), DOT (10), USDA (8), DHS (6), VA, and CFTC round out the top 10, which together account for 126 of 148 active significant actions — over 85%. That is a HIGHER concentration than the unweighted 46-61% figures, meaning regulatory weight is even more concentrated in a small set of agencies than rule counts alone suggest.

But the agencies carrying that concentrated weight are not the same ones that dominate by volume. Commerce — a top-5 agency by raw rule count (344 rules, 11% of the 2024 total) — does not appear in the top 10 by significance at all. DHS drops from #2 by volume (414 rules) to a modest #8 by significance (6 actions). Conversely, DOE issued only 70 total final rules in 2024 (2% of the national total) yet ranks #2 by significance with 17 active high-impact actions — 24% of its own rule output is economically significant. SEC's ratio is starker still: 15 of roughly 26 SEC final rules in 2024 (57%) are economically significant, versus DOT's 10 of 678 (1.5%) and EPA's 12 of 370 (3.2%). (These agency-own-output ratios mix two different pipelines — Unified Agenda actions and Federal Register final rules — so they should be read as an indicator of regulatory intensity per agency, not a precise rate with a shared denominator.)

HHS is the clearest illustration of the reframing: it issues a modest share of all federal rules (169 of 3,248, about 5%) but is doing more high-stakes rulemaking right now than any other agency — 30 of 148 active significant actions, or 20% of the significance-weighted total, more than double its share of raw rule volume.

Step 4 — What the reweighting changes about 'biggest'

The honest answer to 'how much of the burden do the biggest agencies account for' depends entirely on which 'biggest' you mean, and both answers cluster in a similar range (roughly 46-61% for the unweighted top 5, 85% for the significance-weighted top 10) — so the headline concentration number does not move as much as the underlying agency roster does. The practical finding is not 'concentration is higher or lower once you weight it' so much as 'the identity of the dominant agencies changes': Commerce and DHS look outsized only because they generate high volumes of routine, low-stakes rules, while DOE, SEC, DOL, and HHS punch well above their rule-count weight in actual economic significance. A policy conversation about 'cutting red tape at the biggest rule-writers' aimed at raw counts would target a substantially different set of agencies than one aimed at the rules that actually carry $100M+ annual economic effects.

Data notes and limitations

  • Economic significance ($100M+ threshold) is the standard, OIRA-defined proxy for 'how significant a rule is' used across this literature; it is not a complete cost model and does not capture every dimension of regulatory burden (compliance time, distributional effects, non-monetized harms).
  • The Unified Agenda significance figures (148 active / 243 total) come from the Spring 2025 edition — a single pipeline snapshot, not a full-year flow of finalized rules — while the 3,248-rule figure is a full calendar-year 2024 count of finalized Federal Register rules. They are not perfectly comparable denominators, which is why the report presents both counts (raw share, significance share) rather than a single blended ratio.
  • The AskAmerica fedregister.fr_documents count (3,248 final rules, 2024) was cross-checked against the independently published CEI/National Archives figure of 3,248 and matched exactly, which validates the connector data for the raw-count side of this analysis.
  • The 'significant actions as % of own rule output' panel divides Unified Agenda figures by Federal Register final-rule counts as an illustrative intensity ratio only, since the two series are not from the same pipeline.
