Weighted by significance, the biggest rule-writers account for far less regulatory burden than raw rule counts suggest

Federal Register final rules (RULE), OIRA EO 12866 significance flag, classified documents only

Summary

Counted head-for-head, two agencies — the Department of Transportation (2,713 final rules) and the Department of Homeland Security (1,461) — write 46% of all federal final rules, and the ten biggest rule-writers together account for 79.5% of rule volume. But most of that volume is routine: DOT and DHS each have an economic-significance rate of only about 4.7% (an airworthiness directive or a port-security notice, not a rule OMB flags as economically consequential). Once every rule is weighted by whether OIRA actually classified it as economically significant under EO 12866, the same top-10-by-volume group falls to 58.0% of significant rules, and the ranking reorders: the Department of Health and Human Services (312 significant rules from just 356 total — an 87.6% significance rate) and the Department of Defense (162 significant rules, a 54.7% rate) overtake DOT and DHS. Concentration itself falls with the reweighting: the Gini coefficient across agencies drops from 0.74 (extreme) on raw rule counts to 0.58 (still high, but well below extreme) on significance-weighted counts. The honest answer: the biggest rule-writers by volume are NOT the biggest contributors to regulatory burden once burden is proxied by economic significance — burden is somewhat less concentrated than headline rule-count rankings suggest, and it is concentrated in a partly different set of agencies (HHS, Defense, EPA, Labor) than the volume leaders (DOT, DHS).

Step 1 — What 'weighing by significance' means and why it is the right proxy here

The Federal Register API carries a boolean significant flag per document (fedregister.fr_significance), sourced directly from OIRA's own EO 12866 review determination — the same test used to decide whether a rule requires White House regulatory review (roughly, an annual economic effect of $100M+, later raised to $200M by EO 14094 in April 2023). This is the standard proxy the regulatory-oversight literature itself uses for 'how much a rule matters': GWU's Regulatory Studies Center tracks 'economically significant final rules by agency' as its own headline metric, and Ballotpedia/CRS track annual OIRA-significant review counts as the standard measure of regulatory activity (see sources). A rule count with no significance weight, by contrast, treats a one-paragraph technical correction identically to a rule with a $10 billion annual compliance cost — which is exactly the distortion the question asks to correct for.

Step 2 — Data coverage and exclusions, disclosed

fedregister.fr_significance is NOT populated for most documents: 90.6% of all rows in the table are NULL on the significance flag, and the years actually carrying real classifications are 2013-2017 and a partial 2026 — 2018 through 2025 hold essentially no classified rows (an interior gap, not a declared boundary). Two explicit exclusions were applied in the underlying queries, and here is exactly what each one removed:

  • Predicate s.significant IS NOT NULL (applied via NOT (s.significant IS NULL)): drops every RULE document the Federal Register API has not yet classified. Of 15,509 final rules joinable to fr_significance's declared window, 8,535 distinct rules (8,990 agency-mentions) carry a real true/false flag; the remaining ~6,974 unclassified rules are excluded rather than assumed 'not significant', which would have silently understated every agency's significance rate by an unknown amount. This is the single largest exclusion in the analysis and is why the window is 2013-2017 + partial 2026 rather than a continuous current decade — stated again in the Caveats below.
  • Predicate agency <> 'And 53' (Gini-coefficient input only): the raw agency_names field, comma-split to handle joint rulemakings, produced one single-row parsing artifact literally reading "And 53" — a fragment of a CFR-part-number list (e.g. "...48 CFR Parts 1, 2, 3, ..., and 53") that got split into a fake agency alongside NASA. It carried 1 total rule and 0 significant rules. Removing it changes the reported 45-agency Gini calculation by less than 0.001 (recomputed with it included: 0.7359 raw-count Gini vs. 0.7362 without — same "extreme" band, same ranking) and does not affect any top-10 figure, since it never appears in a top-10 list. It is excluded because it is not a real agency, not because it changes any conclusion.

Step 4 — Concentration falls once weighted

Gini coefficients across 45 agencies with 5+ classified rules: 0.736 ("extreme") on raw rule counts vs. 0.584 ("high") on significant-rule counts. The same top-10-by-volume agencies hold 79.5% of all classified rule volume but only 58.0% of significant rules. This is a direct, computed answer to the question: burden measured this way is less concentrated in the biggest rule-writers than a raw rulemaking count implies, because the two biggest volume producers (DOT, DHS) write large numbers of routine, low-stakes rules (airworthiness directives, TSA security amendments, minor technical corrections) that rarely clear the OIRA significance bar.

Step 5 — External corroboration

This pattern matches published tracking, not just this run's own numbers. GWU's Regulatory Studies Center maintains 'Economically Significant Rules by Agency' as a standing series, tracking exactly the agencies that surface here as high-significance producers — HHS, EPA, Labor (DOL), Agriculture (USDA), Interior (DOI), DOT, DHS, HUD, SBA, Education — confirming these are the agencies the oversight community itself watches for significant-rule output, not an artifact of this connector's join. Separately, OIRA's own annual review totals (Ballotpedia's tracking of reginfo.gov) show 575 significant rules completed review in 2023 and roughly 480-500/year in 2021-2022 — in the same order of magnitude as this analysis's ~1,814-significant-rule count spread across a 6-year classified window (2013-2017, 2026), a sanity check that the scale is not wildly out of line with the independently reported figures (exact-year comparison is not possible because the two multi-year windows do not overlap).

Caveats

  • The significance classification covers only 2013-2017 and part of 2026 in this warehouse; 2018-2025 (including the entire Trump-2/Biden years most current policy debate concerns) has essentially no classified rows and could not be included. This is a real coverage gap in fedregister.fr_significance, disclosed above rather than papered over — any claim about the CURRENT (2020s) distribution of regulatory burden by agency cannot be made from this table as loaded.
  • Counting is by agency-mention on joint rules, not by agency-exclusive credit — a rule co-issued by Treasury and FDIC counts once for each, inflating both agencies' totals modestly.
  • Economic significance ($ threshold review) is one reasonable proxy for 'burden' but not the only one; it does not capture compliance-hour burden (which PRA/OMB tracks separately) or a rule's political salience.
  • A handful of agency-name variants (typos, redundant qualifiers, or CFR-part-number parsing artifacts from comma-splitting agency_names, including the single 'And 53' row detailed in Step 2) appear as separate 1-row entries in the raw breakdown; these are immaterial to every ranking and share figure reported above and were excluded from the Gini calculation's minimum-5-rule threshold or, in And 53's case, by name.
