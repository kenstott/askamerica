DOT files half of all final rules, but they are overwhelmingly routine; HHS and Treasury rules are far likelier to be economically significant

Federal Register final rules (RULE documents), 2019-2026 (2026 partial through late August); significant-rule flag from federalregister.gov API

Summary

Across the 22,839 final rules this corpus holds for 2019 through mid-2026, the Department of Transportation issued more than any other agency (5,502, 24% of the total) — but that count is dominated by routine FAA airworthiness directives (2,726 of DOT's 5,502 rules, 49.5%, matched on title text). Only 2.5% of DOT's rules were flagged "significant" under EO 12866, versus 36.3% of HHS's and 20.3% of Treasury's. Raw rule counts by agency are therefore a poor and misleading proxy for regulatory weight; ranking agencies by significant-rule count gives a materially different picture than ranking by raw volume. Annual rule volume differs by administration (one-way ANOVA on monthly counts, F=9.59, p<0.001, n=94 months: 25 under the final two years of Trump 1, 49 under Biden, 20 under the first 19 months of Trump 2), with the second Trump administration's monthly average (191.6 rules/month) running below both Trump 1 (260.8) and Biden (254.9) — consistent with independent reporting that 2025-2026 rule counts are historic lows. A within-agency, agency-and-year fixed-effects test of four large agencies found no statistically detectable relationship between an agency's annual obligated spending and its rule count (coefficient 0.11, p=0.35, n=28) — but that test has very little power (4 agencies × 7 years) and should be read as inconclusive, not as evidence of no relationship. A within-year seasonal/deadline-cycle test (ANOVA on month-of-year, n=84 agency-months) also found no statistically significant pattern (F=1.55, p=0.13) despite a visually higher rate in mid-year and December.

Step 2 &mdash; How the agency distribution has shifted

DOT has been the top-ranked agency in every year 2019-2026, but its share and absolute count moved with events: DOT rules rose from 550 (2019) to a peak of 904 (2022, the year of major FAA/pipeline-safety rule activity) before settling back to 678-702 in 2024-2025. EPA's count fell fairly steadily from 475 (2019) to 281 (2025), a 41% decline, while DHS held roughly flat (437 in 2019, 414 in 2023-2024, 261 in 2025). Commerce grew modestly (314 in 2019 to a peak of 344 in 2023-2024). The clearest compositional shift is EPA's declining share paired with DOT's growing dominance: EPA and DOT were roughly comparable in 2019 (475 vs 550) but by 2025 DOT outnumbered EPA by more than 2.5:1 (702 vs 281).

Ranked by raw count, DOT leads by a wide margin; ranked by significant rules, HHS, Treasury and EPA are closer together and DOT drops toward the bottom of this group. A large, directly checkable part of DOT's routine volume is FAA airworthiness directives — 2,726 of DOT's 5,502 rules (49.5%) match "airworthiness" in the title, and these are individually narrow, aircraft/engine-model-specific safety mandates, not broad economic policy. This does not fully account for DOT's low significance rate (airworthiness directives are a documented example of the routine category, not the whole explanation), and this corpus cannot classify the remaining ~50% of DOT rules or any other agency's routine/significant split beyond the EO 12866 flag itself — a finer categorization (e.g. by CFR title/part or dollar impact) was not attempted here and would need OIRA's underlying review records.

Step 4 &mdash; Testing (not asserting) a relationship to administration and to budget

Administration. Rather than compare 8 noisy annual totals, monthly final-rule counts (n=94 months, 2019-01 through 2026-08) were labeled by administration using inauguration dates (Trump 1: through 2021-01-19; Biden: 2021-01-20 through 2025-01-19; Trump 2: from 2025-01-20) and tested with one-way ANOVA. Result: F=9.59, p=0.00017, group means 260.8/month (Trump 1, n=25), 254.9/month (Biden, n=49), 191.6/month (Trump 2, n=20). The difference is statistically significant at conventional thresholds. Caveat on power: n=94 is a count of months, but months within one administration are not independent draws — they share the same appointees, priorities and OMB/OIRA leadership, so the true number of independent 'treatments' here is 3 administrations (2 transitions), not 94. The test correctly detects that Trump 2's period differs from the other two; it should not be read as 94 independent confirmations of that difference, and it says nothing about whether the drop is a deliberate deregulatory choice, an early-term staffing/ramp-up lag (also visible after the 2021 transition, though less pronounced), or a partial-year artifact (Trump 2's window includes an apparent July 2026 data gap, discussed above).

Budget. Annual final-rule counts for DOT, EPA, DHS and HHS were joined to fiscal.usaspending_by_agency (obligated federal spending) 2019-2025 (n=28 agency-years) and tested with a two-way (agency + year) fixed-effects panel regression, which nets out each agency's typical size and any year common to all four. Result: coefficient on obligated spending = 0.11 rules per additional billion dollars obligated, std. error 0.12, p=0.35 — not statistically significant. This is not strong evidence that budget and rule volume are unrelated: with only 4 entities and 7 years (n=28, 17 residual degrees of freedom) the test has limited power to detect anything but a large effect, and it examines only 4 of dozens of rule-issuing agencies. It should be read as 'no relationship was detected with the power available,' not as 'no relationship exists.'

Statutory/seasonal deadline cycles. As an observable proxy for deadline-driven cycles (fiscal year-end, holiday-season pushes, etc.), month-of-year final-rule counts 2019-2025 (n=84 agency-months, 7 years × 12 months) were tested with one-way ANOVA. Visually, rule counts are higher in mid-year (June-September, averaging 268-281/month) and December (280/month) than in January and November (217-221/month) — a pattern consistent with a fiscal-year-end or year-end push. But the test does not clear significance: F=1.55, p=0.13. With only 7 years per month, this test lacks the power to confirm a real seasonal effect from a plausible-looking one; the honest finding is 'suggestive but not established' rather than a confirmed deadline-cycle effect.
