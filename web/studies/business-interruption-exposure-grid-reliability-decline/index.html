Grid strain from data centers: outage metrics haven't clearly worsened yet — but reliability risk and business-interruption exposure to neighbors are already being priced in

EIA Electric Power Annual, PJM/Utility Dive, AM Best/Risk & Insurance, and insurance trade press, fetched Sept 2026

Summary

Outage frequency and duration have been drifting worse nationally for over a decade, but that trend predates the AI data-center boom and is driven mainly by aging infrastructure and worse storms — not, on current evidence, by data centers. What IS new and does trace directly to data centers is a run of acute grid-disturbance events in the most data-center-dense regions (mass automatic disconnections of gigawatts of data-center load during transmission faults), plus explicit grid-operator warnings that a real capacity shortfall and rolling-blackout risk could arrive within a few years in PJM. On the insurance side, the industry's own analysis (AM Best, via Risk & Insurance) explicitly names the mechanism a reader would expect: data centers' grid and water demand can constrain resources for surrounding communities, "potentially giving rise to third-party property damage lawsuits from residents or other businesses affected by those constraints." That confirms the concern is real and recognized by insurers — but it is described as an emerging liability exposure, not yet a measured rise in business-interruption claims or premiums for unrelated nearby businesses.

1. National outage trend: real, but not obviously data-center-driven

EIA's Electric Power Annual data (Form EIA-861) shows a genuine multi-year decline in U.S. distribution reliability, excluding major storm events: SAIDI (average outage minutes per customer per year) rose from roughly 102.8–106.1 minutes in 2013 to 125.7 minutes in 2022, and 123.6 minutes in 2024. SAIFI (average interruptions per customer) rose about 4.8% from 2013–2021. Trade coverage of this trend (POWER magazine) attributes it to aging distribution infrastructure and worsening weather and does not cite data centers or large-load growth as a driver — this decline was underway well before the current AI/data-center buildout accelerated after 2022.

2. Where it gets specific to data centers: disturbance events and capacity warnings, not (yet) routine outage stats

In the regions where data-center growth is most concentrated — PJM's footprint broadly, and specifically Northern Virginia's "Data Center Alley" in Loudoun County — the documented risk shows up differently than a rising SAIDI number:

  • Interconnection delays, not yet outages: in 2022 Dominion Energy told data-center developers that new connections in Eastern Loudoun County would be delayed for years because transmission infrastructure hadn't kept pace; the utility resumed only partial, reduced allocations, with constraints expected to persist until new 500kV transmission lines complete around 2026 (Data Center Frontier, fetched directly).
  • Mass-disconnection disturbance events: a 2022 incident and a subsequent 2026 event each saw several gigawatts of Northern Virginia data-center load automatically trip offline to backup generation during a transmission fault, sending a voltage disturbance across the entire PJM footprint from Virginia to Chicago and taking roughly ten minutes to stabilize — far longer than a typical disturbance. PJM has proposed new reliability rules requiring large loads to ride through disturbances instead of tripping.
  • Explicit reliability-shortfall warnings: Utility Dive (fetched directly, a Dec. 2025 NRDC opinion piece drawing on PJM's own published scenarios) reports PJM will have "just enough power to keep the grid reliable" starting summer 2026, and that by June 2027 the region may fall below reliability standards — meaning a greater risk of rolling blackouts during winter storms and summer heat waves — if data-center interconnections keep outpacing new supply. PJM's own published scenarios reportedly show "deep shortages" by 2030. The same piece states data-center-driven capacity-price increases added $9.4 billion to PJM-region electricity bills this past summer, with another $1.4 billion locked in for next summer and an estimated $100 billion by 2033.

What could not be verified: a utility-specific (Dominion Virginia) before/after SAIDI/SAIFI series filed with the Virginia State Corporation Commission was not fetched as a primary document in this session, so this answer cannot say whether routine, everyday outage minutes in the data-center-heavy service territory have themselves measurably risen relative to other Virginia utilities or the national trend. The documented risk today is concentrated in acute large-scale disturbance events, interconnection/connection delays, and forward-looking capacity-adequacy warnings — not (yet) a demonstrated rise in everyday SAIDI/SAIFI specific to that territory.

3. Business-interruption exposure for other local businesses: named as a real risk, not yet a measured trend

AM Best's own data-center risk analysis, as reported in Risk & Insurance (fetched directly), identifies business interruption as "potentially the most consequential exposure" in the data-center insurance market because a disruption at one facility "can instantly cascade across interconnected networks, affecting multiple clients and services simultaneously." Critically, the same report goes further and names exactly the mechanism this question asks about: data centers' "heavy water consumption for cooling and their demand on local electrical grids can constrain resource availability for surrounding communities, potentially giving rise to third-party property damage lawsuits from residents or other businesses affected by those constraints." That is the clearest, most direct documented link found between data-center grid strain and exposure for unrelated local businesses — but it is framed by AM Best as a liability/lawsuit exposure that could arise, not a business-interruption claims trend that has already been measured or priced with a number.

Separately, Aon's CEO of risk capital (Insurance Business, fetched directly) confirms business interruption, contractor credit, and power infrastructure are the industry's top emerging concerns for the data centers themselves over the next 12 months — overall data-center insurance market capacity has grown roughly 50% in the past year — and that carriers are also developing companion coverage for the dedicated power plants some developers are now building because grid availability is limited. Other coverage (Insurance Business, Veridion) describes carriers tightening pricing and terms "across an entire region, not just at individual sites" where data centers cluster, due to accumulation/correlated-loss risk in their portfolios — a mechanism by which a data center's grid exposure could indirectly raise costs for other insureds nearby, though again not quantified for unrelated local businesses specifically.

Net: the industry itself has identified and named the pathway from data-center grid/water strain to third-party exposure for neighboring residents and businesses. That confirms the concern is legitimate and being actively underwritten around, but no source found puts a number on business-interruption claims frequency, severity, or premium increases specifically for non-data-center local businesses as a result of data-center-driven grid strain — that remains an anticipated risk, not yet a documented trend.

Data availability note

The AskAmerica connector's SQL warehouse was checked directly (search_catalog) and confirmed to carry no table for grid outage indices (SAIDI/SAIFI) or business-interruption insurance claims — the energy schema has generation, capacity, and utility financial data but not reliability/outage statistics. That should be read as a genuine coverage gap in the connector rather than an absence of the underlying government data (EIA does collect SAIDI/SAIFI via Form EIA-861, and Virginia's SCC separately tracks it for Dominion Energy).
