Yes — Faster FEMA Disaster Declarations Are Associated With Stronger County Employment Recovery, Even After Controlling for Aid Size and County Size

FEMA disaster declarations joined to BLS QCEW county employment, 2010-2022 declarations, n=12,609 county-disaster observations

Summary

Yes, there is a real, statistically robust association: counties whose FEMA disaster declaration came faster after the disaster began saw stronger employment growth over the following three years. Counties declared within 14 days of the incident's start averaged +2.95% total employment growth over the next 3 years, versus +0.67% for counties that waited 46-120 days. In a regression that controls for the dollar amount of federal Public Assistance obligated per worker (a proxy for disaster severity) and county size, each one-tier increase in declaration delay is associated with roughly 1.16 percentage points lower 3-year employment growth (p < 0.001, n=12,609 county-disaster observations, 2010-2022). This holds up when any single state is dropped from the analysis. The relationship is real but modest in explanatory power (R²≈0.01) -- disaster speed is one of many things driving a county's post-disaster trajectory, not the dominant one.

Why declaration speed, not literal disbursement speed

The question asks about how fast aid actually arrives. This corpus's FEMA data (disasters.public_assistance_projects, 404,509 project-worksheet rows) carries an approved project amount and cumulative obligated totals, but no obligation-date or disbursement-date field -- only declaration_date, which is constant across every project tied to a disaster. There is therefore no way, from this data alone, to measure how many days after a disaster the first dollar was actually obligated to a given project. The earliest point in the pipeline that IS reliably timestamped and gates all Public Assistance funding is the presidential disaster declaration itself (declaration_date vs. incident_begin_date in disasters.disaster_declarations) -- no PA money can be obligated before a Major Disaster Declaration with the PA program activated. This is used here as the proxy for aid speed, and is disclosed as such rather than presented as literal dollar-arrival timing.

What the literature says

Existing research supports the plausibility of an aid-speed → recovery-speed link, and complicates it in ways worth naming. The Federal Reserve Bank of San Francisco's working paper on the local economic impact of natural disasters finds that disaster shocks can raise local employment and income in the short-to-medium run, driven substantially by rebuilding activity and federal aid inflows -- consistent with the positive average growth seen across every speed bucket in this analysis. Tatyana Deryugina's body of work (see her IDEAS/RePEc page), including "The Fiscal Cost of Hurricanes: Disaster Aid versus Social Insurance" (AEJ: Economic Policy, 2017) and the Katrina tax-return study with Kawano and Levitt, documents that federal disaster aid materially affects household and local economic trajectories for years after a disaster -- establishing that aid magnitude matters, which is exactly why this analysis controls for aid dollars per worker rather than letting speed proxy for aid size. A more directly on-point paper, Arora, Tran & Khaitan's "Do Politics Shape Economic Recovery through Federal Disaster Assistance?" (SSRN, could not be fetched directly -- paywalled/403 on this session, cited from its title and abstract summary in search results only), argues that the speed and generosity of federal disaster assistance is itself shaped by political alignment between a disaster-hit jurisdiction and the federal government, which in turn shapes recovery -- meaning declaration speed is not randomly assigned, an important caveat to the causal reading of the result below.

Method and what was excluded

1. Built one row per county x disaster-year for every DR-type FEMA declaration with Public Assistance activated, 2010-2022 (deduplicating multiple program rows per disaster to one row per county-year using the fastest declaration that year).

2. Bucketed declaration speed into 4 tiers by days from incident_begin_date to declaration_date: 0-14, 15-45, 46-120, 120+.

3. Measured 3-year county employment growth: (employment in declaration_year+2 minus employment in declaration_year-1) / employment in declaration_year-1, from BLS QCEW (econ.county_wages, all-industry, all-ownership).

4. Ran a regression of growth on the speed-tier code, controlling for log(1 + federal PA dollars obligated per pre-disaster worker) and log(pre-disaster employment).

5. Ran a leave-one-state-out sensitivity analysis on the speed-tier coefficient.

Exact exclusion accounting, starting from 12,782 eligible county x disaster-year rows. county_fips IS NOT NULL dropped 0 rows. A direct count confirmed every 2010-2022 DR/PA disaster-declaration row already has a non-null county_fips, so nothing was excluded by this predicate. incident_begin_date IS NOT NULL dropped 0 rows and declaration_date IS NOT NULL dropped 0 rows -- every eligible row already carried both dates, so nothing was excluded by either predicate. pre.emp IS NOT NULL dropped 165 rows and the equivalent column check emp_pre IS NOT NULL also dropped 165 rows (the same underlying BLS QCEW column, checked in two separate queries this session): BLS QCEW suppresses employment for county-industry cells dominated by one or two employers, so those 165 county-years were excluded for lacking a disclosed pre-disaster employment figure. post.emp IS NOT NULL dropped 169 rows and the equivalent column check emp_post IS NOT NULL also dropped 169 rows (again the same underlying BLS QCEW column, checked twice): those 169 county-years were excluded for the identical BLS-suppression reason, two years later. The pre.emp and post.emp null sets overlap on 161 rows, so together they excluded a net 173 of the 12,782 eligible rows, leaving 12,609 retained, which is 98.6% of the eligible rows. pre.emp > 50 dropped 0 further rows -- a direct count confirmed every non-null pre.emp value in this population already exceeded 50 workers, so nothing was excluded by that floor. Because BLS's suppression targets a small, specific set of tiny or single-employer counties rather than systematically fast- or slow-declared ones, and the exclusion rate is under 1.4%, it is unlikely to be manufacturing the fast-vs-slow gap reported below; recomputing the group means on the 173 excluded rows is not possible since their outcome variable, the employment figure itself, is exactly what BLS suppressed, but the small share excluded bounds how much they could move the headline number.

Regression result: coefficient on speed-tier = -0.0116 (SE 0.00110, robust HC1 std. errors), p < 1.99e-15, n=12,609. Coefficient on log(federal aid $/worker) = -0.0016 (p<0.001) -- more aid per worker is associated with slightly slower measured growth once severity correlates with aid size, an expected sign given aid targets the worst-hit counties. Coefficient on log(pre-disaster employment) = +0.0018 (p=0.04) -- larger counties recover marginally faster.

Robustness and limits

Leave-one-state-out testing across all 53 jurisdictions found no sign flip and no loss of significance at any omission. Texas (FIPS 48) is the most influential single state (|influence|=3.17 standard-error units) -- dropping it moves the coefficient from -0.0116 to -0.0083, still highly significant, still the same direction. No other state moves the estimate by more than 0.8 standard errors.

Caveats that matter for interpretation: (1) This is an association from observational data, not a randomized or quasi-experimental design -- declaration speed is plausibly endogenous to disaster severity, political salience, and state capacity, all of which independently affect recovery, and the Arora/Tran/Khaitan finding above suggests declaration speed itself may be politically determined. (2) The employment measure (QCEW) captures jobs, not output, wages, or business survival, and a 3-year post-declaration window may miss both very short-run disruption and very long-run divergence. (3) R²≈0.01 means this regression explains a small share of the variance in county employment growth -- declaration speed is one modest factor among many, not the dominant driver. (4) Declaration-speed is a proxy for aid speed broadly, not the same as "how many days until the check cleared," which this corpus cannot measure directly (no obligation-date field on the project-level FEMA data).

WITH d AS (

    CAST("year" AS INT) AS decl_year,

         WHEN declaration_date <= incident_begin_date + INTERVAL '45' DAY THEN 1
         WHEN declaration_date <= incident_begin_date + INTERVAL '120' DAY THEN 2
         ELSE 3 END AS lag_code
  FROM disasters.disaster_declarations
  WHERE declaration_type = 'DR' AND pa_program_declared = TRUE

dedup AS (

  FROM d GROUP BY county_fips, decl_year

emp AS (
  SELECT county_fips, CAST(year AS INT) AS yr, annual_avg_emplvl AS emp
  FROM econ.county_wages WHERE industry_code='10' AND own_code='0'
),
joined AS (
  SELECT dd.county_fips, dd.decl_year, dd.lag_code,
    pre.emp AS emp_pre, post.emp AS emp_post
  FROM dedup dd
  JOIN emp pre ON pre.county_fips = dd.county_fips AND pre.yr = dd.decl_year - 1

  WHERE pre.emp IS NOT NULL AND post.emp IS NOT NULL AND pre.emp > 50
)
SELECT lag_code, COUNT(*) AS n, AVG((emp_post-emp_pre)*1.0/emp_pre) AS avg_growth,
  stddev_samp((emp_post-emp_pre)*1.0/emp_pre) AS sd_growth,
  median((emp_post-emp_pre)*1.0/emp_pre) AS median_growth
FROM joined
GROUP BY lag_code
ORDER BY lag_code
