In FY2023, only 3 states (NJ, MA, WA) were net donors to the federal government — every other state got back more than it paid, led by DC (+$81,084/resident) and New Mexico (+$18,039/resident)

Net federal spending received minus federal taxes paid, per resident, federal fiscal year 2023 — NY State Comptroller / Rockefeller Institute / OMB methodology

Summary

In federal fiscal year 2023 (the most recent year with a full, methodologically consistent state-level estimate), every state except three received back more in federal spending than its residents and businesses paid in federal taxes. Nationally, every $1 paid to the federal government came back as $1.32 in federal spending — the federal government runs a deficit, so the whole "pie" being distributed is larger than what was collected. The three net donor states were New Jersey (-$1,900/resident), Massachusetts (-$518/resident), and Washington (-$104/resident, essentially breaking even). At the other extreme, New Mexico received $3.21 back per $1 paid (+$18,039/resident) and Virginia received $2.18 per $1 (+$16,031/resident) — the largest gaps among actual states. Washington, DC (a federal district, not a state) is an extreme outlier at +$81,084/resident, since federal salaries dominate its local economy.

The full picture: why 'more' varies so much by measure

"How much more" can be reported three different ways, and they rank states differently:

  • Total dollar gap (largest states dominate): for FY2024, USAFacts (built from IRS SOI + USASpending.gov data) reports California's residents paid $275.6B more than they received, followed by New York (-$76.5B) and Texas (-$68.1B) as the biggest donors in absolute dollars; Virginia (+$89.0B), Alabama (+$44.7B), and South Carolina (+$38.9B) had the largest absolute beneficiary gaps.
  • Per-resident dollar gap (controls for population, the measure used in the dashboard above): this is the standard way state finance researchers (Rockefeller Institute of Government, the NY State Comptroller, OMB) present the figure, since it isolates how favorably or unfavorably an average resident's tax dollar is treated, rather than just reflecting a state's size.
  • Return ratio ($ received per $1 paid): useful for comparing a state to itself against the national baseline (currently $1.32 per $1) rather than to other states in raw dollars.

All three measures agree on the qualitative story for FY2023-2024: the number of true "donor" states has shrunk to a small handful (3 by the Comptroller's FY2023 count) because federal deficit spending has pushed total outlays well above total collections nationally — so most states now get back more than they send in, even ones (like New Jersey, Massachusetts, Washington, Minnesota, California, New York) whose residents are relatively high-income and therefore pay disproportionately more in taxes per person.

What this warehouse (askengine) could and could not compute directly

search_catalog surfaced fiscal.federal_spending_vs_income_tax_by_state_year, which joins USAspending obligations to IRS SOI individual income tax by state — but its own table description states plainly that this is not a valid balance-of-payments measure, because individual income tax is only part of federal revenue (it excludes payroll/FICA, corporate, and excise taxes, none of which exist as a state-level table anywhere in this warehouse yet). I ran it anyway as a check: for FY2023 (the one recent year with non-null tax data in that view), every single state showed a positive net position under this narrower measure — including New Jersey, Massachusetts, and Washington, the three states that the Comptroller's real balance-of-payments accounting shows as actual net donors. That is exactly the distortion the table's own documentation warned about, so the warehouse figure was discarded in favor of the published, complete accounting above rather than presented as a state-by-state estimate.

What This Report Does Not Answer

  • Attempt to answer from the AskAmerica/askengine warehouse directly: The warehouse has no table combining total federal receipts (income+payroll+corporate+excise) by state — only USAspending obligations vs. individual income tax alone, which its own documentation says overstates every state's net position. I ran that query as a check (it confirmed the distortion) but used published external data as the primary, credible answer instead of presenting the warehouse's structurally biased figure as a state ranking.
