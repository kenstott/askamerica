Every state but three got back more from Washington than it paid in — federal fiscal year 2023

Rockefeller Institute of Government Balance of Payments analysis, FFY2023 preliminary data, per capita

Summary

In federal fiscal year 2023 (the latest year with a complete, professionally-vetted balance-of-payments estimate), 47 of the 50 states received more in federal spending than their residents and businesses paid in federal taxes. The gap ranged from +$16,650 per person in Virginia (federal spending there runs about $2.22 for every $1 in federal taxes paid, driven heavily by proximity to Washington DC, federal wages, and defense/procurement contracts) down to a net loss of $2,011 per person in New Jersey, the only state whose residents paid meaningfully more in federal taxes than the state got back (New Jersey received just $0.88 back for every $1 paid). The national average balance was +$4,099 per person. Only three states — New Jersey, Massachusetts, and Washington — ran a negative balance in 2023; a fourth, New Hampshire, was essentially break-even (+$23).

This pattern is stable in direction but not in exact ranking from year to year: it is driven mainly by two things — how much federal money flows to a state through defense/procurement, federal wages, and need-based programs (Medicaid, veterans' benefits, Social Security relative to population) versus how much income and payroll tax base the state has (high-income, high-tax states like NJ, MA, CT, NY, CA tend to send more to Washington per person than they get back).

Why the ranking looks the way it does

Two forces mostly explain the pattern:

  • Spending side: states near Washington DC (Virginia, Maryland) get outsized federal wages and procurement spending; several lower-income, rural, or older-population states (New Mexico, West Virginia, Mississippi, Kentucky, Alabama, Maine) draw disproportionately more per capita from need-based transfer programs (Medicaid, Social Security, veterans benefits) and defense/energy installations relative to their small tax base.
  • Revenue side: high-income, high-cost states with steeply progressive federal income tax bases — New Jersey, Massachusetts, Connecticut, New York, California, Washington, New Hampshire — pay disproportionately more per capita in federal taxes, so even substantial federal spending in those states isn't enough to offset it.

The ranking moves somewhat year to year (see the 2022 and 2021 rank columns in the source table) — Virginia, New Mexico, Alaska, and Maryland have held the top four spots in all three years shown, while the bottom of the list (NJ, MA, WA, NH) has also been consistently negative or near-zero across the three years, indicating this is a structural pattern rather than a one-year fluke. However, COVID-19 emergency spending (2020-2022) temporarily made every state's balance positive, including chronic donor states, so the 2023 comparison — which strips most of that out — is closer to the state's underlying, non-emergency relationship with the federal government than 2020-2022 figures would be.

Data and method notes

The authoritative figures above come from the Rockefeller Institute of Government's annual Balance of Payments report, which nets ALL allocable federal tax receipts (individual income tax, payroll/Social Security tax, corporate tax, excise taxes) collected from a state against ALL allocable federal expenditures in that state (direct payments to individuals, grants, contracts, and federal wages), using Budget of the U.S. Government and federal agency source data. This is the standard, most rigorous public methodology for this question, and the one used every year by outlets like Axios, USAFacts, and Visual Capitalist when they publish 'donor state / recipient state' maps.

I separately queried this connector's own fiscal.federal_money_by_state_year view, which pairs USAspending total federal obligations against IRS SOI individual income tax receipts only (2023 is the latest year with both fields populated). That comparison is NOT a valid substitute for the Rockefeller methodology: individual income tax is only a fraction of what a state's residents actually send to Washington (it excludes payroll/FICA tax, corporate tax, and excise tax), so netting full federal spending against only individual income tax mechanically inflates every state's apparent 'surplus' — in the connector's own numbers essentially every state (49 of 51 with data) showed a large positive net, which contradicts the professionally-computed result that only 47 of 50 states are net recipients and by far smaller margins. I am reporting the Rockefeller Institute figures as the answer and flagging this as a genuine gap in this connector's data: it does not carry total federal tax receipts by state (only the IRS SOI individual-income-tax slice), so a true from-scratch balance-of-payments calculation cannot currently be reproduced against this corpus.
