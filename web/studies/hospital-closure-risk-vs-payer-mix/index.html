Payer mix is the strongest single predictor of hospital closure risk — but the mechanism runs through commercial rates, not just Medicaid/uninsured share

CMS Hospital Compare (AskAmerica health schema, Sept 2026 snapshot) plus CHQPR, Chartis, Public Citizen, and a 2011-2022 national closure-prediction study

Summary

Patient mix tracks closure risk closely, but the operative driver is the mix of what payers actually pay relative to cost — not simply how much Medicaid or uninsured care a hospital delivers. The two national risk screens active in 2026 (CHQPR's cost-report-based model and Public Citizen's payer-mix screen) converge on a similar population: roughly a third of the country's ~1,900 rural hospitals (about 700) are financially at risk, and a March 2026 screen flagged 446 hospitals nationally using a payer-mix threshold of ≥20% Medicaid/low-income-government coverage combined with negative net margins. A 2026 machine-learning study of 2,683 rural hospitals over 2011-2022 found payer mix and financial-margin variables among the top predictors of the 5.2% that actually closed, with the model discriminating closers from survivors at AUC 0.89. The mechanism CHQPR's own cost-report data highlights, however, is not the intuitive one: at already-vulnerable rural hospitals, losses from underpaying private/commercial insurance plans are typically larger than losses from Medicaid or uninsured patients, because roughly half of rural hospital volume nationally is privately insured (employer plans and Medicare Advantage) and it is those payments, not Medicare/Medicaid rates, that most often determine whether the hospital is profitable.

1. What "patient mix" concretely predicts, per the closure-risk literature

  • CHQPR (Center for Healthcare Quality and Payment Reform), July 2026: ~700 of the nation's roughly 1,900 rural hospitals (about one-third) are at risk of closing on financial grounds; over 260 are at immediate risk. Over 40% of rural hospitals lose money on patient services alone, and almost a third lost money overall in 2024-25 once pandemic-era federal aid ended. In 11 states, 50%+ of rural hospitals are at risk.
  • Public Citizen, March 31 2026: identified 446 hospitals nationally as "at heightened risk of closing or reducing services" using an explicit payer-mix + margin rule — hospitals with a payer mix of at least 20% Medicaid and other low-income government coverage, and that averaged negative net margins from 2022-2024 — flagged as exposed to Medicaid cuts under the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA). Several named hospitals publicly disputed the individual classification (citing transitional ownership changes or state backstops), underscoring that the payer-mix screen is a risk indicator, not a certainty.
  • BMC Health Services Research (2026), explainable ML study: of 2,683 U.S. rural hospitals tracked 2011-2022, 139 (5.2%) closed. The XGBoost/SHAP model (recall 86%, accuracy 75%, AUC 0.89) found closure risk driven by sustained financial weakness/volatility (total margin, ROE variability, current ratio), operational instability (low volume, bed-day variability), policy context (years since Medicaid expansion — expansion states see fewer closures via reduced uncompensated care), and community sociodemographics. Prior literature it cites also found higher Medicare Advantage penetration associated with improved stability, and a higher uninsured share a significant closure predictor in hospital-year panel data.
  • Cumulative toll: over the last decade, 208 hospitals have closed nationally (MedPAC), including 100+ complete rural closures (Sheps Center/CHQPR) plus 53 additional rural hospitals since 2023 that eliminated inpatient services to convert to Rural Emergency Hospital status — together affecting more than 4 million rural residents' access to an ER or inpatient bed.

2. The mechanism is more specific than "bad payer mix = closure"

CHQPR's hospital cost-report data for 2024-25 show that among at-risk rural hospitals, losses on uninsured and Medicaid patients are real but are typically smaller than the median loss on privately insured patients. That is counterintuitive against the usual "Medicaid/uninsured-heavy hospitals close" framing, and it is explained by volume: private insurance (employer coverage plus Medicare Advantage) covers roughly half of rural hospital services nationally, so underpayment by commercial plans relative to cost is the single biggest swing factor in whether a small rural hospital's total patient-services margin is positive or negative. Chartis data corroborate that commercial+Medicare Advantage payer mix is nearly identical between rural and urban hospitals in 2025 (rural inpatient 50% / outpatient 55% vs. urban inpatient 51% / outpatient 55%) — so it is not that rural hospitals see categorically different payer mix from urban ones; it is that the same commercial share generates a much larger loss per rural hospital because fixed costs (24/7 ED staffing, etc.) are spread across far fewer patients.

Government and policy exposure compounds this: hospitals that depend on Medicaid/uninsured volume are disproportionately reliant on local tax subsidies or state appropriations to survive underpayment, which is why closure risk also tracks ownership type, not payer mix alone — see the ownership chart above. In the AskAmerica CMS Hospital Compare snapshot, 34.8% of nonmetro (rural) hospitals are government-owned (hospital district/authority, local, state, or federal) versus 13.9% of metro hospitals, while for-profit/physician ownership is less than half as common in rural areas (11.2% vs. 26.8%). For-profit systems tend to avoid or exit markets where the payer mix cannot support a margin without a subsidy backstop; government ownership is the structural adaptation that keeps many high-Medicaid/uninsured rural hospitals open despite an unfavorable payer mix.

3. What this warehouse could and could not verify directly

AskAmerica's health schema (health.cms_hospital_quality, health.hospital_with_geography, health.cms_pos_facilities) carries hospital identity, ownership, quality ratings, and a rural/urban (RUCC) classification, but is a current CMS snapshot with no hospital financial cost-report data (patient-services margin, payer-specific revenue) and no closure-event history — so the payer-mix-to-closure relationship itself could not be computed from this corpus and is reported from the cited primary/secondary sources instead. What the warehouse could verify directly, and did (live query against health.hospital_with_geography, deduplicated on facility_id), is the ownership-type skew between rural and urban hospitals shown above — which is the structural corroborating evidence for why payer mix translates into closure exposure unevenly by ownership form. A reader wanting the underlying payer-mix and margin figures hospital-by-hospital should consult CHQPR's RuralHospitals.org methodology page or Public Citizen's March 2026 report directly, both of which publish hospital-level detail this corpus does not carry.

Every query behind this report

2 warehouse calls ran in this session, in order. Each is reproducible against the same snapshot.

query — 24 rows — 18472 ms
SELECT metro_nonmetro, hospital_ownership, COUNT(*) AS n
FROM health.hospital_with_geography
WHERE metro_nonmetro IS NOT NULL AND hospital_ownership IS NOT NULL
GROUP BY metro_nonmetro, hospital_ownership
ORDER BY metro_nonmetro, n DESC
query — 24 rows — 4712 ms
SELECT metro_nonmetro, hospital_ownership, COUNT(DISTINCT facility_id) AS n_hospitals
FROM health.hospital_with_geography
WHERE metro_nonmetro IS NOT NULL AND hospital_ownership IS NOT NULL
GROUP BY metro_nonmetro, hospital_ownership
ORDER BY metro_nonmetro, n_hospitals DESC
