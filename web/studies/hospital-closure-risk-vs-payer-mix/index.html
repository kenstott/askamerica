Payer mix predicts hospital closure risk strongly for rural facilities, but the mechanism runs through state Medicaid policy and margins, not raw hospital-type composition

Literature synthesis (Chartis, CHQPR, Health Affairs, Sheps Center) plus an original askamerica cross-check of hospital-type mix by state Medicaid-expansion status

Summary

Yes, strongly — but the relationship runs mainly through a hospital's government-payer reliance interacting with state Medicaid policy, not through a simple 'more Medicaid patients = more risk' rule. The best-documented model (Chartis Center for Rural Health, a multilevel logistic regression on 1,844 open rural hospitals benchmarked against hospitals that already closed) finds that being in a state that expanded Medicaid cuts a rural hospital's predicted closure risk by about 62% on average, holding financial and operational performance fixed — one of the single largest effects in the model, alongside government ownership (-70%) and health-system affiliation (-50%). Separately, a Health Affairs analysis of 2,986 hospitals (2010-2016) found hospitals with a 65% Medicare-patient share had roughly 24% probability of closing or being acquired versus 16% for an otherwise-identical hospital at 35% Medicare share. But the mechanism is not uniform: a 2024 Health Affairs Scholar study of Critical Access Hospitals specifically found higher Medicare and Medicaid shares were associated with better, not worse, operating margins (cost-based reimbursement protects them), and a July 2026 Center for Healthcare Quality and Payment Reform report — using hospital cost-report data — found that at today's at-risk rural hospitals, underpayment by private insurance, not Medicaid or uninsured care, is the single largest driver of patient-service losses. An original cross-check we ran against askamerica's own CMS hospital directory found no significant difference in Critical-Access-Hospital share or rural/non-rural composition between Medicaid-expansion and non-expansion states (p=0.54 and p=0.77) — meaning the closure-risk gap between those state groups is not explained by a different mix of hospital types, it shows up in how those similar hospitals perform financially once state policy and payer rates are applied.

What the closure literature actually finds

The single most-cited quantitative model here is the Chartis Center for Rural Health's 2022 rural hospital vulnerability study, which built a multilevel logistic regression from Medicare Hospital Cost Report data, Kaiser Family Foundation Medicaid-expansion status, and the Area Deprivation Index, tested against the closure history tracked by the UNC Cecil G. Sheps Center. Of 16 candidate indicators, 9 were statistically significant, including: Medicaid expansion status (-62% closure likelihood), government control status (-70%), system affiliation (-50%), percent change in total revenue (a 1-point increase cuts closure odds ~3%), and percent of outpatient revenue (a 1-point increase cuts closure odds ~5%). The model flagged 453 of 1,844 open rural hospitals (24.6%) as 'vulnerable,' split into 216 'most vulnerable' and 237 'at risk.' Notably, 75% of the 'most vulnerable' cohort sat in non-expansion states, and of the eight states with the most closures since 2010, none had expanded Medicaid.

Chartis's 2025 update reports 46% of rural hospitals now run a negative operating margin (up from 39% five years earlier) and 432 hospitals flagged vulnerable to closure — the underlying payer-mix mechanism identified in 2022 has, if anything, intensified.

At the acute-care level generally (not just rural), a Health Affairs study of 2,986 hospitals' cost reports, 2010-2016, found a hospital with a 65% Medicare-patient share had about a 24% chance of closing or being acquired versus 16% for an identical hospital with a 35% Medicare share — Medicare-heavy hospitals had lower, more rapidly declining margins. Note on sourcing: a direct fetch of this article returned an HTTP 403 from healthaffairs.org; the figures above are reported as summarized by independent secondary coverage of the same study, not read directly from the primary article's own data table. A separate exploratory study of hospital bankruptcy filings (PMC9858769) similarly found a higher Medicaid share in the payer mix significantly raised the likelihood of financial collapse.

The relationship is not universal across hospital types, however. A 2024 Health Affairs Scholar study of Critical Access Hospitals (2011-2023) found the opposite sign for that subgroup: a higher Medicare payer-mix share was associated with a higher operating margin (coefficient 0.10, 95% CI 0.05-0.15) and a higher Medicaid share was also associated with a higher margin (coefficient 0.09, 95% CI 0.04-0.14) — because CAHs are reimbursed on a cost-plus basis by Medicare, government-payer volume is a financial cushion for them specifically, not a drag.

Most tellingly, a July 2026 Center for Healthcare Quality and Payment Reform report, built directly from hospital cost-report data, complicates the simple payer-mix story further: at today's roughly 700 at-risk rural hospitals (one-third of all US rural hospitals), while hospitals do lose money on uninsured and Medicaid patients, losses on privately-insured patients are the single biggest driver of overall financial losses — because about half of the services at the average rural hospital go to privately-insured patients (including Medicare Advantage), and many private plans pay less than the cost of care in low-volume rural settings. CHQPR's conclusion: the popular narrative that under-expansion of Medicaid or high Medicaid dependence alone explains rural closures is incomplete; inadequate private-payer rates are doing at least as much damage.

What the closure record shows geographically

The Sheps Center's authoritative closure tracker counts 197 rural hospital closures and conversions since January 2005 (109 complete closures, 88 service-reducing conversions), concentrated heavily in the Southeast and lower Great Plains. Chartis's tabulation for 2010-2019 found Texas (20 closures), Tennessee (12), Oklahoma (7), Georgia (7), Alabama (6), and Missouri (6) as the hardest-hit states — a list dominated by non-Medicaid-expansion states at the time of that study (Missouri has since expanded). This state-level clustering is the strongest visible signature of the patient-mix/policy relationship: it is not that these states' hospitals serve categorically different types of patients, but that the same kind of hospital (small, Medicare/Medicaid-dependent, low-occupancy) performs financially worse where state Medicaid policy leaves a larger uninsured population and lower program reimbursement.

What askamerica's own data can and cannot add

askamerica's health schema (health.cms_hospital_quality / health.hospital_with_geography) carries facility-level hospital type, ownership, rural/urban classification, and CMS quality ratings, but no facility-level payer-mix, financial (margin, days-cash-on-hand), or closure/survival data — the actual variables the Chartis and CHQPR models use come from Medicare Hospital Cost Report Information System data, which is not in this corpus. We flagged this gap rather than substituting a nearby but different column.

What we could test with the corpus: whether the closure-risk gap between Medicaid-expansion and non-expansion states is simply a difference in the underlying mix of hospital types those states have (more Critical Access Hospitals, more rural hospitals) rather than a difference in how similar hospitals perform. Using CMS's current hospital directory (deduplicated to distinct facility_id after finding and correcting a 15x row fan-out from the geography join), we computed each state's share of hospitals that are Critical Access Hospitals and its share of non-metro hospitals, then compared the 10 current non-expansion states (AL, FL, GA, KS, MS, SC, TN, TX, WI, WY, per KFF's current tracker) against the 40 expansion states + DC.

Result: mean CAH share is 31.0% in expansion states vs. 26.2% in non-expansion states (Welch t=-0.63, p=0.54), and mean non-metro hospital share is 40.3% vs. 42.8% (Welch t=0.30, p=0.77) — neither difference is statistically distinguishable from noise at this sample size (n=50 states). This is a genuine, if modest, extension of the literature: it rules out the simplest alternative explanation (that non-expansion states just happen to have structurally more rural/CAH hospitals) for why they show up disproportionately in the Chartis vulnerability rankings. It is consistent with Chartis's own regression, where Medicaid-expansion status carried an independent effect net of hospital type — the risk gap is about how a given hospital type performs under different state payer conditions, not which hospital types each state happens to have.

Bottom line and caveats

  • For rural and small hospitals specifically, patient/payer mix is one of the most consistently significant predictors of closure risk in peer-reviewed and industry research, but its effect works jointly with state Medicaid policy, ownership structure, and system affiliation — not as an isolated payer-share number.
  • The direction of the effect is not universal: it is protective for Critical Access Hospitals (cost-based Medicare/Medicaid reimbursement) and a risk factor for general acute-care hospitals reliant on standard Medicare/Medicaid rates.
  • The newest primary-source analysis (CHQPR, July 2026) argues private-insurance underpayment, not Medicaid/uninsured mix, is now the dominant driver of losses at the highest-risk rural hospitals — a genuine point of disagreement with the framing that Medicaid dependence alone is the main story.
  • Our own state-level check found no significant difference in hospital-TYPE composition between Medicaid-expansion and non-expansion states, so the observed closure-risk gap between those groups reflects differential financial performance of similar hospitals, not a different underlying hospital mix.
  • Caveat: the Health Affairs 65%-vs-35%-Medicare-share statistic could not be independently verified against the primary journal article (403 on direct fetch); it is reported via secondary summary only.
