Payer mix predicts hospital financial distress but is not an independent predictor of actual closure once occupancy, ownership and margin are controlled for

Rural: ASPE/HCRIS-AHA Cox hazard model, 2012-2023 (2,005 hospitals, 21,659 hospital-years, 120 closures/conversions). Urban: GAO case studies of 5 closures, FY2018-2022.

Summary

Weakly, and mostly indirectly. The single most rigorous study that actually models hospital closure as an event — an HHS/ASPE Cox proportional-hazards analysis of 2,005 rural hospitals over 2012-2023 (120 real closures/conversions) — finds that a hospital's Medicare or Medicaid share of discharges has no statistically significant independent effect on its hazard of closing (Medicaid share: hazard ratio 0.45, p=0.40; Medicare share: HR 0.87, p=0.84), once occupancy rate, ownership type, profit margin, leverage, Critical Access Hospital status and local market position are held constant. What DOES predict closure strongly is low occupancy (a 7-point occupancy drop raises risk from 3.3% to 4.5%), for-profit ownership (for-profit hospitals are ~3x more likely to close than government hospitals), and being in a county adjacent to an urban area (+80% relative risk, i.e. "bypass" competition).

That does not mean patient mix is irrelevant — it means its effect runs through margins rather than acting as an independent risk factor once you already know a hospital's financial and operating profile. Policy-facing vulnerability indices (Chartis, GAO, Commonwealth Fund) treat payer mix — specifically Medicaid/uninsured concentration and state Medicaid-expansion status — as a leading indicator of who will be pushed into distress by a *future* payment shock (e.g., projected Medicaid work-requirement or DSH cuts), which is a different, forward-looking claim than "payer mix has historically caused closures." Both can be true at once: payer mix shapes a hospital's baseline margin cushion, but among hospitals that actually closed historically, their Medicaid share looked statistically indistinguishable from hospitals that stayed open (17% vs 18% of discharges) — it was occupancy, ownership, and accumulated financial distress that separated them.

What the closure-hazard model actually shows

The ASPE issue brief (Volkov, Ghosh, Sheingold & Bai, May 2026) merged HCRIS cost-report data with AHA Annual Survey data, 2012-2023, and matched closures/conversions to the Sheps Center's verified rural closure list. Descriptively, closed hospitals differed sharply from open ones on occupancy (31% vs 47%), ownership (38% for-profit vs 9%), profit margin (-2% vs 5%) and leverage (64% vs 34% liability-to-asset) — but their Medicaid share of discharges was nearly identical (17% vs 18%).

The authors' own conclusion: "We found no evidence that system affiliation or the proportion of Medicare or Medicaid patients predicts a rural hospital's risk of closure or conversion." This is a rare instance of a data-driven finding cutting against the dominant policy narrative, and it is worth taking seriously precisely because the study controls for the confounds (occupancy, ownership, margin) that a raw payer-mix comparison would miss.

Why the policy literature still foregrounds payer mix

Several widely cited sources emphasize payer mix heavily, but they are answering an adjacent question — financial vulnerability to a *future* payment shock, not the historical determinants of realized closure:

  • Chartis (2025 Rural Health State of the State) reports 46% of rural hospitals operating at a loss and 432 at risk of closure, using a 10-factor vulnerability index that includes case-mix index, occupancy, and Medicaid-expansion status. In non-expansion states, 53% of rural hospitals operate in the red vs. a lower share in expansion states — Medicaid-expansion status is described as the variable with the single greatest power to *reduce* projected closure likelihood in their model. A 15-20% Medicaid revenue cut is projected to cost rural hospitals $1.8-2.4 billion.
  • GAO (GAO-18-634, GAO-21-93) found 64 rural closures from 2013-2017, with 77% in the South and disproportionately in non-Medicaid-expansion states (only 17% of closures occurred in expansion states) — a state-level correlational pattern, not a hospital-level controlled hazard estimate.
  • GAO (GAO-25-106473, Aug 2025), examining 5 urban hospital closures in 2022-2023 in depth, found the proximate cause in every case was multi-year financial decline, compounded by aging infrastructure, low inpatient volume, weak/ separate ownership structures, and poor management — payer mix was contextual background (Medicare+Medicaid+private = 81% of national hospital revenue) rather than a named driver in the case narratives.
  • Commonwealth Fund / CBO-based projections (2025) model that proposed Medicaid work-requirement policy would disproportionately erode margins at hospitals with the highest existing Medicaid patient share — again a forward margin-shock claim, not a backward-looking closure-cause finding.

Put together: payer mix is a real and heavily used input to forecasting who is exposed to the *next* payment shock, and it correlates with weak baseline margins — but in the one study that tests it against actual closures with proper controls, it drops out once occupancy, ownership and existing financial performance are already known.
