Litigation Drove a Real, Measurable Delinquency Shock; the Fiscal/Budget Baseline Has Already Repriced Around a Settled Post-SAVE Regime

NY Fed Consumer Credit Panel/Equifax and CBO federal credit-program estimates, 2024-2026

Summary

Litigation over student-loan forgiveness has produced a large, real, and still-unresolved shock to actual repayment and delinquency flows -- but the federal government's own fiscal accounting has already moved on and re-priced around a settled, post-litigation baseline. On the ground: about 3.6 million borrowers newly defaulted on federal student loans in 2025:Q4-2026:Q1 alone (NY Fed), the quarterly flow into serious (90+ day) delinquency has swung between roughly 8% and 13% annualized since 2024 rather than settling into a stable trend, and roughly 7 million borrowers who were enrolled in the litigation-frozen SAVE plan are still sitting in an administrative forbearance that has kept them out of the data entirely -- meaning a second default wave is still ahead, not behind, as of September 2026. On the budget side, by contrast, the Congressional Budget Office has already treated the outcome as settled: it dropped its projected subsidy cost on new federal student loans from 18 cents to 4 cents per dollar lent between the FY2025 and FY2026 estimates, entirely because OBBBA's Repayment Assistance Plan (RAP) is scored as replacing SAVE and the older IDR regime going forward. So the honest answer is: the flow effects are large and still live; the pricing that has moved on is specifically the forward-looking government cost estimate, not the delinquency data itself, and even that official estimate is contradicted by CBO's own fair-value (market-risk-adjusted) subsidy rate, which still sits at 18 cents per dollar -- suggesting professional risk-pricing has not fully bought the 4-cent number either.

1. What litigation actually did to loans in repayment

The sequence matters because it explains why the delinquency data looks volatile rather than trending: (1) COVID-era forbearance (2020-2023) suspended payments and interest and drove the delinquent/defaulted share of borrowers below 1% by late 2022; (2) payments resumed October 2023 with a 12-month credit-reporting "on-ramp" that shielded missed payments from credit bureaus through October 2024; (3) the Biden administration's SAVE income-driven repayment plan launched in 2024 was immediately sued by Republican-led states, and in February 2025 the Eighth Circuit held it unlawful; roughly 7 million SAVE enrollees were placed into an administrative forbearance -- not required to pay, and (until August 1, 2025) not accruing interest -- while the litigation played out; (4) the first real delinquencies therefore only started appearing on credit reports in 2025:Q1, and because federal default requires 270 days past due, the first wave of actual defaults only began in 2025:Q4, five-plus years after the pandemic pause started.

The NY Fed's Consumer Credit Panel data show this landing in two visible ways. First, the quarterly annualized flow into serious (90+ day) student-loan delinquency swung from 12.88% in 2024:Q2 down to 7.83% in 2025:Q2 and back up to 10.86% in 2026:Q1 -- a pattern driven by reporting-cutover mechanics (on-ramp expiration, SAVE forbearance timing), not a normal economic cycle. Second, an estimated 1 million borrowers defaulted in 2025:Q4 and another 2.6 million in 2026:Q1 (about 3.6 million cumulative), with average credit scores among the newly defaulted falling 91 points (567 to 476) between 2024:Q3 and 2025:Q4. Defaulted borrowers are also disproportionately behind on other debt (about 40% past-due on auto loans, 56% on credit cards, 20% on mortgages among those holding each product), though the NY Fed's own researchers conclude the population is small enough (about 2% of the whole credit panel) that broader contagion into other credit markets is 'premature' as a concern.

2. The second wave that hasn't happened yet

Critically, the roughly 7 million borrowers who were in the SAVE plan and shunted into litigation-driven forbearance have not yet flowed through the same pipeline as the borrowers who defaulted in late 2025/early 2026 -- 'very few have re-entered repayment since missed payments were reported to credit bureaus,' per the NY Fed's May 2026 analysis, which explicitly flags that 'a second wave of defaults might emerge as these 7 million borrowers reach the nine-month mark' after being moved off SAVE and into RAP or IBR under the December 2025 Missouri settlement and the March 2026 court order that vacated most of the SAVE rule. In other words: as of this writing (September 2026), the litigation's largest single repayment-flow effect -- what happens to the SAVE cohort now that the program itself is gone -- is still working its way through the data, not something that has already been absorbed and settled.

Separately, collections mechanics that would translate delinquency into real cash-flow and credit consequences are themselves still being turned back on in stages, not already resolved: the Treasury Offset Program restarted May 2025, and wage garnishment of defaulted borrowers only began with notices to the first 1,000 borrowers around January 2026 -- so the downstream economic bite of the default wave already recorded in the data is still mostly ahead of borrowers, not behind them.

3. What has actually been 'priced in as settled'

The one place the litigation-and-policy churn has demonstrably been treated as resolved is the federal government's own forward budget scoring. CBO's January 2026 estimate of federal credit program costs shows the projected subsidy rate on newly issued student loans falling from 18 cents lost per dollar lent (FY2025 estimate) to just 4 cents (FY2026 estimate) -- one of the lowest subsidy rates in the 30+ year history of the Direct Loan program. That collapse is driven almost entirely by CBO now scoring RAP, not SAVE or the older IDR menu, as the governing repayment regime for new borrowers: the subsidy rate on undergraduate Unsubsidized Stafford loans fell from an estimated 37 cents under the old IDR/SAVE framework to under 10 cents under RAP. CRFB frames this correctly as a 'new era' in student lending, reversing a run-up in projected costs from a projected $135 billion 2014-cohort gain to a projected $205 billion 2024-cohort loss.

Two qualifications keep this from being a clean 'it's all settled' story. First, CBO's own fair-value estimate -- which incorporates market risk the way a private lender would, rather than discounting only at Treasury rates -- still puts the subsidy cost at 18 cents per dollar lent overall, and 44 cents for Subsidized Stafford loans in RAP. That is essentially unchanged from the FCRA-basis 2025 estimate, which suggests that once market-based risk pricing is applied, none of the 'savings' claimed on paper actually reflects reduced borrower default risk -- it reflects an accounting choice (FCRA discounting at the risk-free Treasury rate) plus a policy choice (steeper, faster-vesting payments under RAP) rather than evidence that delinquency risk itself has fallen. Second, Grad PLUS loans under RAP still carry a 27-cent subsidy rate and remain in flux as the program phases out over the next three years, and CRFB explicitly flags that the President 'still thinks he can unilaterally stop collecting defaulted debt for political reasons' (citing a further pause announced after OBBBA) and that PSLF, unreformed, keeps driving graduate-loan costs -- both of which mean the policy environment is not actually locked in even where the budget scoring already assumes it is.

Bottom line

Litigation has already changed real repayment and delinquency flows substantially -- millions of borrowers pushed through an on-ramp, into serious delinquency, and now into default in a compressed 18-month window that would not have happened on this timeline absent the SAVE lawsuits and the forbearance they forced -- and the largest single piece of that effect (the ~7 million former SAVE enrollees) is still moving through the pipeline as of September 2026, not resolved. What IS priced in as settled is narrower than the question of borrower risk: it's the federal budget's forward subsidy-cost estimate, which has been rebuilt around RAP as the enduring legal regime and shows a large paper improvement (18%→4% FCRA subsidy rate) that its own fair-value companion estimate (18%, unchanged) does not corroborate as a real reduction in default risk.
