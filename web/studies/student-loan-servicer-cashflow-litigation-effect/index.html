Student-loan delinquency spikes are mostly the pandemic pause unwinding, not the forgiveness litigation itself — but SAVE-specific forbearance is a real, separate, still-unresolved cost

NY Fed Household Debt and Credit Report, 2026Q1-Q2; AskAmerica corpus carries no student-loan delinquency series

Summary

Most of the recent jump in student-loan delinquency and default is a mechanical, already-anticipated unwind of the 2020-2023 payment pause — not new financial distress caused by the forgiveness litigation itself. About 3.6 million borrowers defaulted in just two quarters (2025Q4-2026Q1) as missed payments finally hit credit reports again, pushing the serious-delinquency rate to 10.3% of balances, close to (but still below) the ~11% pre-pandemic norm. The pace of new delinquencies has already crested (the year-over-year flow into serious delinquency fell from 12.9% to 7.8% between Q2 2025 and Q2 2026), and total student debt outstanding has been flat to slightly declining for over a year — evidence that the credit market has largely absorbed and 'priced in' this adjustment as one-time normalization, not an accelerating crisis. The pieces that are genuinely litigation/policy-driven and NOT yet settled are (1) the SAVE repayment plan, where roughly 7-7.7 million borrowers were held in court-ordered forbearance for about two years that did not count toward forgiveness, interest resumed in August 2025, and the plan was formally terminated after a reversed-and-remanded settlement fight that only resolved in spring 2026; and (2) collections enforcement itself, which the Administration started, paused, and is restarting on its own separate political timetable — with well over 5 million borrowers already in default and Education Department estimates of 4 million more headed there, before the 'Big Beautiful Bill' permanently restructured which repayment plans even exist going forward.

What actually moved, and why

At the start of the pandemic, federal student loan payments were paused and interest set to zero. After multiple extensions, the pause ended in September 2023, and a 12-month 'on-ramp' (through October 2024) meant missed payments still were not reported to credit bureaus. The first real delinquencies therefore only appeared on credit reports in 2025Q1, and since it takes 270 days of missed payments to reach federal default, 2025Q4 was the first quarter new defaults could show up at all. NY Fed economists estimate about 1 million borrowers defaulted in 2025Q4 and 2.6 million more in 2026Q1 — a total of 3.6 million in two quarters. Critically, three-quarters of these newly defaulted borrowers were current on their loans (or had no payment due) back in 2019, before the pandemic — this is a reporting/timing artifact of the pause ending, not evidence that the litigation over forgiveness pushed previously-healthy borrowers into distress.

The serious-delinquency rate (90+ days past due, share of balances) rose from 9.6% in 2025Q4 to 10.3% in 2026Q1 — approaching but still below the roughly 11% pre-pandemic baseline. More tellingly, the *flow* into serious delinquency — the leading indicator of where this is headed — has already turned: the four-quarter moving sum fell from 16.2% (2025Q4) to 10.9% (2026Q1), and the year-over-year quarterly comparison fell from 12.88% (2025Q2) to 7.83% (2026Q2). NY Fed's own researchers concluded in May 2026 that 'the initial wave has crested' and that 'the largest wave of student loan defaults has passed.'

The genuinely litigation-driven, still-unsettled piece: SAVE and forgiveness policy

Separate from the general pause-unwind, the SAVE (Saving on a Valuable Education) income-driven repayment plan was launched by the Biden administration in August 2023 and almost immediately sued over by a Missouri-led coalition of states arguing ED lacked authority to alter repayment terms. In July 2024, the 8th Circuit ordered ED to stop implementing parts of SAVE while the case proceeded, and ED responded by placing nearly 8 million SAVE borrowers into interest-free administrative forbearance. Under the Trump administration, interest resumed accruing on those forborne balances in August 2025 — a policy choice made to comply with the injunction, though which specific part of the injunction required it was never fully clarified. None of that roughly two years of forbearance counted toward IDR or Public Service Loan Forgiveness, a real and quantifiable cost in delayed forgiveness timelines for millions of borrowers.

The legal endgame itself whipsawed: ED and the plaintiff states reached a settlement in December 2025 to formally end SAVE, but in March 2026 a federal judge declined to approve it and instead dismissed the case outright, only for the 8th Circuit to reverse that dismissal and order the settlement accepted. SAVE effectively terminated in March 2026, and servicers only began actively moving borrowers off it in July 2026 (a 90-day window, with some servicers like Nelnet notifying borrowers in waves through the end of 2026). Separately, the 'One Big Beautiful Bill' signed July 4, 2025 permanently restructured the repayment landscape — phasing out SAVE, PAYE, IBR, and ICR entirely by July 2028 in favor of a new Repayment Assistance Plan (RAP) and a tiered standard plan, and eliminating economic-hardship deferment.

As of the most recent NY Fed data (2026Q1), very few of the ~7.7 million ex-SAVE borrowers had re-entered active repayment even after missed-payment reporting resumed. Liberty Street Economics explicitly flags this as a reason a 'second wave of defaults might emerge' once these borrowers reach the nine-month mark of required repayment — meaning the litigation-specific effect on delinquency flows is still working its way through the system and is not yet reflected in the 2026Q1-Q2 figures used above. This is the part of the question that is NOT settled.

Collections enforcement compounds the uncertainty on its own separate track. In May 2026 the Education Department announced it would resume involuntary collections — wage garnishment and Treasury offset of tax refunds, federal salaries, and Social Security — against more than 5 million borrowers already in default, with an estimated 4 million more expected to follow (a combined toll approaching 25% of all federal borrowers). Garnishment notices began going out in small batches (about 1,000 borrowers) starting the week of January 7, 2026. But ED then announced a delay to involuntary collections to accommodate implementation of the Working Families Tax Cuts Act's repayment reforms, and roughly 2.6 million severely delinquent borrowers (120+ days past due) were instead administratively referred to ED's Default Resolution Group in 2026Q1 rather than moved straight to garnishment — a policy choice actively holding back the final, most consequential stage of the delinquency-to-collections flow while the mechanics get sorted out.

What is priced in / settled

  • Aggregate balances are stable: total student debt outstanding has been essentially flat for over a year — $1.658T (2026Q1) to $1.651T (2026Q2), a $7 billion quarterly decline — showing no sign that litigation uncertainty is distorting aggregate borrowing or paydown behavior.
  • Contagion to other credit is limited: delinquent/defaulted student-loan borrowers make up only about 2% of the entire credit population, and their balances represent just 2.7% of auto loan debt, 2% of credit-card debt, and 1% of mortgage debt nationally. NY Fed explicitly states that 'fears of broader contagion to other credit products are premature.'
  • The pace of new delinquency has already peaked: the sharp decline in the flow-into-serious-delinquency rate (both the four-quarter moving sum and the year-over-year quarterly comparison) indicates markets and servicers have already absorbed the bulk of the pause-unwind shock as a one-time adjustment rather than an open-ended deterioration.
  • The forgiveness-litigation question itself is legislatively settled going forward, even though the SAVE wind-down was not: the One Big Beautiful Bill's permanent elimination of SAVE/PAYE/IBR/ICR by 2028 in favor of RAP and a tiered standard plan removes the underlying legal uncertainty for future borrowers, even as the transition cost for current SAVE enrollees is still being worked through.
