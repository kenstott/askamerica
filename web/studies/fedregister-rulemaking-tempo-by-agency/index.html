Rulemaking pace varies ~27x across federal agencies, and has declined roughly 15% economy-wide since 2010

Federal Register final and proposed rules by issuing agency, 2010-2025 (fedregister.fr_documents, govinfo.gov bulk XML)

Summary

Rulemaking pace varies enormously by agency and has drifted down for most large regulators since 2010. Over 2010-2025, the Department of Transportation (DOT) averaged 747 final rules a year, roughly 27 times the Securities and Exchange Commission's 28 a year — a gap that reflects DOT's and DHS's many operating sub-units (FAA, FMCSA, FHWA, NHTSA, PHMSA; TSA, CBP, USCIS, Coast Guard) each issuing their own technical rules, versus single-mission agencies like SEC or NRC. Economy-wide, average annual final-rule output fell from about 3,595/year in 2010-2017 to about 3,073/year in 2018-2025, a 14.5% decline (linear trend: about -61 rules/year, R²=0.64). The decline is not uniform: EPA (-15.8 rules/year, R²=0.61) and DHS (-17.6 rules/year, R²=0.59) show the steepest, most consistent downward trends among large agencies, while Interior and the Nuclear Regulatory Commission show flat-to-slightly-rising paces.

Four departments (DOT, EPA, DHS, Commerce) together publish roughly half of all final rules issued government-wide in a typical year. The gap between the busiest and slowest agencies is a level effect from jurisdiction breadth, not just tempo: DOT's five operating administrations (FAA, FHWA, FMCSA, NHTSA, PHMSA) and DHS's seven regulatory components (CBP, USCIS, ICE, TSA, Coast Guard, FEMA, CISA) each generate their own technical rulemakings, while SEC, Labor, VA, and NRC issue comparatively few but often higher-stakes rules. Year-to-year variability (coefficient of variation) is highest for Defense (41%) and lowest for Commerce (10%) and DOT (11%) among the top agencies, meaning DOT is both the busiest agency and one of the most consistent in its output from year to year.

How the pace has changed over time

Total final-rule output (all agencies combined) fell from a 2010-2017 average of about 3,595/year to a 2018-2025 average of about 3,073/year, a decline of roughly 14.5% (regression slope -61.2 rules/year, R²=0.64, n=16 years). Proposed rules (PRORULE, the leading indicator of future rulemaking) fell more sharply in percentage terms, from roughly 2,441/year average in 2010-2017 to roughly 1,943/year average in 2018-2025 — about a 20% drop — consistent with a longer-running slowdown in the regulatory pipeline, not just final publication.

The decline is agency-specific rather than uniform. EPA and DHS account for most of the aggregate decline (slopes of -15.8 and -17.6 rules/year respectively, both with R² above 0.58, meaning a genuinely consistent downward trend rather than noise). DOT, the single largest rule-producer, shows only a modest downward trend (-5.0/year, R²=0.08 — essentially flat with high year-to-year noise, e.g. a spike to 904 rules in 2022). Interior and NRC show slight upward trends. 2025, the most recent complete year in the data, recorded 2,442 final rules — the lowest annual total in the 2010-2025 window — consistent with the typical first-year dip seen at the start of prior administrations (e.g. 2017 also dipped, to 3,282 from 3,855 in 2016) as new political leadership pauses and reviews the regulatory pipeline before issuing its own rules.

External corroboration

The GW Regulatory Studies Center's RegStats tracker and its annual 'Regulatory Year in Review' reports corroborate both the overall pattern and the administration-driven fluctuation the warehouse data shows. Per Regulatory Studies Center reporting, total pages published in the Federal Register rose 19% in 2024 to the highest level on record since 1936, and by the end of their respective fourth years, the number of economically significant final rules stood at 319 for the Biden administration versus 247 for the first Trump administration and 227 for the Obama administration — a genuine rise in high-impact rulemaking intensity even as this warehouse's broader RULE-type counts (which include routine as well as significant rules) show a modest downward drift in raw volume over the same span. This is not a contradiction: raw rule COUNT and rule SIGNIFICANCE/IMPACT are different metrics, and the data here (fedregister.fr_significance) has too many gaps (missing entirely for 2018-2025, 91% null even where loaded) to independently verify the significant-rule trend from the warehouse alone, so that figure is sourced externally rather than recomputed. The pattern of a first-year dip in final-rule output at the start of a new administration (seen in this data for both 2017 and 2025) also matches the well-documented 'regulatory pause' new administrations typically impose while reviewing pending and recent rules from their predecessor.
