A decade after the fracking boom, host counties have more people (Bakken) or fewer (Marcellus/Utica), but income and jobs growth lag the nation either way

Census decennial population (2010→2020), ACS 5-yr median household income (2013→2023, CPI-adjusted), BLS QCEW employment (2019→2023)

Summary

Ten years on, fracking-boom counties are, at best, no better off than the nation as a whole on income and jobs — and in the Marcellus/Utica gas region, most measures moved backward. The two big U.S. shale plays diverged sharply on population: North Dakota's Bakken oil counties grew population 74% from 2010 to 2020 (vs. +7% nationally), while the 22-county Appalachian Marcellus/Utica gas region lost 4.3% of its population over the same decade. But on the outcomes that matter most for living standards, both regions underperformed the country: real (inflation-adjusted) median household income in Bakken counties was essentially flat (+0.3%) from 2013 to 2023, versus +13.4% nationally; Marcellus/Utica counties gained 10.4% real income, still below the national pace. Total employment in both regions has been shrinking even in the last five years (2019-2023) while the U.S. as a whole added jobs — Bakken employment fell 11.8% and Marcellus/Utica fell 3.2%, against national growth of +3.4%. Independent peer-reviewed and think-tank research on the same counties reaches the same conclusion: the boom raised output and wages in a handful of extraction jobs, but most of the wealth left the region, and jobs, income, and population shares of the nation all declined over the 2008-2019 decade in the Appalachian counties specifically.

Step 1 — Which counties, and why these two regions

There is no single U.S. Census or BLS definition of a 'fracking county,' so this analysis follows the county lists used in the two most-cited long-run studies of shale-boom outcomes:

  • Bakken (oil), North Dakota — McKenzie, Williams, Mountrail, and Dunn counties, the core of the 2008-2014 Bakken oil boom (per Minneapolis Fed and Montana/ND state economist coverage).
  • Marcellus/Utica (natural gas), Appalachia — the 22-county core identified by the Ohio River Valley Institute's 2021 decade-long study: Belmont, Carroll, Guernsey, Harrison, Jefferson, Monroe, Noble (OH); Bradford, Greene, Lycoming, Sullivan, Susquehanna, Tioga, Washington, Wyoming (PA); Doddridge, Harrison, Marshall, Ohio, Ritchie, Tyler, Wetzel (WV) — the counties producing over 90% of the region's natural gas.

Eagle Ford (TX) and Permian Basin counties are discussed qualitatively below from published projections, but were not pulled into this connector's own county-level computation because the available literature for those plays is mostly forward-looking economic-impact projections (2013-2014 studies projecting 2020/2023 outcomes) rather than a settled after-the-fact accounting comparable to the Bakken/Marcellus studies — a genuine coverage gap, disclosed rather than papered over with a projection presented as an outcome.

Step 2 — Population: boom counties diverged completely

Using Census decennial counts (the only true census-quality county population series spanning the boom decade in this connector; the annual county Population Estimates table here only goes back to 2019), the two regions moved in opposite directions from 2010 to 2020:

Bakken (4 counties)39,96769,558+74.1%

The Bakken's oil-rig-count boom drew large in-migration (Williston and Watford City more than doubled in population 2000-2020, per the Minneapolis Fed), while gas-drilling in Appalachia is far more capital- and machinery-intensive per well and drew comparatively few permanent residents even as production surged — matching the ORVI finding that the 22 counties' population share of the nation fell nearly 10% from 2008-2019.

Step 3 — Real household income: both regions lag the nation

Median household income (ACS 5-year estimates, comparing the 2009-2013 and 2019-2023 vintages, both converted to 2023 dollars using BLS CPI-U) tells a more unified story than population does:

Region2013 real MHI (2023$)2023 nominal MHI% real change
Bakken (avg. of 4 counties)$88,385$88,623+0.3%
Marcellus/Utica (avg. of 22 counties)$55,147$60,896+10.4%
United States (population-weighted)$70,173$79,549+13.4%

Bakken counties' household income was already elevated by 2013 (oil-and-gas jobs pay roughly triple the national average weekly wage), but that level has essentially not moved in a decade in real terms — the initial income windfall did not compound the way national income did. Marcellus/Utica counties, starting from a much lower base, did see faster proportional income growth than Bakken, but still trailed the national rate by 3 percentage points over the decade.

Step 4 — Jobs: still shrinking in both regions, even recently

BLS QCEW county employment data here is only loaded back to 2019, so the reliable comparison is the last five years rather than the full decade — but the direction is unambiguous and consistent with the longer-run literature:

Total wages paid did rise in Marcellus/Utica counties (+13.7%, reflecting a higher-wage remaining workforce even as headcount fell) but fell in Bakken counties (−3.8%) — both regions employed fewer people in 2023 than in 2019 while the U.S. overall added jobs.

Step 5 — Cross-check against the published decade-long study

The connector's own 2010-2020/2013-2023/2019-2023 computations line up with the one true before/after decade study available in the literature — the Ohio River Valley Institute's analysis of the same 22 Marcellus/Utica counties from 2008 (pre-boom) to 2019 (near-peak production): personal income share of the nation fell 6.3%, jobs share fell 7.6%, and population share fell nearly 10% even as regional GDP grew 60% and the counties supplied the gas driving that output growth. ORVI's headline conclusion — 'gas drilling has done the opposite' of the promised prosperity — is corroborated, not contradicted, by this connector's own numbers for the same counties in the years since. Ninety percent of the wealth generated is estimated to leave local communities, largely as returns to out-of-state landowners, drilling companies, and capital rather than local wages (ORVI, 'Frackalachia'; Inside Climate News summary).

For the Bakken, independent research (Community Development journal, 2020) documents that economic benefits accrued disproportionately to new and non-resident workers, alongside sharply rising housing costs, crime, and declining social capital and volunteerism — a pattern consistent with population and wages rising together (unlike Appalachia) but quality-of-life and community-cohesion measures not improving in step. A separate academic study in the same journal on Bakken 'comprehensive wealth' likewise finds the region's broader wealth accounting (including social and human capital, not just income) is far less favorable than the raw income and population numbers alone suggest.

For Texas's Eagle Ford and Permian plays, the widely cited economic-impact figures — $87 billion in 2013 output, ~155,000 jobs, and a 2014 projection of 196,000 jobs and $137 billion by 2023 (Rigzone; UTSA/IED county-level appendix) — are industry-commissioned economic-impact projections, not audited after-the-fact outcomes, and this analysis did not locate (nor compute from primary data) a comparable independent ten-years-later accounting for those counties. That gap is disclosed rather than filled with an unverified number.

Caveats and what this does not show

  • Grain and averaging. Each region's figures average across counties of very different size — Belmont (OH) and McKenzie (ND) are each one data point in their region's average, so a single large or small county can move the regional number. The population and income results were not additionally leave-one-out tested here given the modest 4- and 22-county samples; a reader building on this should treat the regional averages as directional, not precision estimates.
  • Different vintages, not a single clean window. Population uses 2010→2020 decennial counts (true censuses, but count years, not '10 years before/after the boom' exactly); income uses 2009-2013 vs. 2019-2023 ACS 5-year windows (smoothed, overlapping periods, ~2-year publication lag); employment uses QCEW 2019→2023 only, because this connector's county-level QCEW table does not go back further. These are the best available windows in each source, not a single harmonized 10-year clock.
  • Correlation, not a causal isolation of fracking's effect. None of these figures control for the 2020 pandemic, the 2014-2016 oil price crash, or other shocks that hit these same years nationally and locally. The Marcellus/Utica employment decline, in particular, overlaps entirely with the pandemic-recovery period industry nationally was still working through.
  • 'Better off' is operationalized here as population, real household income, and employment — it does not capture housing costs, local tax-base and public-service funding gains, environmental and health costs, or long-run reclamation/decommissioning liabilities, all of which the cited qualitative literature (Community Development journal; ORVI) flags as material to how residents actually experienced the boom.
