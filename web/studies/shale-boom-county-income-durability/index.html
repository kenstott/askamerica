How much better off are the counties that hosted the fracking boom, ten years on?

Headline: mostly not much better off, and in the Bakken, worse. Comparing 2012 (boom underway) to 2022 (a decade later) across 30 core fracking counties in three plays, only the Eagle Ford (TX) core clearly outpaced the nation on jobs and real wages. The 22-county Marcellus/Utica core (OH/PA/WV) lost 5.4% of its jobs while the US gained 13.8%, and its total real wage bill grew only 1.5% against 26.8% nationally — essentially treading water while the country grew. The Bakken core (ND) lost employment (-17.7%) and its real wage bill fell 29.5%, even as its population grew 61% as workers and families settled in around the fields — more people splitting a shrinking pie.

What the literature already found

  • Feyrer, Mansur & Sacerdote, "Geographic Dispersion of Economic Shocks: Evidence from the Fracking Revolution" (American Economic Review 107(4), 2017) found real local benefits — $80k in wages and $132k in royalty/business income per $1M of new production within-county — but these effects decay quickly with distance and over time; roughly two-thirds of the wage bump persisted only two years.
  • Ohio River Valley Institute (2021, data through 2019) tracked the same 22 Appalachian counties used here from 2008–2019: county GDP grew 60% while the counties' shares of national jobs, income and population all fell (jobs -7.6%, income -6.3%, population ~-11%), and Pennsylvania's gas counties underperformed the state despite output growth.
  • University of Washington / Program on Climate Change, Ohio study: fracking counties had higher unemployment and poverty than non-fracking counties throughout the boom, despite outsized GDP growth — output and household welfare diverged.
  • James & Smith, "There will be blood: Crime rates in shale-rich U.S. counties" (J. Environmental Economics and Management, 2017): shale counties saw a rise in violent crime — one of several social costs (also documented: rises in STI rates and heavy-truck accidents in fracked PA counties) invisible in GDP or employment counts.
  • Heritage Foundation (contrarian): compared Pennsylvania (allowed fracking) to New York (banned it) across the Marcellus footprint and found the PA side did better on income and jobs than the NY side — against a real counterfactual, not just the national average, these counties can look better off even while trailing the nation.

Two findings not in the cited literature:

  1. The Bakken's real wage bill fell nearly 30% over the decade — it didn't just underperform the nation, it went backward in real dollars, even as population rose 61%, meaning per-capita prosperity fell further than the wage-bill number alone implies.
  2. Eagle Ford is the one play here that beat the national economy on every jobs/wage measure — a useful counter-case to a universal "resource curse" reading of the Ohio/ORVI findings, and consistent with the Heritage Foundation's counterfactual framing.

Per-worker real wages in Appalachia (CPI-U, 2022 base) rose roughly +7% over the decade even as total jobs fell — consistent with a capital-intensive industry paying a smaller, better-paid workforce: the "boom without broad-based benefit" pattern the literature describes, and with James & Smith's finding that production/GDP gains don't translate one-for-one into local household welfare.

Caveats

  • This is a before/after descriptive comparison against the national trend, not a causal estimate — it doesn't net out the 2020 pandemic shock, the 2014-16 oil price bust, or what these counties would have done absent fracking. Feyrer et al.'s geology-instrumented design is the right tool for causal attribution; this corpus doesn't carry well-level production data to replicate that at county grain.
  • 2012 and 2022 are single years, not averages — a bust year or price spike at either endpoint moves the growth rates shown.
  • The three county sets are widely used "core" definitions (ORVI/EIA), not an exhaustive shale-county list; a broader or narrower set would shift exact percentages, though the ranking (Eagle Ford best, Bakken worst) matches how the literature characterizes these plays.
  • ACS poverty rates are 5-year-vintage estimates (small-county reliability caveat applies).
  • CPI-U deflator used is 2 months stale per the tool's own staleness check (latest loaded 2026-05 vs. expected 2026-07) — irrelevant here since nothing is expressed in "today's dollars," only 2012 vs. 2022.
