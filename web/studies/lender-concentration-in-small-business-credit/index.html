SBA Small Business Lending Is Not Concentrated Among Lenders (HHI≈94), But Loan Dollars Are Extremely Concentrated Geographically (Gini≈0.83) — and Slightly More So in Metro Counties

SBA 7(a)/504 loan approvals, FY2019-2023, matched to USDA ERS Rural-Urban Continuum Codes

Summary

Two different things can both be called 'concentration' here, and they point opposite directions. Among lenders, SBA 7(a)/504 small business lending is NOT concentrated: computing a Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI) on each lender's share of $170.1 billion in gross loan approvals (FY2019-2023) gives a national HHI of about 94 on the standard 0-10,000 scale — far below the DOJ/FTC 'unconcentrated' cutoff of 1,500. 3,259 distinct lenders made SBA-guaranteed loans over the period; even the largest single lender (Live Oak Banking Company) holds only about 5.3% of dollar volume, and the top 10 lenders combined hold about 22.8%.

Across geography, by contrast, loan dollars are extremely concentrated: a Gini coefficient of 0.83 across ~2,900 county-level markets means the top 10% of counties by lending volume capture roughly three-quarters of all dollars — the ordinary signature of business activity itself being concentrated in big metro areas, not a lending-market defect.

Splitting by USDA's Rural-Urban Continuum classification, lender-market concentration is nearly identical and unconcentrated in both settings (HHI 98 in metro counties vs. 92 in nonmetro counties). Geographic concentration of dollars is high in both but somewhat more extreme within metro counties (Gini 0.75) than within nonmetro counties (Gini 0.59) — a few giant metros dominate the metro total more than a few large rural counties dominate the rural total. On a per-resident basis, metro counties received about 54% more SBA loan volume per capita ($548 vs. $357, 2019-2023 cumulative) and more loans per 1,000 residents (0.91 vs. 0.66), consistent with published findings that bank branch presence and local GDP are the strongest predictors of small business lending volume, and that both are lower in rural areas.

What 'concentration' can mean here, and why both need reporting

The question does not specify which sense of 'concentrated' is meant, so this report computes both, because the literature on this topic uses both and they disagree in interesting ways:

  • Lender/market concentration — is a handful of banks writing most of the loans, which would raise pricing-power and access concerns? Standard measure: HHI on lender dollar share.
  • Geographic concentration — is loan volume spatially clustered in a few places, leaving others underserved? Standard measure: Gini coefficient / Lorenz curve across counties.

A market can be geographically lopsided (because business activity itself is lopsided) while still being competitively unconcentrated (many lenders operating in every market), which is exactly the pattern found here.

Data and method

Source: SBA 7(a) and 504 loan approvals, FOIA public data (data.sba.gov), accessed via AskAmerica's fiscal.sba_loan_approvals table (one row per approved loan, borrower/project geography, lender, gross approval amount, fiscal year). Window: fiscal years 2019-2023 (pre-pandemic through post-pandemic recovery, avoiding both the small early-2010s sample and the 2020-21 PPP-adjacent lending surge as the sole basis for a multi-year figure). Total volume in window: $170.1 billion across roughly 287,000 approved loans.

Rural/urban classification: USDA Economic Research Service Rural-Urban Continuum Codes (RUCC), 2022 vintage (geo.rural_urban_continuum), which classifies every U.S. county into a 9-category code and a binary Metro/Nonmetro flag. RUCC is decennial-ish (updated roughly every 10 years) so a single 2022 vintage was applied across the whole 2019-2023 loan window; county boundaries and metro/nonmetro status do not typically change year to year, so this is a reasonable simplification, but it is a fixed-point classification, not a per-loan-year one.

Matching loans to counties: the SBA table carries a free-text project_county name and USPS state abbreviation, not a FIPS code, so counties were matched by uppercasing and appending 'COUNTY' to the SBA name and joining on state FIPS + county name against geo.rural_urban_continuum. This matched 274,008 of 286,833 loan rows (95.5%); the remaining 4.5% (independent cities, parishes, boroughs, and other county-equivalents whose SBA-recorded name doesn't take the 'X County' form, plus any misspellings) could not be matched and were excluded from the county-level and rural/urban splits. This exclusion is not expected to materially change the concentration findings given the sample size, but it means the county-level figures are not a complete census of the $170.1B total.

HHI: computed as the sum of squared percentage shares (0-100 scale, so the ceiling is 10,000 for a pure monopoly) of each lender's share of total gross-approval dollars, computed once nationally and once within each RUCC group. DOJ/FTC Horizontal Merger Guidelines treat HHI below 1,500 as unconcentrated, 1,500-2,500 as moderately concentrated, and above 2,500 as highly concentrated — conventions built for merger review of firms, applied here descriptively to a lending market, not as a regulatory finding.

Gini coefficient: computed across counties' total gross-approval dollars (summed loans per state+county-name group) within each population (all counties matched; metro counties only; nonmetro counties only), using the standard Lorenz-curve definition (0 = perfectly even, 1 = one unit holds everything).

Nonmetro counties have fewer distinct SBA lenders in absolute terms (1,649 vs. 2,838), which might suggest less lender choice for a rural borrower — but their HHI is nevertheless slightly LOWER than metro (92 vs. 98), meaning the lending that does happen in nonmetro counties is spread slightly more evenly among the lenders present, not more concentrated in a dominant few. This is consistent with the composition of top nonmetro lenders (Live Oak Banking, Huntington National Bank, First Financial Bank, Celtic Bank, Newtek) overlapping heavily with the national top 10, i.e. the same handful of active national SBA lenders serve rural markets too, rather than rural markets being captured by a single regional monopolist.

The geographic-concentration finding is the sharper one: even restricted to nonmetro counties only, dollar volume is still highly unequal (Gini 0.59) — a small number of larger or more commercially active rural counties still receive a disproportionate share of the rural total. But this inequality is less extreme than the corresponding metro-only figure (0.75), where a handful of major metro counties (containing large numbers of bank branches and much higher local GDP, per Federal Reserve and NCRC research on lending drivers) command an outsized share of even the metro total.

How this compares to published research

Federal Reserve small business survey data (Fed Small Business Credit Survey) and NCRC's 'Relationships Matter' branch-location research both find that bank branch presence and county GDP are the two strongest predictors of small business lending volume, and that rural areas systematically have fewer branches per business — consistent with the lower per-capita SBA volume found here for nonmetro counties. NCRC estimates large-bank business lending generates roughly $2.5-3.0 million per branch per year in urban areas versus roughly $1.9 million per branch in rural areas, reinforcing that the gap is a branch-density and local-economy story rather than an SBA-program-specific one.

One industry source (iBusinessFunding, covering FY2025 SBA data specifically) reported that rural borrowers received 43% MORE approved loans than urban borrowers in FY2025 with a larger average loan size — the opposite direction from the five-year, per-capita pattern found here. This is not necessarily a contradiction: that figure appears to be a raw loan-count comparison for a single fiscal year rather than a per-capita, multi-year figure, and SBA loan volume is volatile year to year with programmatic changes (guarantee-fee waivers, rural-initiative pushes) that can shift a single year's mix without changing the five-year structural pattern. This report's own multi-year, population-adjusted calculation should be read as the more durable structural measure; the single-year raw-count figure is flagged here as a genuine point of disagreement in the literature rather than smoothed over.
