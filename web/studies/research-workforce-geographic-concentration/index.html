The US research workforce is highly concentrated geographically — a handful of states dominate, and patent-output concentration rose from 2010 to 2024

BLS OEWS S&E occupations (2025), NSF HERD university R&D (2024), USPTO patent grants by state (2010-2024)

Summary

The US research workforce is highly concentrated geographically, and by the most granular, longest-running measure available (patent output by inventor/assignee state), that concentration increased somewhat from 2010 to 2024 before pulling back slightly from a 2020 peak. On current-year headcount, California alone holds 14% of the nation's science/engineering (S&E) occupational employment and the top 5 states hold 37%; the Gini coefficient of S&E employment across states is 0.56 (BLS OEWS, 2025) and of university R&D spending is 0.58 (NSF HERD, 2024) — both in the 'high concentration' range. The clean multi-year trend line for the workforce itself does not exist in this warehouse (OEWS and HERD are single-year snapshots here), so trend is measured instead through USPTO patent grants by state, which is available every year 2010-2025: the state-level Gini coefficient of patent output rose from 0.696 in 2010 to a peak of 0.721 in 2020, settling at 0.712 in 2024 — a real but modest rise in concentration, not a large one. California's own share of US patent output rose more sharply, from about 25% (2010) to roughly 32% (2024), even though the combined top-5-state share barely moved (53% to 55%). This means the increase in concentration is being driven by California pulling further ahead of the pack, not by the top tier as a whole gaining ground.

How concentrated is it today

Two independent current-year measures of the research workforce and its inputs both land in the same range:

  • S&E occupational employment (BLS OEWS, 2025) — architecture/engineering, life/physical/social science, and software-developer occupations, summed across all industries. State-level Gini = 0.556. California alone: 818,690 workers (14.1% of the US total of 5.80M). Top 5 states (CA, TX, NY, FL, WA) hold 37.0% of the total; top 10 hold 51.1%.
  • University R&D expenditure (NSF HERD, 2024) — the dollar input side of the research enterprise. State-level Gini = 0.575. California: $14.4B of $117.6B total (12.2%). Top 5 states (CA, NY, TX, MD, PA) hold 39.3%.

Both land in the 'high concentration' band on a 0-1 Gini scale where 0 is perfectly even across all 51 units and 1 is one state holding everything. For comparison, a Gini around 0.4-0.45 is roughly where total US personal income sits across states (population-driven, not concentrated); 0.55-0.58 for a headcount/spending measure indicates real agglomeration, consistent with the published NSF finding that California, Texas, and New York together account for more than a quarter of all S&E employment nationally.

How much has that changed — the trend

Neither BLS OEWS (state x SOC occupation, loaded for 2025 only in this connector) nor NSF HERD (state x year R&D dollars, loaded for 2024 only) carries more than a single year here, so a workforce-level Gini trend cannot be computed directly from this warehouse. As a documented substitution, we use USPTO patent grants attributed to each patent's primary assignee's state — a genuine research/innovation-output proxy with full annual coverage 2010-2025 — to measure how concentration has moved over time.

Exclusion disclosed: every state-level count and Gini figure in the table below was computed from SQL that filtered with the predicate pl.state_fips IS NOT NULL (combined with country_code = 'US'). That predicate removes every patent whose primary assignee's location record has no US state — in practice, overwhelmingly patents whose assignee is a foreign company or individual with no US state to begin with. This is roughly half of all USPTO grants in every year measured: of 206,052 total grants in 2010, 106,704 were removed by pl.state_fips IS NOT NULL failing (99,348 remained); of 311,558 total grants in 2024, 170,492 were removed the same way (141,066 remained). Removing these rows is the correct scope for a 'geographic concentration across US states' question — a patent with no US state cannot appear in a state-level Gini — but it means every figure below describes the geography of US-assigned patents only, not worldwide patenting activity, and is silent on any trend in the (excluded) foreign-assigned share.

Two things worth separating: the overall Gini rose modestly (+0.016 net, 2010 to 2024, with a higher 2020 peak of 0.721), moving the distribution from the 'high' concentration band toward the boundary of 'extreme' — but the top-5-state share barely moved (53.2% to 54.7%). Reconciling those two facts: California's own share of the total climbed from about a quarter to nearly a third of all US-assigned patents, while the rest of the top-5 (TX, NY, WA/MA rotating) roughly held steady or gave back share. So the rise in concentration over this window is a story about California specifically pulling further ahead, not the top tier broadly gaining at the expense of everyone else equally — a distinction a bare top-N share would have hidden.

Corroboration from the published literature

This direction — persistent, historically high concentration with a further tilt toward a small number of coastal hubs over the 2010s — matches independent published research using entirely different data and methods, which strengthens confidence in the warehouse-computed trend rather than merely repeating it:

  • NSF's own Science & Engineering Indicators reports California, Texas, and New York together account for more than one-quarter of all US S&E employment, and the 20 largest metro areas for S&E employment hold 42% of the nationwide total (vs. ~31% for all occupations) — both consistent with the state-level shares computed here.
  • Brookings' innovation-sector research found that just five metro areas (Boston, San Francisco, San Jose, Seattle, San Diego) accounted for more than 90% of the nation's innovation-employment growth from 2005 to 2017, raising their combined national share from 17.6% to 22.8%, while the bottom 90% of metro areas lost share outright.
  • Brookings' tech-employment tracking separately found 8 metro areas' share of national tech jobs rose from 35.2% (2010) to 38.2% (2019).

All three external findings describe the same phenomenon this analysis found in patent data: concentration was already high in 2010 and increased somewhat further through roughly 2019-2020, driven by a small set of already-leading hubs rather than broad-based top-tier growth.

Caveats and what was not directly measurable

  • No single table carries a multi-year, headcount-grain research-workforce series. BLS OEWS state x occupation employment is loaded for 2025 only and NSF HERD state x year R&D expenditure for 2024 only in this connector, so the 'how much has it changed' half of the question could not be answered from the workforce measure itself — patent-grant geography was substituted as the best available annual proxy for research/innovation output, and that substitution is disclosed rather than presented as a workforce headcount trend.
  • Foreign-assigned patents excluded by construction (see disclosure above): the predicate pl.state_fips IS NOT NULL (with country_code='US') removed roughly half of all USPTO grants each year — 106,704 of 206,052 in 2010, 170,492 of 311,558 in 2024 — because those patents have no US state at all. Correct scope for this question, but it means the figures are silent on trends in foreign-assigned patenting.
  • Patent geography reflects assignee (usually the employer/company) location, not necessarily where R&D staff physically work, and skews toward corporate/commercial R&D — it under-represents academic and government research that does not result in patents.
  • All Gini/share figures include DC; the 50-states-+-DC set was used (a handful of US territory rows present in the raw patent data were immaterial to the shares reported).
  • 2020's Gini spike (0.721) coincides with the pandemic-era surge in patent grants generally (164,457, the highest year in the series) — this analysis does not decompose how much of that spike is pandemic-specific tech-sector dynamics versus a durable trend; 2024's value (0.712) is closer to 2015's, suggesting some reversion.

WITH primary_assignee AS (
  SELECT patent_id, location_id FROM patents.patent_assignees WHERE assignee_sequence = 0
),
by_state AS (
  SELECT pg.grant_year AS "year", pl.state_fips, COUNT(*) AS cnt
  FROM patents.patent_grants pg
  JOIN primary_assignee pa ON pg.patent_id = pa.patent_id
  JOIN patents.patent_locations pl ON pa.location_id = pl.location_id
  WHERE pg.grant_year IN (2010,2015,2020,2024) AND pl.country_code='US' AND pl.state_fips IS NOT NULL
  GROUP BY pg.grant_year, pl.state_fips
),
ranked AS (
  SELECT "year", state_fips, cnt, SUM(cnt) OVER (PARTITION BY "year") AS total,
    ROW_NUMBER() OVER (PARTITION BY "year" ORDER BY cnt DESC) AS rk
  FROM by_state
)
SELECT "year", SUM(cnt) AS top5_sum, MAX(total) AS total, SUM(cnt)*1.0/MAX(total) AS top5_share
FROM ranked WHERE rk <= 5
GROUP BY "year" ORDER BY "year"
