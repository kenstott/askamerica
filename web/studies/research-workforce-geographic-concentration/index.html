The US Research Workforce Is Highly Geographically Concentrated — and Concentration Has Edged Up Since 2008

A handful of coastal and mid-Atlantic states — led by California — hold a growing share of the nation's R&D jobs

Summary

The US research workforce is highly concentrated geographically, and that concentration has increased modestly since the 2008 financial crisis. Using BLS QCEW employment in NAICS 5417 (Scientific Research and Development Services) as a direct, measurable proxy for the research workforce, the 51-unit Gini coefficient across states rose from 0.642 in 2008 to 0.656 in 2024 (peaking around 0.663 in 2020). California alone held 18.8% of the sector in 2008 and 20.2% in 2024 — roughly a fifth of all US scientific R&D jobs in one state, more than the bottom 30 states combined. The top 5 states (California, Massachusetts, New York, Pennsylvania, Maryland) held 43.9% of the sector in 2008 and 47.5-48.9% for most of the 2016-2024 period. Independent NSF/NCSES and Brookings research on the broader science-and-engineering workforce and innovation-sector jobs corroborates this: concentration is real, large, and — by most measures researchers have tracked — has been rising, not falling, over the past two decades.

What 'research workforce' means here, and why

There is no single official definition of "the research workforce." Three measurable operationalizations exist, each capturing a different slice, and this report uses the first as its primary computed measure while cross-checking against the other two from the literature:

  • Industry-based (computed here): BLS QCEW employment in NAICS 5417, "Scientific Research and Development Services" — establishments whose primary business IS to conduct R&D for others or for their own account. This is the cleanest single-industry proxy for "people whose job is research," available annually by state from 2008 to 2025 in this connector's data (BLS QCEW, ~1-year publication lag).
  • Occupation-based (from NSF/NCSES, cited): everyone in a science/engineering occupation (SOC codes for engineers, computer/math/life/physical/social scientists, and postsecondary S&E teachers) regardless of what industry employs them — a much larger population (~6.9 million people, 4.8% of the US workforce, per NSF/NCSES data reported by SSTI for 2017) that includes, e.g., a software engineer at a bank.
  • Innovation-sector-based (from Brookings, cited): a broader industry cut ("innovation industries") built around R&D intensity and STEM-worker share, used in Brookings' "superstar cities" work.

These three lenses disagree on the exact number but agree on the pattern: geographic concentration is high and has been increasing.

An HHI computed on the same state shares was 671 in 2008 and 753 in 2024 (both well above the 250 threshold the FTC/DOJ treat as "highly concentrated" market structure, applied here descriptively to a workforce rather than a market). California alone (186,784 jobs in 2024) employs more R&D-services workers than the bottom 30 states combined. From 2008 to 2024, California's R&D-services employment grew 61% while the rest of the country grew 47% combined — California is not just large, it is still pulling ahead. The rise in concentration was not monotonic: Gini peaked around 2020 (0.663) and eased slightly to 0.656 by 2024, as fast growth in New Mexico, North Carolina, and other 2nd-tier R&D states (partly national-lab and semiconductor-driven) offset some of the coastal pull. This nonmonotonicity is itself notable — a single before/after comparison would have overstated the recent trend.

Finding 2: The occupation-based (NSF/NCSES) measure tells the same story

NSF's National Center for Science and Engineering Statistics (NCSES) has published the most cited work specifically on this question. Its 2013 report "Regional Concentrations of Scientists and Engineers in the United States" found California, Texas, and New York together held more than one-fourth of all US S&E employment, with California's Santa Clara, Los Angeles, and San Diego alone accounting for roughly 1 in 10 S&E jobs nationwide. A follow-up analysis of NSF/NCSES data by SSTI (covering 2003-2017) found the sector especially concentrated by workforce SHARE (not just raw count) around Washington, DC (10.8% of DC's total workforce is in S&E occupations — the highest of any jurisdiction), Maryland (7.6%), Virginia (7.5%), Washington state (7.4%), and Colorado (7.1%) — versus a national average around 4.8%. Notably, that report also found the S&E SHARE of the workforce rose in every single state from 2003-2017, with the fastest increases in already-concentrated places (DC +1.6 points, Maryland +1.5, Virginia +1.3, Washington +1.5) — meaning the places that started most research-intensive became relatively MORE so, a within-state confirmation of the same concentrating trend found in the QCEW data above.

NSF's 2024 Science & Engineering Indicators similarly documents extreme sub-state clustering: nationally 21.6% of workers hold a STEM occupation, but in three Public Use Microdata Areas within the San Jose-Sunnyvale-Santa Clara, CA metro (Silicon Valley) that figure reaches about 45% — roughly double the national rate — while several PUMAs in the Bronx and Laredo, TX sit at 12% or below. Concentration is not just state-level; it intensifies further within states, down to specific metro clusters.

Finding 3: The broader 'innovation sector' has concentrated even faster (Brookings)

Brookings Metro's research on "innovation industries" (R&D-intensive sectors with high STEM-worker shares — a wider net than NAICS 5417 alone) found an even starker trend at the metro level: from 2005-2017, over 90% of the NET NEW innovation-sector jobs in the entire country were created in just five "superstar" metros — San Francisco, San Jose, Seattle, San Diego, and Boston — which together raised their combined share of national innovation employment from 17.6% to 22.8%. Meanwhile the other 90% of US metro areas (343 of them) LOST share even where their own innovation-sector employment grew in absolute terms. A 2020 follow-up using "digital services" industries specifically found the same or worse pattern continuing: the top 10 metro areas held 44.3% of the sector in 2018 and captured 47.9% of all NEW tech jobs added 2010-2018, with San Francisco and San Jose alone adding roughly 200,000 digital-services jobs in the decade and now holding about 11% of the national total, up from 7.5% in 2010.

Synthesis: what changed, and by how much

Every measure available — this connector's own QCEW-based Gini/HHI/share computation, NSF/NCSES's occupation-share data, and Brookings' innovation/digital-services metro data — points the same direction: the US research workforce started already concentrated (a small number of states/metros held an outsized share as far back as the early 2000s) and has become MORE concentrated since, though the QCEW-based state Gini shows this leveling off after roughly 2020 rather than continuing to climb. The magnitude differs by measure — a Gini shift of +0.014 (QCEW, state grain, 2008-2024) is a modest absolute move; a rise from 17.6% to 22.8% in 5 metro areas' innovation-job share (Brookings, metro grain, 2005-2017) is a much larger relative shift — reflecting that concentration is sharper and has moved faster at the metro/city grain than the state grain, consistent with agglomeration (talent, capital, and firms clustering in specific cities like San Francisco/San Jose/Boston/DC) being the underlying mechanism rather than anything that shows up cleanly as a state-level phenomenon.
