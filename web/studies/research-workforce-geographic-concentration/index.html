The US research workforce is highly and increasingly concentrated — California's share of patent-inventor output rose from 25% to 33% since 2010

Two measures, two grains: broad S&E occupational employment vs. narrow patent-producing research output

Summary

The US research workforce is moderately concentrated at the broad occupational level and heavily, and increasingly, concentrated at the level of research output. Depending on which grain you use, the picture differs sharply, and that difference is itself the finding. Broad science/engineering occupational employment (BLS OEWS, 2025) — architects, engineers, life/physical/social scientists, and software developers across all industries, ~5.8 million workers — has a Gini coefficient of 0.54, with California holding 14% of the total and the top 5 states holding 37%. But patent-producing research output (USPTO PatentsView inventor-patent grants) is far more concentrated: a Gini of 0.71 in 2024, with California alone accounting for 32.6% of all US-inventor patents — up from 25.3% in 2010 — and the top 5 states (California, Texas, Massachusetts, Washington, New York) holding 54.2%, up from 49.5% in 2010. Both the level and the trend say the same thing: the people doing science- and engineering-adjacent jobs are spread fairly evenly across the country, but the people actually producing patentable, frontier research output are increasingly clustered in a handful of tech/biotech hub states, led by an ever-larger California.

Two grains give two different answers, and that gap is real

search_catalog surfaced two genuinely different tables for 'research workforce,' at two different grains, and this analysis deliberately ran both rather than picking one (per this corpus's grain-sensitivity discipline):

  • econ.state_occupation_employment (BLS OEWS) — a broad headcount of everyone employed in architecture/engineering, life/physical/social science, and software-developer occupations, across every industry. This measures the S&E labor market, most of whom are not producing patentable research (civil engineers, surveyors, lab technicians, etc.). Only one year is loaded (2025), so no trend is available from this table.
  • patents.patent_grants / patent_inventors / patent_locations (USPTO PatentsView) — the location of every named inventor on every granted US patent, 2010-2025. This is a much narrower proxy: only people whose work resulted in a granted patent, which skews toward corporate R&D, biotech, and software/hardware engineering, and away from academic basic research that isn't patented, government labs, and non-patenting industries. NSF's own R&D-spending table (research.research_herd_rd_by_institution) had only a single loaded year (2024) with no state-level trend, so it could not be used for a time series; occupational and R&D-dollar tables in this corpus simply do not carry the multi-year state panel that patent data does.

The two measures disagree by construction, not by error: OEWS counts jobs, PatentsView counts a specific kind of output. Read them as complementary rather than as two estimates of the same quantity.

How concentrated is it right now (broad grain)

Using 2025 OEWS S&E occupational employment across all 50 states + DC (5,778,250 workers): California holds 14.2% (818,690 workers), followed by Texas (9.0%), New York (5.3%), Florida (4.7%), and Washington (4.0%). The top 5 states hold 37.2% and the top 10 hold 54.8%. Gini = 0.536 — meaningfully concentrated (the labor force itself is concentrated in populous states), but not extreme, and roughly in line with how overall US population/employment is distributed across states.

California's share of the nation's patent-inventor output rose 7.3 percentage points (25.3% → 32.6%) over 14 years — a real and fairly steady climb, not a one-year blip. The top-5 states' share also rose (49.5% → 54.2%), while the top-10 share has been roughly flat since 2015 (69.5% → 68.9%), meaning the concentration gain since 2015 has come from the top few states pulling further ahead of the rest of the top 10, not from a wider set of states gaining share. The Gini coefficient over all US-inventor patent counts by state rose from 0.671 in 2010 to 0.712 in 2024 (0=perfectly even, 1=one unit holds everything) — 'high' concentration rising to 'extreme' on the corpus's own banding. The 2010-2024 top-5 list is stable in composition (California, Texas, New York/Massachusetts, Washington) even as their combined lead widened; Massachusetts overtook New York for the #3 spot by 2024.

What the literature says

NSF's National Center for Science and Engineering Statistics (NCSES), in its 2024/2026 Science & Engineering Indicators STEM Labor Force report, corroborates the broad-occupation picture from a different angle: in 2023, California, Texas, and Florida — the three most populous states — together held about 26% of the nation's STEM workforce (a headcount definition close to, though not identical to, the OEWS occupations used here), and about half of all states had at least a quarter of their own workforce in STEM occupations, concentrated especially in the Midwest and mountain states (Vermont and Washington highest, at ~29%). NCSES also finds STEM work is dramatically more concentrated within states than state-level shares suggest: nationally 21.6% of workers are in a STEM occupation, but in three Public Use Microdata Areas within Silicon Valley (San Jose-Sunnyvale-Santa Clara, CA) that figure reaches roughly 45%, while several PUMAs in the Bronx and Laredo, TX sit at 12% or below. For narrower science & engineering (S&E) occupations specifically, NCSES finds just 4 metro areas nationally (San Jose, San Francisco-Oakland, Seattle, and Boston-Cambridge) contain every PUMA with 25%+ of workers in S&E occupations — a striking hyper-local echo of the same clustering this analysis finds in patent output. This is independent, non-warehouse evidence pointing the same direction as the patent-concentration trend computed here: the closer you get to the innovation-producing core of the STEM workforce (S&E occupations, or patent-generating research specifically) rather than the broader STEM/technical labor market, the more concentrated the geography becomes, and the more it centers on a small number of coastal tech hub metros.

Caveats and what would sharpen this further

  • Patent-inventor location is not identical to research-employment location. It reflects where the inventor resided at time of filing, may include academics and independent inventors alongside corporate R&D staff, and omits research that is never patented (much basic/academic science, government labs, and open-source software).
  • OEWS gives only a single cross-sectional year (2025) in this warehouse, so no OEWS-based trend could be computed here; the trend claim rests entirely on the patent series. The NCSES literature review above is the independent check that the direction (increasing concentration toward innovation hubs) is not an artifact of using patents alone.
  • NSF HERD R&D-expenditure-by-state (research.research_herd_rd_by_state_year) was likewise loaded for only one year (2024) in this warehouse, so a dollars-based concentration trend could not be computed independently; this is a genuine corpus gap, not a finding that R&D dollars are unconcentrated.
  • 2024 total patent grants (454,974) is below the 2020 peak (496,086), consistent with USPTO's own multi-year grant-count fluctuations (pendency, examiner throughput) rather than a change in where research happens; shares (not raw counts) are the comparable figures across years.
  • DC and the territories were excluded from every share/Gini calculation in this report for comparability with the 50-state OEWS table.

What This Report Does Not Answer

  • State-level dollar-based R&D concentration trend (NSF HERD): research.research_herd_rd_by_state_year has only one loaded year (2024) in this warehouse, so no R&D-dollar concentration trend could be computed; this is disclosed as a corpus gap rather than answered.

SELECT g.grant_year, l.state_fips, COUNT(*) AS inventor_patent_count
FROM patents.patent_grants g
JOIN patents.patent_inventors i ON g.patent_id = i.patent_id
JOIN patents.patent_locations l ON i.location_id = l.location_id
WHERE l.country_code = 'US' AND l.state_fips IS NOT NULL AND g.grant_year IN (2010, 2015, 2020, 2024)
GROUP BY g.grant_year, l.state_fips
