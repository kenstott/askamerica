Rural America's mortality disadvantage has roughly tripled since the late 1990s, and the gap is still widening for most major causes of death

CDC/NCHS, USDA ERS, and USC Schaeffer literature, cross-checked against AskAmerica-computed rural/urban physician-supply, hospital-quality, and poverty figures

Summary

Rural Americans die younger and get sicker than urban Americans, and the gap has been widening for about 25 years, not narrowing. The clearest single number: the all-ages, age-adjusted death rate was only 7% higher in rural (nonmetropolitan) than urban (metropolitan) counties in 1999; by 2019 it was 20% higher (CDC/NCHS). Among working-age adults (25-54), the gap in natural-cause mortality grew even faster — from about 6% higher in rural areas in the late 1990s to 43% higher by 2019 (USDA Economic Research Service). A 60-year-old rural man today can expect to live about 2 fewer years than his urban counterpart, up sharply from two decades ago; the rural-urban gap for women is smaller (about 6 months) and has grown less. CDC's most recent multi-cause tracking (2010-2022) shows the rural-urban gap widening further for cancer, heart disease, stroke, and chronic lower respiratory disease, while narrowing only for unintentional injury — because injury deaths (mostly overdoses) rose sharply in cities. Behind the outcomes gap sits a widening access-and-resources gap that AskAmerica's own tables confirm directly: rural counties have 35% fewer primary-care physicians and 63% fewer physicians of any kind per capita than urban counties, lower-rated hospitals on average, and higher poverty and disability rates — while rural hospitals and obstetric units have been closing at an accelerating pace.

1. How large is the rural-urban health gap today

  • All-cause mortality: In 2021 the U.S. all-cause age-adjusted death rate was 841.6 per 100,000; rural counties have run persistently above the national rate, and the rural/urban gap was 20 percentage points as of 2019 (CDC/NCHS, cited in García et al., MMWR Surveillance Summaries, 2024).
  • Working-age natural-cause mortality (ages 25-54): 43% higher in rural areas in 2019 (USDA ERS, The Nature of the Rural-Urban Mortality Gap, EIB-265, 2024).
  • Life expectancy / healthy life expectancy at age 60: Urban men can expect 22.9 more years of life versus 20.9 for rural men (a ~2-year gap); urban women 25.6 vs. rural women 25.0 (a 6-month gap). Quality-adjusted life expectancy (QALE) gaps are even larger — 17.5 vs. 15.7 years for men, 19.3 vs. 18.7 for women (USC Schaeffer Center / Journal of Rural Health, Chapel et al., 2024-2025, using Health and Retirement Study data).
  • Suicide: 28.7 per 100,000 in rural counties vs. 20.2 in urban counties (39 high-burden states, 2019-2023) — rural rates roughly 42% higher.
  • Infant mortality: Consistently higher in rural counties than in large metro counties every year 2014-2023 (CDC NCHS Data Brief 534); the gap has been persistent rather than closing even as overall infant mortality fell through 2020.
  • Maternal mortality / obstetric access: More than half of rural U.S. counties lack hospital-based obstetric services; a 2011-2023 accounting found 293 rural hospitals (24% of all rural OB units) stopped delivering babies, with 51 more units closing in 2024-2025 alone — 2.6 million women of reproductive age now live in rural counties with no hospital-based obstetric care.
  • Preventable premature death (deaths before age 80 that exceed the 3-best-states benchmark): In every year 2010-2022, the most rural ("noncore") counties had the highest share of preventable premature deaths from the five leading causes — about 69% of premature deaths in noncore/micropolitan counties were "preventable" by this benchmark in 2022 (CDC, MMWR Surveillance Summaries 73(2), García et al. 2024).

2. How much has the gap changed over time

Every major published time series points the same direction: widening, not narrowing, over roughly the last 25 years, though the pace and even direction differs by specific cause:

  • 1999→2019 (all ages): rural excess mortality grew from 7% to 20% (CDC/NCHS).
  • 1999→2019 (ages 25-54, natural causes): rural excess mortality grew from ~6% to 43% — more than a sevenfold increase in the size of the gap — driven by both rising rural rates and falling urban rates over the period (USDA ERS EIB-265). The report specifically flags that rapid growth in early deaths among rural women from treatable/preventable disease is a major driver of the widening.
  • 2010→2022 (preventable premature death, 5 leading causes): the rural-urban gap widened for 4 of 5 causes — cancer, heart disease, chronic lower respiratory disease, and stroke — and narrowed only for unintentional injury, because injury deaths rose fastest in large urban counties (+147.5% in large central metro vs. milder rural increases), not because rural injury mortality improved. Heart-disease preventable deaths rose most in the most rural (noncore, +9.5%) and micropolitan (+9.1%) counties while falling sharply (-10.2%) in large central metros (CDC MMWR SS7302a1, 2024).
  • 1994-2000 cohort → 2014-2020 cohort (life expectancy/QALE at 60): the urban-rural gap "grew substantially for men" and the rural male QALE disadvantage "more than doubled" over the two decades; the female gap grew much less (USC Schaeffer / Journal of Rural Health, 2024-2025).
  • Overdose deaths — a partial exception: the rural-urban overdose gap has narrowed and in some categories reversed since the mid-2010s as fentanyl-driven urban overdose deaths surged. In 2020, synthetic-opioid death rates were actually higher in urban counties (18.3 per 100,000) than rural (14.3); natural/semisynthetic opioid deaths, which had been higher in rural areas through the mid-2000s–2017, converged with urban rates by 2018-2019 (CDC NCHS Data Brief 440). This is the one leading indicator moving toward convergence rather than divergence, and it is arguably a story of urban deterioration rather than rural improvement.

Two independent primary sources (CDC/NCHS all-ages and USDA ERS working-age) agree on both the direction and rough decade-scale magnitude of the widening even though they measure different populations and cause sets — that agreement is a meaningful cross-check on the finding, not just one study's framing.

3. What the connector adds: quantifying the access and resource gap directly

AskAmerica's county-grain rural/urban mortality tables (health.chr_premature_death, health.cdc_county_injury_mortality, health.cdc_county_overdose_deaths) were tested directly for this question and found to be broken — chr_premature_death fails with an S3 404 on every read, and the two CDC injury/overdose tables return exactly one all-NULL row despite a fully-populated declared schema. Both were reported as data-quality issues via report_issue during this session, and a mid-session update_schema rebuild did not resolve chr_premature_death. That means the connector could not directly reproduce the county-grain premature-death (YPLL) trend by rurality that would have been the strongest possible native check on the literature figures above; the mortality-gap numbers in sections 1-2 are therefore drawn from CDC/NCHS, USDA ERS, and peer-reviewed literature rather than computed here.

What AskAmerica's health and census tables COULD compute directly — and did, live, for this report — is the resource and socioeconomic gradient that plausibly drives the outcomes gap:

  • Primary-care physicians per 100,000 population: 50.3 in nonmetro (rural) counties vs. 77.9 in metro (urban) counties — rural counties have 35% fewer primary-care physicians per capita (HRSA Area Health Resources File, joined to USDA RUCC; 1,971 rural vs. 1,252 urban counties, latest available AHRF release).
  • All active physicians per 100,000: 131.6 rural vs. 358.3 urban — rural counties have 63% fewer physicians of any specialty per capita.
  • Hospital quality: CMS overall star rating averages 3.03 across 908 rural hospitals vs. 3.28 across 2,241 urban hospitals — a real but comparatively modest gap next to the physician-supply gap, though it does not capture the fact that many rural counties have no hospital at all.
  • Poverty rate: 15.4% in rural counties vs. 12.3% in urban counties (ACS 2023 5-year estimates, 3.1 percentage-point gap).
  • Disability rate: 17.0% rural vs. 12.5% urban (4.5 percentage-point gap) — consistent with an older, sicker rural population base that itself partly explains higher crude mortality, though the ERS and CDC studies above are age-adjusted and still find a large and growing gap after accounting for that.

These are not proof of causation, but they are directionally exactly what the literature's stated mechanisms predict (worse access to care, higher poverty, an aging population left behind by out-migration of working-age adults) and they are measured independently of the mortality literature, which strengthens rather than merely restates the qualitative story usually told about "why."

4. Caveats

  • The 1999-2019 and 2010-2022 figures are the most recent full-series numbers available from CDC/NCHS and CDC MMWR respectively; 2023-2025 detailed rural/urban mortality breakdowns were not located as fully tabulated series during this research and are not claimed here.
  • "Rural" is operationalized differently across sources: CDC/NCHS uses its 6-category 2013 urban-rural county classification (collapsed to metro/nonmetro for the widely cited headline figures), USDA ERS uses metro/nonmetro, and the AskAmerica computations here use USDA's 9-code Rural-Urban Continuum Code collapsed to RUCC≤3 (metro) vs. RUCC≥4 (nonmetro) for consistency with the literature's metro/nonmetro framing. These definitions are closely related but not identical, and cutpoints matter more for genuinely rural (noncore) vs. suburban (large fringe metro) contrasts than for the broad metro/nonmetro split used throughout this report.
  • The physician-supply, hospital-rating, and poverty figures are a single recent snapshot (not a trend), because AskAmerica's own multi-year rural/urban outcome tables were unusable (see Section 3) — they establish the current SIZE of the access/resource gap, not how it has moved over time.
  • The overdose story is a genuine exception to the general widening pattern and is highlighted rather than smoothed over: it reflects urban fentanyl deaths rising faster than rural ones since ~2018, not rural improvement in an absolute sense.

What This Report Does Not Answer

  • Use AskAmerica connector data, not just literature, wherever the connector can answer the question: The connector's county-grain rural/urban mortality tables (chr_premature_death, cdc_county_injury_mortality, cdc_county_overdose_deaths) were tested and found broken (S3 404 / empty), reported via report_issue. The mortality-gap trend figures therefore come from CDC/NCHS, USDA ERS, and MMWR literature rather than a connector computation. The connector WAS used successfully to compute the physician-supply, hospital-quality, and poverty/disability gap figures (Section 3), which serve as an independent, connector-native check on the mechanisms behind the literature's outcome-gap findings.
