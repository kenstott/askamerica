The map shifts — mobility geography was stable for decades, then moved substantially between the 1978 and 1992 birth cohorts

Opportunity Insights / Census Bureau Opportunity Atlas, birth cohorts 1971–1992; AskAmerica corpus has no intergenerational-mobility table

Summary

Both things are true, at different timescales — and the newest evidence gives a precise answer to "how much" the map moves. County-level mobility outcomes are strongly persistent: the correlation between a county's outcomes for poor children born in 1978 versus 1992 — a single generation, 14 years apart — is about 0.80 for both white and Black children, per the underlying Opportunity Insights study. That is real, substantial persistence, consistent with the historical finding that today's low-mobility counties trace back to 19th-century slavery rates and 1930s redlining. But r=0.80 is not r=1.00: roughly a third of the cross-county variation in outcomes is NOT explained by where that county stood a generation earlier, and the newest county-level trend data shows exactly where the map moved — the coasts and the Great Plains, historically among the best places for a poor child to get ahead, declined sharply; the Southeast and industrial Midwest, historically the worst, improved sharply, especially for Black children. So the honest answer is: the map is a slow-moving one, stable enough to look fixed at a glance and strongly rooted in history, but it does shift meaningfully within a single generation, and the shift is large enough to be the headline finding of the field's own newest research.

The case for “the map stays the same”

Two independent bodies of evidence support persistence:

  • Short/medium-run rank stability (Chetty, Hendren, Kline, Saez, Turner, 2014, AER P&P, and companion QJE papers): using the 1980–82 birth-cohort core sample and linking back to 1971 via dependent-filer tax records, upward mobility (the rank-rank slope of child income on parental income) was found to be close to constant for children born 1971–1993 — commuting zones that were good or bad for mobility in the early 1970s cohorts were still roughly good or bad two decades later. The well-known cross-sectional figure from that era: a child born to bottom-quintile parents had a 4.4% chance of reaching the top quintile in Charlotte, NC vs. 12.9% in San Jose, CA.
  • Deep historical persistence, now quantified as county-level correlation: Berger (2018, Demography), "Places of Persistence," found commuting zones with higher 1860 slavery-population shares have measurably lower upward mobility today — the chance a child born in the bottom quintile reaches the top quintile averages about 4% in the highest-slavery-share zones versus over 10% in zones with no recorded slaves in 1860 — and traces the mechanism mainly to family structure (marriage rates, single-parent households) rather than income, schools, or segregation alone. Separately, Aaronson, Faber, Hartley, Mazumder, and Sharkey (2021) linked 1930s HOLC redlining maps to place-based mobility outcomes that persist to the present.
  • The direct cohort-to-cohort correlation: the 2024/2025 Opportunity Insights study itself reports that "places that had low mobility for the 1978 cohort generally had low mobility in 1992 as well: the correlation in mean income ranks across counties between children born to low-income parents in the 1978 and 1992 cohorts is around 0.8 for both white and Black children." That is the single most direct answer to "does the map stay the same": mostly yes, but not entirely.

The case for “the map shifts” — and the newest data quantifies how much

The same 2024/2025 Opportunity Insights study (Chetty, Dobbie, Goldman, Porter, Yang, "Changing Opportunity," with the Census Bureau releasing companion county-level trend data as "Module 2" of the Opportunity Atlas) directly compares the 1978 and 1992 birth cohorts — 57 million children — and finds real geographic re-sorting on top of that 0.8 correlation, not just level shifts:

  • Coasts and Great Plains declined, Southeast/industrial Midwest rose. For white children from low-income families, mobility fell the most in the Great Plains and parts of the coasts — areas that had enjoyed relatively high mobility in 1978. Those same areas fell to levels on par with historically low-mobility Appalachia by 1992. For Black children from low-income families, mobility rose sharply in the Southeast and industrial Midwest — historically the lowest-mobility regions in the country — with only modest change on the coasts.
  • Race and class gaps moved in opposite directions almost everywhere. The Black-white gap in upward mobility shrank roughly 27–30% nationally (two consistent measures in the source materials), driven by a 72–75% drop in the racial gap in the chance a poor child stays poor: from 14.7 points worse for Black children than white children in 1978 to 4.1 points in 1992. At the same time, the gap between white children raised poor vs. rich grew roughly 28–30%. Chances of reaching the very top of the income distribution from a poor family barely moved for either race across the period — the whole shift is concentrated in escaping poverty, not reaching the top.
  • Individual metros diverged sharply from otherwise-similar peers. Charlotte, NC and Atlanta, GA started 1978 alike (low mobility, especially for poor Black children); by 1992 Charlotte had risen to near the national average while Atlanta stayed low. Grand Rapids, MI and Milwaukee, WI were both low-mobility Rust Belt cities in 1978; by 1992 Grand Rapids rose above the national average while Milwaukee fell further. The dashboard chart shows the 1978→1992 % change in adult income for children born poor across the top and bottom five of the 50 largest metros: Brownsville TX (+6.7%) and Austin TX (+6.4%) at the top, Las Vegas NV (-10.6%) and Philadelphia PA (-12.7%) at the bottom, against a national average of -4.2%.
  • The mechanism is the current local community, not fixed history. The study's central causal finding, using a quasi-experimental movers design, is that these changes track changes in parental employment rates within a child's own community (defined by county, race, class, and birth-cohort peer group) — not the parents' own individual circumstances, and not old structural conditions replaying themselves. Roughly 90% of the mobility gaps by race and class are explained by these shifts in "community conditions," and the effect operates mainly through social interaction with same-grade, same-race, same-class peers rather than through school funding or other economic resources. That is a mechanism that can move within a decade or two — which is exactly what the county correlation of 0.8, rather than 1.0, shows happened.

Reconciling the two findings

These are not contradictory once the timescales and the actual quantitative claim are separated. The 2014 "close to constant" finding covered cohorts born 1971–1993 using data collected mostly through the mid-2010s and was, in practice, largely anchored to the 1980–82 core cohort against its own recent history — a single-era snapshot with limited cross-generation comparison. The 2024/2025 study is the first to directly compare a 1978 cohort against a 1992 cohort at county grain using genuinely new, independently measured outcome data, and its own reported number — a 0.8 county-level correlation, not 1.0 — is itself the quantitative version of "strong persistence, but real movement." The historical-persistence papers (slavery, redlining) explain why the starting level of opportunity in a place has deep roots; they are not incompatible with that same place's ranking moving up or down over a generation for reasons tied to present-day economic and social conditions (here, parental employment rates) rather than history mechanically repeating. In the authors' own framing, opportunity is "malleable in short time frames" even though it is also, on average, persistent. In short: the map is not the same generation after generation, but it is not reshuffled either — it moves by a magnitude between "stays put" and "random," and the newest research identifies the specific regions and the specific present-day driver of that movement.
