Generating capacity additions have outpaced EV charging capacity additions roughly 35-to-1 nationally, 2018-2023

EIA-860 generator inventory vs. NREL AFDC public charging-station registry, aggregated by state

Summary

Between 2018 and 2023, U.S. states added roughly 205,500 MW of new electricity generating capacity (EIA-860 nameplate capacity of generators that entered service in that window) but only an estimated 5,700 MW-equivalent of public EV charging capacity (converting AFDC's counts of newly-opened DC fast, Level 2, and Level 1 ports to power using typical charger ratings). Charging capacity added is therefore only about 2.8% the size of generating capacity added, nationally. In absolute megawatts the gap is largest in the biggest states with the biggest generation buildouts — Texas (~40,900 MW gap, mostly wind and gas), California (~19,200 MW), and Florida (~15,900 MW) — but that ranking mostly reflects state size. Looking at the ratio of charging-to-generation growth instead, the states where charging investment lags generation investment MOST, proportionally, are smaller, lower-EV-adoption, generation-heavy states: South Dakota (0.44%), Wyoming (0.53%), Iowa (0.70%), Texas (0.82%), New Mexico (0.88%), and Nebraska (0.89%) — states that built enormous new wind/gas capacity with comparatively little new public charging infrastructure. States where charging kept better relative pace include the District of Columbia, New Hampshire, Delaware, Vermont, and Washington — small states that added little new generation, so even modest charger buildout looks proportionally large.

Why generating and charging capacity aren't directly comparable, and what this analysis does about it

"Charging capacity" and "generating capacity" are not natively the same unit in the data available here. EIA-860 (energy.eia_power_plants) reports true nameplate MW for every generator. NREL's Alternative Fuels Data Center (AFDC) station registry (energy.ev_charging_stations), by contrast, reports only port counts by charging level (DC fast, Level 2, Level 1) — it does not carry the kW rating of individual chargers. To make an MW-comparable estimate, this analysis multiplied each port type by an industry-typical power rating: 150 kW per DC fast port, 7.2 kW per Level 2 port, and 1.4 kW per Level 1 port. This is a rough, order-of-magnitude proxy, not a measured or EIA-published figure — DC fast chargers installed in this window ranged from 50 kW to 350 kW depending on vintage and network, so the true figure could plausibly be higher or lower by a factor of ~2 for the DC-fast component specifically, though it would not change the broad conclusion (charging capacity added is a small fraction of generating capacity added) by an order of magnitude.

The comparison window (2018-2023) was chosen because energy.eia_power_plants' observed data coverage runs 2012-2023 (EIA-860 publishes with roughly a 3-year lag, so 2024-2026 do not exist in this table yet), and AFDC station open-date records are populated well within that same window. Generating additions are gross additions — new generators entering service — not netted against retirements in the same period, since the question asked what states "added," not net capacity change.

Method

Generating capacity added: distinct (plant_id, generator_id) rows from energy.eia_power_plants (report_year=2023 snapshot, so each generator counted once) with operating_year between 2018 and 2023, summed nameplate_capacity_mw by state_abbr.

Charging capacity added: energy.ev_charging_stations filtered to country='US', status_code='E' (available stations), open_date year between 2018 and 2023, summed ev_dc_fast_num, ev_level2_evse_num, ev_level1_evse_num ports by state_abbr, then converted to MW-equivalent using the ratings above.

Gap = generating MW added minus charging MW-equivalent added. Ratio = charging MW-equivalent added divided by generating MW added, as a percentage.

Where the gap is biggest

  • In absolute megawatts: Texas, California, Florida, Pennsylvania, and Illinois — these are simply large states that also happened to add enormous generating capacity (Texas alone added over 41 GW, mostly wind and gas, more than 10x the next state). This ranking is driven by scale, not necessarily by charging under-investment specifically.
  • In relative terms (the more meaningful "gap" for policy purposes): South Dakota, Wyoming, Iowa, Texas, New Mexico, Nebraska, Hawaii, and North Dakota show the smallest charging build-out as a share of their generation build-out (all under 1%). These are largely lower-population, lower-EV-adoption states that built out large amounts of wind, gas, or (Hawaii) other generation while adding comparatively few public chargers.
  • Best relative pace: DC, New Hampshire, Delaware, Vermont, and Washington show the highest charging-to-generation ratios (17-45%), but this largely reflects that these states/DC added very little new generation in the period, not that they built unusually large charging networks in absolute terms.

Sources

  1. EIA Form 860 annual generator inventory (energy.eia_power_plants)
    Show SQL
    WITH gen AS (SELECT DISTINCT plant_id, generator_id, state_abbr, operating_year, nameplate_capacity_mw FROM energy.eia_power_plants WHERE report_year = 2023 AND operating_year BETWEEN 2018 AND 2023) SELECT state_abbr, SUM(nameplate_capacity_mw) AS gen_added_mw FROM gen GROUP BY state_abbr ORDER BY gen_added_mw DESC
  2. NREL AFDC public EV charging station registry (energy.ev_charging_stations)
    Show SQL
    SELECT state_abbr, SUM(COALESCE(ev_dc_fast_num,0)) AS dcfast, SUM(COALESCE(ev_level2_evse_num,0)) AS l2, SUM(COALESCE(ev_level1_evse_num,0)) AS l1 FROM energy.ev_charging_stations WHERE country='US' AND status_code='E' AND EXTRACT(YEAR FROM open_date) BETWEEN 2018 AND 2023 GROUP BY state_abbr
  3. AFDC / EV charging infrastructure trend context (2024-2025 growth figures) — Confirms ~180,000 to 242,000+ public charging ports 2024-2025, used as external corroboration of national EV charging growth trend
  4. Utility Dive: charging infrastructure growth lagging demand
  5. Joint Office of Energy and Transportation, Q4 2024 NEVI quarterly update
