Yes, but narrowly: only a handful of America's most import-dependent minerals see real federal-land mining, and the ones that do are recent, embattled, or byproduct-scale

ONRR federal mineral royalty data (FY2004-2025) cross-checked against USGS Mineral Commodity Summaries 2026 import-reliance rankings and current mine-status reporting

Summary

The US is 100% net-import-reliant on about a dozen minerals (arsenic, graphite, manganese, mica, gallium, tantalum, yttrium, and several others) and 50%+ reliant on another dozen (rare earths, cobalt, lithium, nickel, uranium, tin, tungsten, antimony, PGMs, titanium). Checking federal royalty and land-status records against that list finds almost no active federal-land production for the highest-reliance group, and only a few, mostly very recent, active or near-term projects among the 50-90% group: a lithium mine under construction on BLM land in Nevada, a uranium mine that just started producing inside a national forest in Arizona, and a copper deposit whose federal forest land was only transferred to the mining company in March 2026. The one steady, decades-old exception is potash, which is genuinely produced on federal land in New Mexico and generates real royalty revenue every year. Cobalt's only US mine, also on federal (Forest Service) land in Idaho, is built but has sat idle since 2023 on economics, not permitting. For most of the highest-import-reliance minerals, the honest answer is that federal land is not currently where the shortfall is being addressed — either because no active US deposit exists at meaningful scale, or because the deposits that do exist are on private/state land (e.g., Mountain Pass rare earths, California).

Why ONRR royalty data understates hardrock mining on federal land

Federal Office of Natural Resources Revenue (ONRR) data records royalties paid on minerals extracted from federal and tribal land. But most of the highest-import-reliance metals (uranium, copper, lithium, cobalt, rare earths, nickel) are 'locating' minerals under the 1872 Mining Law, which lets a claimant patent and mine them on federal land without paying any federal royalty — so their presence in ONRR data, if any, is incidental (e.g., copper royalties here come from ancillary leases, not the primary 1872-Act mines). By contrast, potash, phosphate, sodium, and oil/gas/coal are 'leasable' minerals under the Mineral Leasing Act and do pay royalty, which is why potash shows up cleanly as a real, ongoing revenue stream while uranium and lithium show essentially nothing in this dataset even though real mines exist.

Total federal-land royalty revenue FY2004-2025 for the minerals queried: Potassium (potash) $1.32B, Copper $98.0M, Lead $599.4M, Zinc $124.5M, and 'Hardrock' (catch-all, mostly gold/silver claim-maintenance fees, not ore royalty) just $20.5M — versus $130.7B for oil & gas pre-production alone over the same period. The contrast in scale is itself evidence that federal hardrock mineral extraction for most critical minerals is either non-royalty-bearing, small, or not happening.

Minerals with real, current federal-land activity

  • Lithium (~25% import-reliant, growing critical-minerals priority): Thacker Pass, Nevada — on BLM public land, construction underway since 2023, first production targeted second half of 2026 (Lithium Americas/GM). The largest known US lithium deposit.
  • Uranium (~95% import-reliant for enriched fuel, ~50%+ for concentrate): Pinyon Plain Mine, inside Kaibab National Forest, Arizona (Forest Service land) — began active mining December 2023, was processing ore through Q4 2025-Q1 2026 (Energy Fuels), one of the highest-grade uranium deposits outside Canada. Notably grandfathered into the Baaj Nwaavjo I'tah Kukveni National Monument that surrounds it.
  • Copper (~40-45% import-reliant): Resolution Copper, Tonto National Forest, Arizona — one of the largest undeveloped copper deposits in the world (Oak Flat). The federal land (~2,500 acres of National Forest) was only transferred out of USFS ownership to Resolution Copper (Rio Tinto/BHP) on March 16, 2026, after a 9th Circuit ruling; mine construction has not yet begun and the transfer remains under further tribal legal challenge (San Carlos Apache Tribe seeking en banc review).
  • Cobalt (~76% import-reliant): Idaho Cobalt Operations, Salmon-Challis National Forest — the only US cobalt mine, built and permitted on federal land, but idled since June 2023 due to low prices; not currently producing, though infill drilling continues.

Minerals where federal land is largely NOT where US supply comes from

For the 100%-import-reliant tier — graphite, manganese, gallium, germanium, arsenic, tantalum, yttrium, scandium, mica, niobium, rubidium, cesium, fluorspar, bismuth, indium — there is no significant active federal-land mine in the current data or news record. Rare earths, the most politically prominent case, are produced at Mountain Pass, California, which is private land, not federal. Where the US does have federal-land potential for these minerals (e.g., graphite exploration in Alaska, vanadium at Gibellini, Nevada on BLM land), projects are mostly still in permitting or pre-construction, not producing.
