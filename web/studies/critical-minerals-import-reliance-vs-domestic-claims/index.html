No — not yet: every 100%-import-reliant mineral has zero active mine production on U.S. federal land, though real construction is underway for several

USGS Mineral Commodity Summaries 2025 import-reliance data, cross-checked against ONRR federal royalty revenue and project-level status as of September 2026

Summary

No. Of the 15 mineral commodities for which the U.S. is 100 percent net-import-reliant (USGS, Mineral Commodity Summaries 2025 — arsenic, asbestos, cesium, fluorspar, gallium, natural graphite, indium, manganese, sheet mica, niobium, rubidium, scandium, strontium, tantalum, and titanium sponge metal), not one is being commercially mined anywhere in the United States today, federal land or otherwise — confirmed both by USGS's own commodity-by-commodity production figures and by zero matching entries in the Interior Department's ONRR federal royalty-revenue records, which show federal hardrock mining revenue currently coming only from lead, copper, zinc, phosphate, potash and soda ash, none of which are on the fully import-reliant list.

That said, the premise that nothing is happening is also wrong. There is genuine, well-financed construction activity on federal (BLM and U.S. Forest Service) land aimed squarely at several of these gaps: an antimony/gold mine under construction on Payette National Forest land in Idaho (targeting 2029), a manganese/zinc project under construction partly on Coronado National Forest land in Arizona (zinc by 2027-28), a lithium mine under active construction on BLM land in Nevada (2027-28), and one federal-land cobalt mine and mill that was actually built and briefly opened in 2022 before being idled for economic reasons. None of these has produced a marketable ton yet. So the honest answer is: real capital and real construction, yes; real output, not yet.

1. Which minerals the US most depends on importing

USGS's Mineral Commodity Summaries 2025 (covering full-year 2024 data) found the U.S. was 100 percent net import reliant for 15 nonfuel mineral commodities: arsenic, asbestos, cesium, fluorspar, gallium, natural graphite, indium, manganese, sheet mica, niobium (columbium), rubidium, scandium, strontium, tantalum, and titanium sponge metal. An additional roughly 20 commodities — including antimony (85%), cobalt (76%), the rare-earth-element group, tungsten, and potash — had net import reliance above 50 percent and are also treated as top-tier supply-chain risks by USGS's own 2025 critical-minerals risk methodology. China and, secondarily, Canada were the leading suppliers across the list.

2. Checking the federal royalty ledger directly

The Interior Department's Office of Natural Resources Revenue (ONRR) records every royalty dollar paid for minerals actually extracted from federal and tribal land, back to FY2004. Querying FY2025 hardrock and non-fuel royalty revenue by commodity turns up exactly these currently-producing categories on federal land: lead, copper and zinc concentrate (Missouri lead belt), phosphate (Idaho), potash/langbeinite (New Mexico), soda ash and sodium products (Wyoming, California), and gilsonite (Utah). None of the 15 fully import-reliant minerals, and none of the >50%-reliant minerals named above, appear anywhere in the federal royalty ledger — the clean, direct confirmation that nothing on the most-import-dependent list is generating federal mining revenue right now.

3. What real activity does exist on federal land

  • Antimony/gold — Stibnite Gold Project, Payette National Forest, Idaho (Perpetua Resources). No antimony has been mined anywhere in the U.S. since the last domestic source closed decades ago; the sole U.S. antimony processor (US Antimony Corp., Montana) only smelts imported feedstock and battery-recycling scrap. Stibnite would be the first primary domestic antimony mine. It received its final Forest Service Record of Decision in 2025, secured a $2.9 billion U.S. Export-Import Bank loan in May 2026, and is under active construction, with production targeted for 2029. The U.S. Department of War has identified it as the only project capable of meeting U.S. defense antimony demand.
  • Manganese/zinc — Hermosa Project, Coronado National Forest, Arizona (South32). Construction is already roughly half complete on the private-land portion; the Forest Service in 2026 issued the Final Mine Plan of Operation for the federal-land component (tailings storage, an 8-mile access road, powerline, and groundwater infiltration basins), making Hermosa the first project fully completed under the federal FAST-41 expedited-permitting program. First zinc product is targeted for 2027-28; the manganese circuit is a later project phase.
  • Lithium — Thacker Pass, BLM land, Humboldt County, Nevada (Lithium Americas). Lithium is not on the 100%-reliant list but is one of the highest-profile import-dependent battery minerals. Thacker Pass received its BLM Record of Decision in January 2021 and is under active Phase 1 construction in 2026 (roughly 900 workers on site, rising toward ~1,800), targeting 40,000 tonnes/year of battery-grade lithium carbonate with mechanical completion in late 2027.
  • Cobalt — Idaho Cobalt Operations, Salmon-Challis National Forest, Idaho (Jervois Global). This is the one case where a mine on federal land was actually built and briefly opened, in September 2022. It was suspended in 2023, before reaching sustained commercial production, because of low cobalt prices and construction-cost inflation; infill drilling continues but the mine remains idled as of 2026.

4. Where the picture is more mixed — minerals moving, but not on federal land

Some of the most-cited critical-mineral projects are not federal-land stories. Graphite One's Graphite Creek deposit in Alaska sits entirely on Alaska state mining claims, not BLM or Forest Service land — the only federal touchpoint is an Army Corps Clean Water Act permit. NioCorp's Elk Creek niobium/scandium/titanium project in Nebraska is on private land the company owns or has under option. MP Materials' Mountain Pass rare-earth mine in California operates on privately held patented mining claims (federal land ownership was extinguished when the claims were patented decades ago), even though its output is central to the same import-dependence story. These are real, some already producing, but they are not evidence of mining on land the federal government owns and administers today.
