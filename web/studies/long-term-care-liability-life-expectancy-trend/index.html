National e(65) rose ~1.7-1.9 years since 2000, but the state-level spread not only persists — it widened in the 2021 COVID shock, which is exactly the tail risk LTC reserving needs to price

CDC/NCHS National Vital Statistics System, U.S. and State Life Tables, 1999-2001 through 2023

Summary

Nationally, life expectancy at age 65, e(65), rose from 17.8 years (1999-2001 average) to 19.5 years in 2023 — a gain of about 1.7 years (+9.6%) over roughly two decades. The path was not smooth: e(65) climbed fairly steadily to about 19.6 years by 2019, then fell sharply to 18.4 in 2021 (the COVID-19 mortality shock), before recovering to 19.5 by 2023 — meaning the entire pandemic-era loss has now been recovered nationally, but only just.

The state-level trend differs by enough to matter for reserving. In the pre-pandemic window (1999-2001 to 2019), state gains in e(65) ranged from +0.26 years (North Dakota) to +2.69 years (DC), a more-than-tenfold spread, with a standard deviation of 0.51 years around a mean gain of 1.04 years. The cross-state range (best state minus worst state) was almost unchanged over that 18-year pre-pandemic window — 3.81 years in 1999-2001 versus 3.7 years in 2019 — so geographic inequality in old-age mortality is not closing on its own. Then the 2021 shock widened that range to 4.5 years and pushed the cross-state standard deviation up about 27% (0.82 to 1.08 years), because Southern states with already-lower e(65) (Mississippi, West Virginia, Alabama, Oklahoma, Kentucky) lost the most ground. For LTC reserving, this combination — a persistent ~4-year gap between the best- and worst-longevity states, plus a demonstrated capacity for that gap to widen abruptly in a mortality-shock year — is exactly the kind of geographic heterogeneity a national assumption set will misprice; a block concentrated in low-e(65) states needs a shorter payout-duration assumption than one concentrated in Hawaii, Connecticut, or the Northeast, and both need to hold capital against the shown 2021-style widening, not just the trend increase.

Two decades ago-to-now (using the 1999-2001 state-report baseline of 17.77 against 2023's 19.5) is a gain of +1.73 years; measured pre-pandemic only (1999-2001 to 2019) the gain was +1.83 years, i.e. essentially all of the two-decade gain had already accrued by 2019 and the pandemic years (2020-2022) were a detour, not new trend. The 2019-to-2021 drop (-1.2 years) is the largest two-year swing in this series and is documented by NCHS as driven mainly by COVID-19 and a rise in unintentional-injury (drug overdose) deaths.

2. Does the state-level trend differ enough to matter

State-level e(65), all computed in Python from the CDC/NCHS state life-table reports: NVSR 60-9 (1999-2001, 50 states + DC), NVSR 70-18 (2019), and NVSR 73-07 (2021).

  • Level, 1999-2001: Kentucky lowest at 16.61 years, Hawaii highest at 20.42 years (range 3.81 years); U.S. total 17.77.
  • Level, 2019 (pre-pandemic): Mississippi lowest at 17.5 years, Hawaii highest at 21.2 years (range 3.7 years); U.S. total 19.6.
  • Level, 2021 (pandemic): Mississippi and West Virginia tied lowest at 16.1 years, Hawaii highest at 20.6 years (range 4.5 years); U.S. total 18.4.
  • 20-year change, 1999-2001 → 2019, all 51 jurisdictions: mean +1.04 years, standard deviation 0.51 years. Smallest gains: North Dakota (+0.26), South Dakota (+0.27), Arkansas (+0.30), Arizona (+0.32), Oklahoma (+0.32). Largest gains: New Jersey (+1.90), Maryland (+1.84), Virginia (+1.77), Vermont (+1.73), and District of Columbia (+2.69, an outlier driven substantially by the decline in HIV/AIDS and violence-related mortality in DC over this period, per NCHS's own note that DC's e(0) gain over the same window "was higher than all of the states").
  • Persistence of state ranking: the Pearson correlation between a state's 1999-2001 e(65) level and its 2019 level, across the 51 jurisdictions, is r = 0.81 — states that started low mostly stayed relatively low. A simple regression of the 20-year change on the 1999-2001 starting level gives a slope of -0.22 (mild convergence: lower-starting states gained a bit more on average), but the fit is weak, and it was fully offset by the 2021 shock, which is documented above to have disproportionately hit several of the same already-low-e(65) Southern states (Mississippi, Alabama, Oklahoma, Kentucky, West Virginia, Tennessee, Arkansas) rather than closing the gap.

3. Why this matters for long-term-care reserving

This is interpretation, not a number pulled from a reserving model this corpus has access to (no actuarial LTC-reserving table exists in askamerica's schemas — see the data-gap note below). Three structural implications follow directly from the figures above:

  1. The state gap is a standing ~3.7-4.5-year spread in remaining life at 65, not a rounding error. A national valuation assumption applied uniformly to a geographically concentrated LTC block (e.g., a block written predominantly in the Deep South versus one concentrated in the Northeast/Hawaii/California) will be biased in a predictable direction: understating claim duration and reserves for the higher-e(65) block, overstating them for the lower-e(65) block.
  2. The gap has not been closing on its own. Comparing the same measure 18 years apart pre-pandemic (1999-2001 vs. 2019), the range barely moved (3.81 to 3.7 years) despite a national trend improvement of nearly two years. A reserving assumption that projects convergence toward the national trend for a geographically concentrated block would not be supported by this two-decade record.
  3. The gap can widen abruptly in a shock year, and it widened toward the states that were already worst-off. The 2021 pandemic year pushed the state range to 4.5 years and the cross-state standard deviation up ~27%. This is the tail-risk case reserving needs a margin for: a mortality shock does not fall evenly across the geographic book, and the states with structurally lower e(65) are not a random sample of where such a shock lands — they were disproportionately affected in 2021.

None of this changes the direction of the claim-duration effect (LTC claim costs move opposite to mortality improvement: living longer post-65 generally extends time exposed to LTC need, all else equal), but it argues for reserving with state- or region-differentiated mortality/longevity assumptions rather than a single national e(65) trend, and for holding an explicit tail margin against synchronized regional mortality shocks rather than assuming the historical state gap will average out across a large enough block.

Data and method notes

Corpus gap: the AskAmerica warehouse (search_catalog against all loaded schemas) carries no life-expectancy or mortality life-table data of any kind — the term "expectancy" matched zero tables. Every figure above was pulled from the primary CDC/NCHS PDF reports (not a secondary summary site) via web_fetch through the Wayback Machine, because cdc.gov itself returns HTTP 403 to direct fetches from this environment; the Wayback-archived copies are byte-identical captures of the same CDC PDFs.

Vintage note on the two-decade baseline: "two decades ago" is operationalized as the 1999-2001 three-year-average state life tables (the closest CDC decennial state-level product to the year 2000/2001); the true single-year-2000 national figure (17.6) and the 1999-2001 three-year national figure (17.77) differ by 0.17 years and are both reported above so the choice is checkable rather than hidden.

All arithmetic (deltas, mean, standard deviation, min/max, Pearson correlation, and the convergence-slope regression) was computed directly in Python from the transcribed CDC table values, not estimated by eye.
