Coal Is Far More Dangerous Per Unit of Power Than Natural Gas, Which Is Far More Dangerous Than Nuclear

Deaths per terawatt-hour generated, combining accidents and chronic air-pollution mortality

Summary

Coal is the most dangerous, natural gas is a distant second, and nuclear is by far the safest — for the amount of electricity each actually produces. Standard estimates put coal at about 24.6 deaths per terawatt-hour (TWh) generated, natural gas at about 2.8 deaths per TWh, and nuclear at roughly 0.03 deaths per TWh. That makes coal about 9x deadlier than gas, and gas about 94x deadlier than nuclear per unit of power delivered — coal is roughly 800x deadlier than nuclear.

Nearly all of this gap comes not from dramatic accidents but from routine air pollution: fine particulate matter, sulfur dioxide, and nitrogen oxides from burning coal and gas cause chronic respiratory and cardiovascular disease, which kills far more people, far more quietly, than mining disasters or reactor accidents ever have.

The numbers and where they come from

The standard reference figures, compiled by Our World in Data and consistent with the peer-reviewed literature, are:

  • Coal: ~24.6 deaths/TWh — combines coal-mining accidents and, overwhelmingly, air-pollution-driven premature deaths (heart disease, stroke, lung disease, lung cancer) from particulate and sulfur emissions.
  • Natural gas: ~2.8 deaths/TWh — mostly air pollution (gas combustion still emits PM2.5, NOx, and secondary particulates), plus a smaller contribution from pipeline/extraction accidents.
  • Nuclear: ~0.03 deaths/TWh — dominated by the two major accidents (Chernobyl, Fukushima) plus uranium mining; normal operation emits essentially no air pollution.

These synthesize Markandya & Wilkinson's 2007 Lancet study ("Electricity generation and health"), which found coal ~25 deaths/TWh, oil ~18, and gas ~3, with nuclear causing roughly one death every 14 TWh-years of output, updated with more recent work (Sovacool et al. and others) that extends the accident record and refines the pollution-mortality estimates.

Independent confirmation for the US specifically: a 2023 peer-reviewed study (published in Science, covered by Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health and NIH's National Institute on Aging) found coal-plant PM2.5 carries roughly double the mortality risk per microgram of PM2.5 from other sources, and attributed about 460,000 deaths in the US between 1999 and 2020 to coal-fired power plant emissions — a figure of the same order of magnitude the deaths-per-TWh estimate implies given how much coal power the US burned over that period.

Why the gap is this large

The mechanism is the same for coal and gas — combustion releases fine particulates and precursor gases that convert to secondary PM2.5 in the atmosphere — but coal is dirtier per unit of energy: it has a higher carbon and sulfur content and (absent aggressive scrubbing) releases more particulate matter, SO2, and heavy metals (mercury, arsenic) per TWh generated than gas does. Natural gas combustion is cleaner but not clean: it still emits PM2.5, NOx and VOCs, which is why its rate is meaningfully above zero and clearly above nuclear's.

Nuclear's operating emissions are essentially zero; its death toll is concentrated almost entirely in two historical accidents (Chernobyl 1986, Fukushima 2011) and upstream uranium mining, which is why the rate is so low despite those accidents being individually catastrophic and highly visible — the denominator (total TWh generated safely over decades) overwhelms the numerator.

Caveats

This question could not be answered from AskAmerica's own SQL tables — the connector's energy schema (EIA generation, plant inventory) and health schema (CDC mortality) exist as separate US datasets with no built-in causal linkage between a power plant's emissions and a downstream mortality count; that linkage is exactly the kind of exposure-response epidemiological modeling done in the peer-reviewed literature cited here, not something derivable by joining two warehouse tables. The figures above are therefore drawn from published research (Our World in Data's synthesis, Markandya & Wilkinson 2007, and the 2023 US coal-mortality study), not computed fresh in this session.

Two things worth flagging: (1) researchers who have revisited these estimates generally believe the fossil-fuel figures are underestimates — more recent air-quality epidemiology suggests the true pollution death toll from coal and gas could be several times higher than the Markandya & Wilkinson baseline, which would widen the gap with nuclear further, not narrow it. (2) These are historical, mostly pre-2020 European/global averages; US-specific rates depend on plant vintage, scrubber technology, and proximity to population centers, and modern gas plants with strict emissions controls likely sit toward the low end of the gas range.
