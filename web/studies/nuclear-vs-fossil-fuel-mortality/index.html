Coal is the most dangerous of the three to human health per unit of power produced — roughly 9x more deadly than natural gas and roughly 800x more deadly than nuclear

Deaths per terawatt-hour of electricity generated, from accidents plus air pollution across the full fuel-supply chain

Summary

Per unit of electricity actually produced, coal is unambiguously the most dangerous of the three to human health, natural gas is a distant second, and nuclear is the safest by a wide margin — on the order of two orders of magnitude safer than gas and nearly three orders of magnitude safer than coal. Using the standard peer-reviewed metric, deaths per terawatt-hour (TWh) of electricity, which counts both accidents in the fuel supply chain (mining, transport, plant operation, and for nuclear, meltdowns) and deaths from air pollution: coal is about 24.6 deaths/TWh, oil about 18.4, natural gas about 2.8, and nuclear about 0.03. The gap is driven almost entirely by air pollution, not accidents — coal combustion emits fine particulate matter (PM2.5), sulfur dioxide, and nitrogen oxides that cause cardiovascular and respiratory disease at population scale, while nuclear's death toll is dominated by two accidents (Chernobyl and Fukushima) spread over more than 55 years and nearly 97,000 TWh of cumulative global generation.

The numbers, and where they come from

The most widely cited peer-reviewed source for fossil-fuel death rates is Anil Markandya and Paul Wilkinson, "Electricity generation and health," The Lancet, 2007, which used the European Commission's ExternE methodology to estimate life-cycle mortality (mining, transport, combustion, air pollution) per unit of electricity for European power plants. Nuclear and renewable death rates come from Sovacool et al. (2016)'s accident database and from Our World in Data's own calculation using confirmed Chernobyl (433 deaths) and Fukushima (2,314 deaths, mostly from evacuation-related stress and disruption rather than radiation) tolls divided by cumulative global nuclear generation, 1965–2021 (96,876 TWh), giving 0.028 deaths/TWh — rounded to ~0.03 in the chart above.

These figures likely understate the true global gap. They are calibrated to European plants with strict pollution controls; in regions with weaker controls and denser populations near plants (much of Asia, in particular), the death rate per TWh is materially higher. Cross-checking against total-mortality estimates: multiplying the 2007 European rates by 2021 global fossil generation implies about 280,000 deaths/year from fossil electricity, but Lelieveld et al. (2018, cited by OWID) estimate roughly 3.6 million deaths/year from all fossil-fuel air pollution, of which about 12% (roughly 1.1–2.6 million) is attributable to the electricity sector specifically. Reconciling those two lines of evidence implies the true global coal death rate is likely 93–224 deaths/TWh — 4 to 9 times the European-plant estimate used in the headline chart, which would push coal's advantage over gas well past 30–80x rather than 9x.

A US-specific check

A 2023 study in Science (Henneman, Choirat, Dedoussi, Bell, Zigler & Casey, "Mortality risk from United States coal electricity generation") directly modeled coal-plant SO2-derived PM2.5 exposure and attributed roughly 460,000 excess deaths in the US between 1999 and 2020 to coal-fired power plant pollution, with the majority occurring in the earlier, higher-emission years of that window (before scrubber retrofits and plant retirements cut US coal PM2.5 sharply). The same study found coal-sourced PM2.5 carries roughly double the mortality risk per unit mass of PM2.5 from other sources, because of its sulfate content. This is consistent with, and independently confirms, the qualitative finding above using a completely different dataset and study design (US-specific, not European), even though it is not itself expressed as a deaths-per-TWh rate.

Nuclear's safety record in context

Public perception of nuclear risk is dominated by two accidents. Chernobyl (1986) killed 433 people by UNSCEAR's most recent accounting (workers, first responders, and radiation-attributable cancers), and Fukushima (2011) is attributed 2,314 deaths, overwhelmingly from the stress, disruption, and health consequences of a rushed evacuation rather than from radiation exposure itself — no radiation deaths beyond a single worker's 2018 lung-cancer case have been confirmed. Both events, however tragic, are diluted across nearly six decades and ~97,000 TWh of global nuclear generation, and by comparison air pollution from fossil fuels kills a similar number of people every few days worldwide. A widely cited NASA-affiliated analysis (Kharecha & Hansen, 2013, "Prevented Mortality and Greenhouse Gas Emissions from Historical and Projected Nuclear Power") estimated that nuclear power's historical displacement of coal-fired generation prevented roughly 1.8 million premature deaths globally between 1971 and 2009 — a separate line of evidence pointing the same direction as the deaths-per-TWh comparison.

Answer

Coal is the most dangerous of the three to human health per unit of power produced, natural gas is intermediate, and nuclear is the safest — by a large margin in both directions. This ranking (coal > gas >> nuclear) is robust across every methodology reviewed here: the European ExternE-based Lancet estimates, the Sovacool/OWID accident-and-pollution compilation, an independent US-specific epidemiological study of coal plants, and the historical mortality-prevention literature on nuclear power. The main uncertainty is not the ranking but the exact size of coal's disadvantage — conservative European-based estimates put it at about 9x more dangerous than gas and about 800x more dangerous than nuclear per TWh, while global, pollution-control-adjusted estimates suggest the coal-vs-gas gap could be 30–80x. Under no methodology found does gas or coal come close to nuclear's safety record, and under no methodology does coal come out safer than gas.
