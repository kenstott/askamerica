How much does pipeline capacity utilization out of a producing basin explain the local crude-oil basis spread, and does that relationship hold across different basins?

askamerica run · 2026-09-15

## Answer

Pipeline (and refinery) takeaway capacity utilization explains local crude basis spreads well, but **the relationship is threshold/nonlinear and basin-specific, not a uniform linear "utilization-in, spread-out" rule** — and it clearly does not hold the same way in every basin.

**Data-availability note first:** the AskAmerica corpus has no table of basin-level crude pipeline capacity/utilization or basin-specific crude spot basis prices (Midland, Bakken/Clearbrook, LLS, WTI Cushing, etc.) — confirmed via `search_catalog`, which returned `unmatched_terms: ["midland","cushing","differential"]`. It carries only national WTI (FRED `DCOILWTICO`/`econ.global_conditions`), PADD-level refinery utilization (`energy.refinery_utilization_summary`), and basin rig-count/drilling activity (`energy.eia_drilling_activity` — Permian, Bakken, Eagle Ford, Appalachia, Haynesville) — none of which carries a basin basis-spread series. So this answer is built entirely from the primary economics literature and EIA/industry reporting, not from a warehouse regression.

### The core mechanism (Permian, well identified)

The best-identified estimate is Shaun McRae's NBER working paper *"Crude Oil Price Differentials and Pipeline Infrastructure"* (NBER WP 24170, Dec. 2017), using Permian Basin data 2007–2016. He regresses the WTI Midland–LLS (Gulf Coast benchmark) differential on regional "excess production" — basin output net of available pipeline + refinery capacity — and finds:

- The relationship is **discontinuous**, not linear in utilization: below capacity, an extra 100,000 b/d of production reduces the local price by about **$1.61/bbl**; once production exceeds the combined pipeline + refinery takeaway capacity, the same increment costs producers roughly **$11.15/bbl** — a discrete jump once the constraint binds, not a smooth slope.
- Eliminating the Permian pipeline bottleneck (simulated as a new 100,000 b/d pipeline in Sept. 2014) is estimated to have raised local producer prices by **$5.72–$10.86/bbl**, of which **~90% was a transfer from existing shippers and refiners** rather than a net welfare gain — i.e., most of the basis spread when capacity is tight is midstream rent extraction, not a "real" transport-cost pass-through.
- At the national level, the production-weighted discount to the world price fell from about **$28/bbl (Sept. 2011) to $5/bbl (Dec. 2016)**, and the mean state-level differential fell from **~$10/bbl (2011) to ~$1/bbl (2016)**, tracking the buildout of pipeline capacity (U.S. crude pipeline mileage grew from 54,600 to 73,200 miles 2010–2015).
- Refinery utilization itself showed **no significant relationship** to the Permian discount (refineries there ran near capacity throughout, so the marginal barrel's fate was determined by pipeline egress, not refinery throughput), and the higher wellhead prices did **not** pass through to Houston/Midland/El Paso retail gasoline prices — Gulf Coast refiners set the marginal gasoline price regardless of the crude basis.

EIA's own March 2019 "Today in Energy" note corroborates the mechanism directly: after the Sunrise Pipeline extension (+120,000 b/d) and the Seminole-Red repurposing (+200,000 b/d) relieved Permian-to-Cushing constraints in early 2019, WTI Midland converged to WTI Cushing, while the remaining Midland-to-Houston discount (still ~$7/bbl at the time) reflected the still-binding Permian-to-Gulf-Coast leg — i.e., basis narrows leg-by-leg as the specific constrained segment is relieved, not as an aggregate "capacity" number moves.

### Does it hold across basins? No — not uniformly

McRae repeats the same test for Colorado (Niobrara/DJ Basin) and North Dakota (Bakken/Williston):

- **Colorado/Niobrara:** the relationship holds qualitatively the same way as Permian. Before 2015, production exceeded pipeline + refinery capacity, producing a **~$2.80/bbl discount**; the White Cliffs, Northeast Colorado Lateral, and Saddlehorn pipeline additions (adding ~495,000 b/d of egress) pushed capacity above production by end-2015, and the discount narrowed accordingly.
- **Bakken/Williston: the relationship breaks down.** McRae finds the price differential is **uncorrelated with excess production** in North Dakota. Two reasons: (1) excess production was positive (i.e., the constraint was binding) almost continuously since 2011, leaving too little within-basin variation to identify the effect; and (2) Bakken producers had a genuinely multi-modal, flexible egress network (pipeline south to Wyoming, north to Canada, east to Minnesota, *plus* a large unit-train rail fleet that can reach refineries across North America), which decouples the "how full is any one pipeline" measure from the price the marginal barrel actually receives.

This basin-specific breakdown is confirmed by a later, peer-reviewed follow-up — Phat V. Luong, **"Crude oil pipeline constraints: A tale of two shales,"** *Resources Policy* 80 (2023), art. 103203 — using cointegration tests across the same two basins. He finds Bakken sweet crude at Williston **remained cointegrated** with the national WTI benchmark and downstream gasoline prices even during pipeline bottlenecks, because rail provided a continuous arbitrage channel; Midland crude in the Permian, which relied almost exclusively on pipelines, **decoupled from WTI during the bottleneck and only re-cointegrated after new pipeline capacity came online.** In other words: pipeline-utilization is a strong predictor of basis *specifically in basins without a comparably-scaled alternative transport mode*; where rail (or another substitute) is a real option, the same utilization measure loses its explanatory power because it's no longer the binding constraint on the marginal barrel.

### Later evidence: the same mechanism, but the trigger level has shifted

Post-2019 Permian pipeline buildout (several new greenfield lines to the Gulf Coast) roughly doubled available egress versus 2017–2018. Industry data (East Daley/RBN) show that even as pipeline utilization approached ~80% again in 2024 — a level that produced multi-dollar Midland discounts in 2018 — the Houston–Midland spread stayed inside a **35–90 cent/bbl** range, far narrower than the **$10–15/bbl** spreads seen at similar utilization in 2017–2018. This indicates the utilization→basis relationship is **not stationary over time**: the threshold at which utilization starts to bite depends on the level of redundant capacity and the granularity of individual pipeline segments, so "utilization %" alone, without knowing which specific leg is constrained, is not by itself a stable predictor across periods, let alone across basins.

### Bottom line

- Pipeline (+ refinery) capacity utilization explains a large, well-identified share of the Permian and Colorado/Niobrara basis spreads, but the relationship is a **threshold effect** (large discrete jump once regional production exceeds available takeaway/refining capacity), not a smooth linear one — best estimate: eliminating a binding Permian constraint raised producer prices $6–11/bbl, with ~90% of that being a transfer from shippers/refiners rather than a resource-cost saving.
- It does **not** hold the same way in the Bakken/Williston basin, where a mature rail-egress network decouples pipeline utilization from the price the marginal barrel realizes, and where near-constant capacity bindingness left too little variation to identify an effect at all.
- The strength of the relationship also depends on the available substitute transport mode and the specific constrained segment (Cushing leg vs. Gulf Coast leg), and the utilization level that triggers a wide spread has shifted over time as more redundant capacity was built.
