When FEMA Expands a Flood Zone, Only a Minority of Newly Mapped Properties Buy Flood Insurance — About 30%, Best Estimate

No single national "newly mapped take-up rate" is published; this answer relies on take-up rates measured inside Special Flood Hazard Areas (SFHAs), where newly mapped properties land, plus one live local case.

Summary

There is no single official statistic titled "take-up rate among newly mapped properties." The closest published proxy is the take-up rate measured directly among properties inside Special Flood Hazard Areas (SFHAs) — the destination zone every newly mapped property lands in. That national average is roughly 30% of at-risk properties insured, with wide local variation: under 10% in many inland/low-income areas, up to 80%+ in a handful of high-risk coastal counties, and about 50% in Harris County, Texas, which is itself in the middle of a major 2026 remap. In short: even though flood insurance purchase is legally required for many newly mapped properties, a solid majority still end up uninsured.

What "newly mapped" triggers, and why there's no single official rate

When FEMA issues an updated Flood Insurance Rate Map (FIRM) that moves a property into the SFHA, two things change: (1) if the property carries a federally backed mortgage, flood insurance purchase becomes legally mandatory, enforceable by the lender; (2) FEMA offers a "Newly Mapped" premium discount for the first year, phasing out over subsequent years (up to ~18%/year increases toward full-risk rates), conditional on continuous coverage — a lapse forfeits the discount (FEMA glossary; Newly Mapped fact sheet). As of October 2023, only about 7% of all NFIP policies nationwide were receiving the Newly Mapped subsidy — a modest slice of the overall book. FEMA does not publish a property-level "share that bought a policy" statistic specifically for remapped properties, which is why this answer relies on the SFHA-level take-up rate as the best available proxy.

The take-up LEVEL once inside an SFHA: roughly 30% nationally, but wide variation

Using February 2018 NFIP policy data, the Wharton Risk Management Center (Kousky & Lingle, 2018) put the SFHA take-up rate at around 30% nationally, with strong geographic skew: a handful of coastal counties (e.g., St. Bernard Parish, LA; Okaloosa County, FL) are above 80%, a few high-risk inland counties (e.g., Sacramento County, CA) are above 60%, while most of the country sits well below the 30% average (Wharton/Kousky & Lingle, "The 3 Maps That Explain Residential Flood Insurance Purchases," 2018).

GAO's 2023 review of FEMA's Risk Rating 2.0 methodology puts a similar order of magnitude on a different (broader) denominator — "at-risk" properties rather than strictly mapped-SFHA properties: about 23% of at-risk coastal properties carry an NFIP policy, versus only about 4% of at-risk inland properties (GAO-23-105977).

A live, local example directly on point: reporting on Harris County, Texas's 2026 FEMA remap (which expands the 100-year floodplain by roughly 130 square miles, pulling entire new neighborhoods into the SFHA) puts current coverage, per the latest FEMA data, at only 18% of all residential properties countywide and about 50% of properties already inside the existing SFHA (Houston Chronicle, April 2026). Local researchers quoted in that reporting expect many households newly pulled into the SFHA to buy a policy in year one to capture the Newly Mapped discount, then lapse in year two as the discount phases out and mandatory-purchase enforcement (which allows a lender up to 45 days before force-placing coverage) works its way through.

Why take-up stays well under 100% even though purchase is "mandatory"

  • The mandate only binds mortgaged owners. The 1973 mandatory purchase requirement applies only to properties in the SFHA with a loan from a federally regulated or backed lender. Owners without a mortgage (common among older, longtime residents — exactly the demographic seen in the Harris County reporting) face no legal requirement at all.
  • Enforcement has real lags and gaps. Lenders must notify a borrower of a coverage shortfall and can force-place a (2-3x costlier) policy only after a 45-day cure window; compliance auditing has historically been inconsistent across servicers.
  • Affordability. Researcher commentary (Jesse Gourevitch, Environmental Defense Fund) points to premium cost as a major driver — newly mapped premiums in Harris County often exceed $1,000/year, and current NFIP enrollment figures under the post-2021 Risk Rating 2.0 pricing regime run notably lower than prior levels, especially in lower-income ZIP codes (Gourevitch, Snyder & Kousky, Journal of Catastrophe Risk and Resilience).
  • The discount itself decays. The Newly Mapped first-year discount is designed to ease the transition, but premiums step up toward full-risk rates (up to 18%/year) and any coverage lapse forfeits the discounted rating entirely — creating a "buy in year one, lapse in year two" pattern researchers expect.

Bottom line and confidence

Best available estimate: roughly 30% of newly mapped properties end up carrying flood insurance, using the national SFHA take-up rate as the closest published proxy (Wharton, Feb 2018 snapshot), corroborated by GAO's separate at-risk-property figures and by a live 2026 local case (Harris County, ~50% SFHA take-up, 18% countywide). The true figure for any specific remap will vary widely by local income level, coastal vs. inland location, and prior flood experience — from under 10% in some inland/low-income ZIP codes to well over 50% in high-risk coastal communities. No source publishes a single "take-up rate among newly mapped properties" statistic broken out from the general SFHA population, so this is the best available proxy rather than a direct measurement. We checked AskAmerica's own disasters.nfip_policies table (2011-2026, OpenFEMA source) for a direct property-level newly-mapped flag; the table carries flood_zone and policy dates but no prior-zone or newly-mapped indicator, so a remap-specific figure cannot be computed in-house from this connector's tables — the answer therefore relies on external published research.
