Veterans with the highest VA disability ratings die at more than twice the rate of lower-rated veterans, but private life insurers underwrite the condition and its stability, not the VA percentage

Peer-reviewed VA cohort studies (2018) vs. VA guaranteed-issue insurance and commercial underwriting practice

Summary

Among the roughly 4 million veterans receiving VA disability compensation, mortality rises sharply with disability-rating severity: 1-year mortality was 4.5% for veterans rated 100% disabled versus 1.9% for those rated 10-40% or 50-90% — a 2.45x odds ratio for the 100%-rated group (95% CI 2.40-2.50), from a 2018 VA cohort study of 4,010,720 veterans. Mortality also differs by the type of condition, independent of severity: veterans with service-connected PTSD or major depression had roughly double the share of deaths from external causes (accident, suicide, assault) — 6.5% versus 3.1% for veterans without those conditions — and cause-of-death patterns shift with age, with cardiovascular disease and cancer dominating deaths in older veterans and external causes accounting for nearly a third of deaths in younger rated veterans. On the insurance side, this mortality gradient is NOT mirrored in commercial underwriting practice keyed to the VA rating itself: industry sources describe private insurers as focusing primarily on severity, treatment, and clinical stability of the specific diagnosis, largely regardless of whether it is 'service-connected' or what VA percentage attaches to it. The one place the VA disability rating is used directly in life insurance is the VA's own guaranteed-issue program (VALife), which explicitly waives medical underwriting for any veteran with a 0-100% VA disability rating, up to $40,000 of coverage — the opposite of risk-based pricing, structured as a benefit rather than an actuarial product.

How mortality differs by disability rating severity

Maynard, Nelson & Fihn (Public Health Reports, 2018; PMC6225874) studied all 4,010,720 living veterans receiving VA disability compensation as of October 1, 2016, with 1-year follow-up mortality through September 30, 2017. Veterans were grouped into combined disability-rating bands: low (10-40%, 47.2% of the cohort), medium (50-90%, 39.9%), and high (100%, 12.8%).

  • 1-year mortality: 1.9% (low), 1.9% (medium), 4.5% (high, 100% rated)
  • Adjusted odds ratio for 1-year mortality, 100% vs. low rating: 2.45 (95% CI 2.40-2.50), controlling for age, sex, service branch, pay grade, and health-care utilization
  • The 100%-rated group was older on average (mean age 61 vs. 55-58), more often male (91.8%), and far heavier users of VA health care in the prior year (75.3% had an outpatient visit, vs. 40.5% of the low-rated group) — the study's authors flag these as related but statistically controlled-for factors, not the whole explanation for the mortality gap.

How mortality differs by TYPE of condition, not just severity

A companion study by the same VA research group, Disability Rating, Age at Death, and Cause of Death in U.S. Veterans with Service-Connected Conditions (Military Medicine, 2018; PubMed 29590473), breaks cause of death down by condition type and age:

  • Mental-health conditions carry a distinctive external-cause signature: veterans with service-connected PTSD or major depression had 6.5% of deaths attributable to external causes (accident, suicide, homicide/assault), versus 3.1% for veterans without those diagnoses — roughly double.
  • Cause of death shifts by age cohort, and by extension by which conditions dominate at each age: in the oldest veterans, nearly half of deaths were from cancer or cardiovascular disease and under 2% from external causes; in the youngest rated veterans, cardiovascular disease and cancer accounted for about a third of deaths while external causes accounted for nearly a third as well — a materially different mortality profile than in older veterans.
  • Most-prevalent rated conditions differ by severity band: in the Maynard cohort, tinnitus and hearing loss were the most common conditions overall, but PTSD prevalence rose sharply with rating severity — 9.5% of low-rated veterans, 37.1% of medium-rated, and 44.6% of the 100%-rated group had service-connected PTSD, alongside elevated ischemic heart disease (16.3% of the 100%-rated group vs. 4.6% of low-rated) and diabetes (21.6% vs. 7.8%). The higher mortality seen at the 100% rating band therefore is not simply "more disability points" — it reflects a cohort concentrated in conditions (cardiac disease, diabetes, PTSD) each carrying its own distinct mortality and cause-of-death signature. A related 2022 study, The Mortality Rate of 100% Service-Connected U.S. Veterans (PubMed 35355898), examines this highest-severity group specifically and corroborates the elevated short-term mortality finding for the fully rated population.

Is this reflected in life-insurance underwriting for veteran applicants?

Not directly, and largely not at all for the VA rating itself. Two distinct insurance channels exist for veterans, and they treat the rating very differently:

  • Commercial (private) life insurance: Insurance-broker/underwriting-practice sources (Diversified Insurance Brokers, a licensed broker whose principal author holds an in-house Chief Underwriter role) describe underwriters as evaluating the specific diagnosis on a severity/stability spectrum — time since diagnosis, treatment consistency, medication stability, absence of recent hospitalization or crisis events, and documented daily functioning — the same framework applied to any applicant with the same clinical condition. Quoting that source directly: "Veterans sometimes worry that a service-connected PTSD rating from the VA will automatically produce a decline. In practice, carriers focus primarily on severity, treatment, and stability rather than the service connection or VA rating level." A veteran with a 70% VA rating for well-controlled, stable PTSD with no hospitalizations in years can qualify at or near standard rates; a veteran with a 10% rating for an unstable, actively symptomatic condition can be table-rated or postponed. Underwriting outcomes for PTSD specifically range from standard rates (stable 2+ years, consistent treatment) to postponement or guaranteed-issue referral (recent hospitalization, escalating acute crisis) — a spectrum driven by clinical trajectory, not by the VA percentage. This is directionally consistent with the epidemiology above (mental-health and cardiometabolic conditions carrying different risk signatures by type), but the VA's own administrative percentage — which measures average loss of earning capacity, not mortality risk — is not itself an underwriting input.
  • VA's own guaranteed-issue program (VALife): Confirmed directly from VA.gov: any veteran aged 80 or under with a VA service-connected disability rating of 0% or higher is automatically approved for VALife, up to $40,000 of whole life coverage in $10,000 increments, with no medical underwriting or proof of good health required. Coverage is guaranteed acceptance regardless of the specific condition or its severity; premiums are set purely by age at application (e.g., $22.00/month at age 40 for $40,000, rising to $510.00/month at age 80), not by health status. There is a 2-year waiting period for full death-benefit coverage (during which a death pays back premiums plus interest — 4.23% for 2026), after which the full policy amount is paid. The predecessor program, Service-Disabled Veterans Life Insurance (S-DVI), operated on the same guaranteed-acceptance logic for veterans with a new service-connected rating but stopped accepting new applications after December 31, 2022; existing S-DVI holders can keep their policies or transition to VALife.

The net picture: the epidemiology clearly shows mortality risk is elevated and condition-specific for higher-rated veterans, but that gradient is absorbed by VA itself through flat, guaranteed-issue coverage (rating used only as an eligibility gate) rather than priced into private-market underwriting, which instead re-derives risk from the veteran's individual clinical picture and treats the VA percentage as largely irrelevant to the pricing decision.
