Mortality among rated veterans varies by rating level and condition, but insurers underwrite the condition and its severity, not the VA percentage

Answer

Mortality among rated veterans differs sharply by rating level and by condition, but the evidence I could read covers one year of follow-up, not lifetime. In a VA cohort of 4.0 million rated veterans, one-year mortality was 4.5% at a 100% combined rating versus 1.9% at both 10-40% and 50-90%. After adjustment, the 100% group had odds 2.45 times those of the low-rated group. By condition, chronic kidney disease (OR 2.02) and COPD (OR 1.77) carried the highest adjusted odds, while PTSD (OR 0.67) and sleep apnea (OR 0.57) carried lower odds. Underwriting is only partly aligned with this. Insurers quoted by Military Times and AAFMAA say they weigh the underlying condition, severity, treatment and co-occurring conditions, and AAFMAA says the VA rating does not by itself determine eligibility. I found no insurer-published rating tables or study linking VA rating to table ratings, so the match is directional and rests on insurer statements, not data.

Mortality by rating and condition (published evidence, not computed here)

  • Maynard et al. 2018, Public Health Reports: VETSNET file linked to the VA vital status file, 4,010,720 rated veterans on 1 Oct 2016, one-year follow-up, logistic regression.
  • Mortality by combined rating: 4.5% (100%), 1.9% (50-90%), 1.9% (10-40%). Adjusted OR vs 10-40%: 2.45 (95% CI 2.40-2.50) for 100%, 1.32 for 50-90%.
  • Condition prevalence differs by rating: PTSD is 44.6% of the 100% group, 37.1% of 50-90% and 9.5% of 10-40%.
  • Adjusted odds ratios by condition (from the paper's results, which I read via a summarized extraction after the full text truncated): CKD 2.02, COPD 1.77, anxiety 1.16, ischemic heart disease 1.14, TBI 1.09, diabetes 0.89, migraine 0.78, genitourinary cancer 0.76, PTSD 0.67, sleep apnea 0.57. Age 65+ vs 50 or younger: 15.7.
  • Interpretation caution: an OR below 1 for PTSD in a model that also controls for age and other conditions does not mean PTSD is protective. The model omits combat exposure and duration of disability, and follow-up is one year. The authors themselves note these limits.
  • Cause of death (Military Medicine, via UW summary): among 605,344 rated veterans who died 2004-2014, 28% had service-connected mental health conditions. Among those with PTSD or major depression, 6.5% of deaths were intentional or accidental, about twice the rate of those without these conditions, with many alcoholic liver disease deaths. Veterans born 1958 or later died at an average age of 43, mostly from suicide, assault or accidents. The oldest cohort died mostly of cancer and cardiovascular disease.

Underwriting

  • VALife: guaranteed-acceptance whole life up to $40,000 for veterans 80 or younger with any rating, including 0%, no health questions. Full benefit starts after a 2-year waiting period (premiums plus interest are returned if death occurs earlier). This is a government fallback, not an underwriting practice.
  • Military Times (2016): USAA, AAFMAA, Navy Mutual and Military Benefit Association officials said PTSD is not an automatic disqualifier and the decision turns on severity, onset, medication, counseling and overall health. One veteran was declined individually by USAA citing PTSD, depression, anxiety, chronic pain and asthma, then accepted for group term coverage elsewhere. The article also says insurers look at whether the applicant has a VA disability rating, though the fetched text of that passage was cut off.
  • AAFMAA: a VA rating indicates severity but does not necessarily determine eligibility. Underwriters weigh date of diagnosis, severity, co-occurring conditions, treatment adherence and stability. Mild and moderate cases can generally be approved, severe or complex cases get individual review, and guaranteed-acceptance products (up to $25,000) are a fallback.

Is that consistent with the mortality pattern? Partly. Condition-and-severity underwriting fits the finding that risk varies by diagnosis and by rating, and mental health severity and comorbidity are explicitly weighed. But the highest-risk physical conditions in the cohort (kidney disease, COPD) are ordinary medical underwriting items, not veteran-specific, and I found no evidence that any insurer prices off the VA percentage or off these cohort estimates. These sources are insurers' own marketing and a news article, so they show stated practice, not measured pricing.

Limits

The AskAmerica warehouse has no mortality by rated condition (it holds ACS veteran counts and VA claims workload), so nothing here was computed from it. Rating-level figures come from the full text of the Maynard paper. The condition-level odds ratios came from a summarized extraction of the paper's results section, since the text truncated, and the same extraction flagged a major depression confidence interval that did not contain its point estimate, so I omitted that estimate. The Military Medicine paper itself was not opened; I relied on the UW summary. Short follow-up and a 2016 snapshot limit extrapolation to lifetime mortality or current cohorts.
