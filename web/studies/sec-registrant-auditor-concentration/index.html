How concentrated is the US public-company audit market, and how much has it changed?

Bottom line: The Big Four (Deloitte, EY, KPMG, PwC) audit a minority of SEC registrants by simple company count (~46-52%) but an overwhelming majority of the market capitalization they collectively represent (~90-97%). Concentration is heavily size-weighted: it is moderate across the full population of ~7,000-8,000 filers (many of them micro-caps using small regional CPA firms) and extreme among large, economically significant companies. There is no evidence of a strong secular increase in Big Four share over the last five years — it has been roughly flat to modestly declining by simple count — but the level of concentration among large filers remains high enough that PCAOB and academic commentary treat "too few to fail" as a live systemic-fragility concern, not primarily a "getting worse" one.

1. Market share today

By company count (askamerica-computed, real scale)

Using SEC XBRL cover-page tag dei:AuditorName (mandatory since fiscal year 2021 under the Holding Foreign Companies Accountable Act disclosure rules) in sec.financial_line_items, I took each filer's most recent tagged auditor across 7,969 distinct CIKs (34,981 filing-year rows, 2010-2026 vintage, effectively concentrated in 2021-2026 since the tag is recent):

Auditor Companies Share
EY 1,044 13.1%
Deloitte & Touche 1,026 12.9%
PwC 857 10.8%
KPMG 771 9.7%
Big Four combined 3,698 46.4%
All other firms (~200+ named firms, e.g. Marcum, Grant Thornton, BDO, Withum, RSM, BF Borgers, M&K CPAS, Crowe, MaloneBailey...) 4,271 53.6%

This computed figure (46.4%) is in the same neighborhood as, though a bit below, Ideagen/Audit Analytics' published "Who Audits Public Companies" figures (49.7% of SEC registrants in 2024, ~51% in 2025) — the difference is denominator: my query counts every CIK that has ever tagged dei:AuditorName (skews toward smaller/newer registrants that entered EDGAR post-2021), while Audit Analytics counts the full active SEC registrant population including older filers that may not have refiled a 10-K recently enough to carry the tag.

Concentration index (company-count basis, my own estimate): Approximating a Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI) by splitting the "Other" bucket using each named firm's relative share of total filing-year mentions (Marcum, Grant Thornton, BDO, Withum, RSM, BF Borgers, etc., scaled to the snapshot totals above, remainder treated as a long tail of small independent CPA firms) gives an estimated HHI ≈ 600-650 for the full filer population — below the DOJ/FTC "moderately concentrated" threshold of 1,500. This is an approximation (exact only for the Big Four; the non-Big-Four split is scaled from mention counts, not audited directly), disclosed as such.

By economic weight (market cap / size) — sourced, not computed here

I was not able to complete a market-cap-weighted join at scale in this session (see Limitations) — a real gap I am flagging rather than papering over. The published, well-established figures are unambiguous on this dimension and orders of magnitude more concentrated than the count-based numbers: - The Big Four collectively audit ~97% of total US public-company market capitalization (PCAOB, "Audit Industry Concentration and Potential Implications"). - The Big Four audit 90% of "large accelerated filers" (public float ≥ $700M) as of January 2024, up from 88% the year before (Ideagen/Audit Analytics, "Who Audits Public Companies — 2024 edition"). - In specific S&P 500 sectors the concentration is even more extreme: one firm audits ~92% of S&P 500 telecom market cap; two firms audit ~75% of S&P 500 energy, materials, and IT market cap each (PCAOB). - A 2017 estimate specifically for large-accelerated filers put the HHI at 2,128 — well above the DOJ "highly concentrated" threshold of 1,800 (ProMarket / McKenna, citing DOJ guidelines).

Read together, my computed count-based numbers and the sourced market-cap numbers tell a coherent, not contradictory, story: count-weighting treats a $50M micro-cap audited by a regional CPA firm the same as a $2 trillion mega-cap audited by a Big Four firm, which is why the company-count HHI (~600) looks moderate. Weight by the dollars actually at stake and the picture flips to genuinely extreme concentration (HHI >2,000, ~90-97% of market cap) — which is the systemic-fragility-relevant number for a PCAOB-adjacent regulator, since it is large, economically significant, interconnected issuers whose audit-firm failure would matter most.

2. How has it changed over time?

Historical baseline (GAO, 2008): the four largest firms already dominated large-company audits by the mid-2000s. GAO's 2008 report found 82% of Fortune 1000 companies felt their choice of auditor was limited to three or fewer firms, and concentration for companies with revenue >$500M was already "highly concentrated," while the market for smaller issuers (≤$500M revenue) was comparatively less concentrated — the same size-stratification pattern still visible in 2026.

Recent trend (2020-2025), from published Audit Analytics/Ideagen data: - 2019→2020: Big Four combined share fell 2.2 percentage points (after gaining 2.1 points in 2019) — no one-directional trend even short-term; smaller firms (Grant Thornton, BDO, RSM, Marcum, Withum, BF Borgers) gained the offsetting 3.4 points. - 2023→2024: the top-10-firm combined share fell from 70% to 65% of the market (excluding SPACs) — a decline in concentration among the largest cohort of firms, driven partly by SEC registrant count itself shrinking (down 4.9% in 2024, the first decline in five years) and partly by smaller firms gaining registrants. - 2024→2025: Big Four share held roughly steady at just over 50% (49.7% in 2024, ~51% in 2025). - Among large accelerated filers specifically, Big Four share crept up slightly, 88%→90%, 2023→2024 — i.e., the segment that matters most for systemic risk shows a small increase even while the market-wide count share was flat or falling.

My own year-by-year computed series (same dei:AuditorName tag, deduplicated to one auditor per CIK per fiscal year) is noisy — 2021 is a partial/rollout year for the tag and 2023/2026 rows look under-filed relative to neighboring years, which I flag as a data-completeness artifact rather than a real swing:

Year Big Four companies Other Big Four share

The 2024 (52.9%) and 2025 (52.8%) figures from my own query land close to the independently published 49.7%/51% figures — a useful cross-check that the tag-based approach is measuring approximately the right thing, even though the 2021-2023 rows are too incomplete to support a clean multi-year trend line on their own. Net read: no strong, consistent secular increase in Big Four share by company count over 2021-2025; it oscillates in a ~43-57% band that is plausibly mostly filing-completeness noise around a genuinely flat-to-slightly-declining level, consistent with the published top-10/Big-Four figures for the same years. The more robust, slow-moving increase is in the large-filer segment (88%→90%) and has held near/above 90% for years, not something moving fast in either direction.

3. Why "too few to fail" is a live concern regardless of the recent flat trend

  • Each Big Four firm individually would rank in the Fortune 500 (two in the Fortune 250); together they employ ~900,000 people globally (PCAOB).
  • Precedent: Arthur Andersen's 2002 collapse forced ~1,300 companies to find new auditors virtually overnight and triggered direct SEC intervention (PCAOB; ProMarket).
  • When KPMG faced comparable criminal exposure in 2005, DOJ used a deferred-prosecution agreement instead of an indictment — explicit regulatory forbearance attributed by commentators to the absence of a "fifth" firm able to absorb another failure (ProMarket/McKenna).
  • The concern is structural (only three firms would remain to absorb a fourth's clients) rather than a claim that concentration is currently accelerating — the level, not necessarily the trend, is what sustains the fragility argument.

Limitations / what I could not do

  • I attempted to join auditor identity to company size (revenue via sec.financial_facts/financial_line_items, or market cap) to compute my own market-cap-weighted HHI at scale. The connector's underlying queries against sec.financial_line_items (a very large XBRL fact table) were slow enough in this session that several attempts (window-function dedup by year, COUNT(DISTINCT cik) ... GROUP BY year) either ran many minutes without returning or errored; I did not force a low-value partial result and instead report the sourced, well-established market-cap figures above with clear attribution.
  • The "Other" firm breakdown behind my ~600-650 HHI estimate is an approximation (scaled from each firm's share of total filing-year mentions across the corpus, not an exact per-firm latest-year count), disclosed above.
  • dei:AuditorName is only mandatory from FY2021 onward, so pre-2021 rows in the table are sparse/incidental, and my company-count denominator (7,969) is the set of filers that have ever carried the tag, not the full historical SEC registrant population — a different, generally younger-skewing population than Audit Analytics' full registrant count.

  • Market-cap-weighted concentration (~90-97%) and the 2017 HHI=2,128: Medium.
