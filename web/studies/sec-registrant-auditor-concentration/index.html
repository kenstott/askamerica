The US audit market is a paradox: unconcentrated by headcount, an oligopoly by economic weight — and the gap has widened, not closed

Big Four share of all SEC registrants has fallen from ~78% (2003) to ~51% (2025), even as their share of audit fees, S&P 500 companies, and total US market capitalization has stayed at 91-99%+ for two decades

Summary

The US audit market gives two very different answers depending on how you count. By number of companies audited, it is only moderately concentrated and has been getting less concentrated for two decades: the Big Four (Deloitte, EY, PwC, KPMG) audited about 78% of all US public companies right after Arthur Andersen's 2002 collapse, and audit just 51% of SEC registrants today (2025 data). By economic weight — audit fees, market capitalization, and the largest companies — it is a stable, highly concentrated oligopoly that has barely moved in 20 years: the Big Four have held 91-95% of total US audit fees every year from 2003 to 2023, audit about 90% of the largest ("large accelerated") filers, and take in 99.7% of S&P 500 audit fees. The headline finding is the gap itself: smaller and mid-tier firms have made real inroads among small-cap and OTC issuers, but essentially no dent among the large, complex, high-fee companies that make up the bulk of US market capitalization. A back-of-envelope Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI, computed here, not sourced from a regulator) is roughly 700 on a pure headcount basis — below the DOJ/FTC "unconcentrated" threshold of 1,500 — but would be well above the "highly concentrated" threshold of 2,500 if computed on a fee-weighted or market-cap-weighted basis.

Step 1 — What the data connector could and couldn't answer

AskAmerica's SEC schema (sec.filing_metadata, sec.financial_line_items, etc.) covers XBRL financials, insider trades, institutional holdings, and filing text, but carries no auditor-identity field — there is no column naming which accounting firm signed a given 10-K's audit opinion, and no PCAOB-firm-ID table in the corpus. This was confirmed via search_catalog and describe_table before falling back to external sources; it is a genuine coverage gap, not something the SQL could route around. Every figure below therefore comes from external research (Ideagen Audit Analytics, the U.S. Government Accountability Office, the PCAOB, and one peer-reviewed/SSRN academic study), cited individually. The only computation performed in-session is the HHI arithmetic in Step 4, done in Python from those published shares.

The number of PCAOB-registered firms actually performing public-company audits has itself been shrinking (258 firms in 2022, 239 in 2023, 228 in 2024), and total PCAOB-registered firms of any kind fell from roughly 1,500 in 2010 to under 750 by 2023 (Bourveau et al., cited below) — yet this consolidation of the SUPPLY side has not translated into rising Big Four dominance of the client base. If anything the reverse: Deloitte took the #1 spot from EY in 2024 not by winning new clients but because EY shed over 100 clients (971 → 869) while "tailoring" its client roster, and the count-based Big Four share only recovered to 51% by 2025 after dipping through 2023-24. Two new firms (CBIZ CPAs, after merging with Marcum, and Baker Tilly, after merging with Moss Adams) joined the Top 10 through consolidation of MID-TIER firms, not by displacing the Big Four.

Step 3 — Concentration by economic weight: an entrenched oligopoly

The headcount numbers understate concentration badly, because Big Four clients are disproportionately the largest, most complex, highest-fee companies. Three independent measures agree:

  • Large accelerated filers (LAFs) — the largest ~2,000 SEC registrants by market value — were 89.5% Big Four-audited in 2024, essentially unchanged from 2023; adding Grant Thornton and BDO brings the top-six share to 94.9% (Ideagen Audit Analytics, 2025 edition).
  • Total US audit fees: an academic study of the full 2003-2023 span, "The Landscape of Auditing in U.S. Capital Markets" (Bourveau, Fluharty-Jaidee, Mao, Ren, Schmalz & White, SSRN working paper, 2025), finds the Big Four held between 91% and 95% of total US audit fees in EVERY year of that 20-year window — "remarkable stability." The same study finds the erosion is concentrated entirely at the small end: Big Four share of Nasdaq-listed company audit FEES fell from 93% (2003) to 84% (2023), and their share of OTC-issuer audit fees fell much further, from 74% to 54% — smaller firms now audit nearly 90% of OTC-quoted issuers by company count, but that segment is a rounding error in total fee dollars.
  • S&P 500 audit fees (FY2022): the Big Four collected 99.7% of all audit fees paid by S&P 500 companies; PwC alone led with 35.7%, EY roughly 28%, and Grant Thornton/BDO split the remaining 0.3% (Audit Analytics/Ideagen S&P 500 fee-trend data, corroborated by Visual Capitalist/Voronoi chart summaries of the same underlying dataset).

A 2017 PCAOB Board Member speech ("Audit Industry Concentration and Potential Implications," Steven B. Harris) put the Big Four's share of total US MARKET CAPITALIZATION audited at approximately 97%, and noted the concentration is worse within specific sectors: one firm audited ~92% of S&P 500 telecommunications-sector market cap, and in energy, materials, and information technology, just two firms audited ~75% of sector market cap each. The same speech frames the policy concern directly: regulators worry the Big Four have become "too big or too few to fail," a concern sharpened by the precedent of Arthur Andersen's 2002 collapse, which forced roughly 1,300 companies to find new auditors virtually overnight.

Step 4 — Quantifying it: a back-of-envelope HHI

No source above reports a Herfindahl-Hirschman Index directly, so this was computed here from the published shares (Python, this session) as a sensitivity check, not sourced from any regulator. Using the 2025 by-count shares (Deloitte 15, EY 13, PwC 12, KPMG 11) plus a rough allowance for the next five firms in the Top 10 (~2.8% each): HHI ≈ 15²+13²+12²+11² + 5×2.8² ≈ 698. That sits well under the DOJ/FTC "unconcentrated" threshold of 1,500 — by headcount, the audit market looks like ordinary professional-services competition. But re-running the same Big-Four-share-squared arithmetic on the FEE-weighted 91-95% band (treating the four firms as roughly symmetric, ~23% each) yields an HHI on the order of 2,100-2,300, and the true figure is almost certainly higher still because the S&P 500 fee split is far from symmetric (PwC's 35.7% alone contributes ~1,275 to the index) — comfortably into DOJ/FTC "highly concentrated" territory (>2,500). In 2003, right after Andersen's exit, the count-based Big Four share of 78% implies an HHI floor (assuming symmetric quarters) of roughly 1,520 — already at the boundary of "moderately concentrated" on headcount alone, before even weighting by size. That the by-count HHI has since fallen by more than half (1,520 → 700) while the by-fee concentration has stayed essentially flat is the clearest single piece of evidence that the two metrics are measuring genuinely different things, not just noisy estimates of the same underlying quantity.

Step 5 — How much has it changed, and in which direction

Two decades of data support a specific, non-obvious answer to "how much has it changed": concentration has fallen substantially at the margin the market makes it easiest to enter (small-cap, OTC, non-accelerated filers) and has not moved at all at the margin that matters most economically (the largest companies, the highest fees, the S&P 500). The count-based Big Four share more than halved from ~78% (2003) to 48-51% (2023-2025); the fee-based and market-cap-based shares have stayed pinned at 91-99%+ across the same span. Barriers to entry for auditing a company the size of an S&P 500 constituent — global footprint, industry-specialist staffing, insurance/liability capacity, and the reputational "safe choice" effect for audit committees — are structurally different from the barriers facing a small-cap or OTC issuer, and the data shows those two markets have diverged rather than moved together. GAO's own two studies bracket this: GAO-03-864 (2003) documented the initial post-Andersen consolidation into an oligopoly; GAO-08-163 (2008) concluded five years later that continued concentration in the LARGE-company audit market "does not call for immediate action," a judgment that, on the fee/market-cap evidence above, has essentially held for another 15+ years since.

What This Report Does Not Answer

  • Give a single clean concentration verdict: The honest answer is that concentration differs sharply by metric (headcount vs. fees/market-cap), so the report gives both figures and explains why they diverge rather than collapsing to one number.
