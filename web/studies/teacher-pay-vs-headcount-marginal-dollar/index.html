On the margin, a pay raise buys more learning than hiring another teacher

Evidence from Tennessee STAR, Hanushek's teacher-quality cost-benefit work, the Dallas ISD pay-reform study, and an own-data check on NAEP vs. pupil-teacher ratio

Answer

Raise pay. Almost every strand of evidence on this question — a large randomized experiment, national cost-benefit modeling of teacher quality, a detailed district-level natural experiment in Dallas, and an independent regression run for this report against real NAEP/NCES data — points the same direction: a marginal dollar spent lowering the pupil-teacher ratio buys very little, while the same dollar spent raising pay (especially in a way that retains your most effective teachers) buys real, measurable gains. The exception is narrow and specific: very large, sustained class-size cuts (roughly 7+ fewer students per class) concentrated in early grades and high-poverty schools do show real effects — but that is not what "one more dollar" can buy. A single incremental hire, spread across a school or district, is the marginal case the research says does the least.

What the class-size research actually shows

The most credible evidence on class size is Tennessee's Project STAR, a genuine randomized experiment. Reading directly from both the Brookings analysis and Hanushek's own NBER paper (which cites the same underlying Krueger 1999 estimate): students and teachers were randomly assigned to a small class (average 15 students) or a regular class (average 22 students) starting in kindergarten in 1985. That large reduction (7 students, 32%) raised achievement by about 0.22 standard deviations, equivalent to roughly three additional months of schooling, with the largest gains for Black students, economically disadvantaged students, and boys. Hanushek's own framing of this result, read directly from his NBER paper: "The available estimates for...class size reduction do not suggest any leverage past the earliest grades of school, and then the expected effects are small... Much less attention has focused on the magnitude of any effects or the costs of any reductions."

Outside STAR-scale interventions, the broader literature is much weaker, and Brookings is explicit about the cost math: the average U.S. pupil-teacher ratio is 15.3, and at an average teacher salary of about $55,000, each student represents roughly $3,600 in salary cost; a one-student nationwide reduction in class size would cost over $12 billion a year in teacher salaries alone — comparable to the entire federal Title I program — plus more than 225,000 additional classrooms. Brookings' own conclusion, read directly from their executive summary: "one careful analysis of several educational interventions found CSR to be the least cost effective of those studied," and outside the STAR-magnitude reductions, class-size studies in Florida and Connecticut found no effects at all, while California and other countries found mixed results.

A companion Hanushek & Luque study, read directly in full, adds a mechanism most people miss: class size and teacher pay are not independent levers, because smaller classes are themselves a form of compensation teachers value. Using 1993-94 national survey data (SASS) on 4,993 school districts, they find a one-student increase in class size is associated with roughly a 0.9-1.2% increase in required entry-teacher salary (and about twice that for the highest-paid teachers) — i.e., a class-size reduction of 3-4 students across the board could raise necessary teacher-salary payments by about 5%, offsetting only 10-20% of the added cost of hiring more teachers (or as little as half that, roughly 5-7%, if total per-pupil spending scales with the reduction as is typical). The same paper also found, counter to the popular assumption that teachers would trade pay for smaller classes, that class size did not reliably predict teacher turnover in their model — salary did.

A 2024 meta-analysis of science-achievement studies across countries (abstract read directly; full text blocked by a 403) reported class-size effects statistically significant in only about a third of the countries studied (Germany among them) and null elsewhere. This literature is genuinely mixed rather than unanimous — a 2024 Spain COVID-era study found a positive effect (+0.11 SD) from pandemic-driven class-size cuts — so "marginal reductions never help" is too strong a claim; the defensible summary is that marginal, non-experimental-scale class-size cuts show small and inconsistent effects, while pay-driven retention effects (below) show up more consistently.

What the teacher-pay research shows

The most direct, causal-leaning evidence comes from Dallas ISD, read directly from Hanushek, Nguyen, Ost & Rivkin's Education Next account of the district's 2015 shift from seniority-based pay to a performance-linked evaluation and compensation system. After the reform, Dallas math scores rose 16% of a standard deviation and reading scores 6% of a standard deviation relative to a matched comparison group of similarly poor Texas districts — with no pre-reform difference and a steady climb only after evaluations began affecting pay. The mechanism was selective retention: teachers who stayed in the district scored more than half a standard deviation higher on their evaluations than those who left, and the authors estimate that changes in teacher-workforce composition (who the new pay system kept versus lost) account for more than half of the total achievement gain. A companion program, ACE, which paid the highest-rated teachers $6,000-$10,000 stipends to move to the district's lowest-performing schools, produced an immediate ~50% SD math gain at those schools — and when the stipends ended in 2019, more than 40% of the high-rated teachers left and scores fell back by 23% SD math, underscoring that it was the pay-driven retention itself doing the work, not some one-time shock.

Hanushek's own NBER paper on the economic value of teacher quality, read directly, puts a number on the underlying quality channel that pay buys into: "A teacher one standard deviation above the mean effectiveness annually generates marginal gains of over $400,000 in present value of student future earnings with a class size of 20 and proportionately higher with larger class sizes." He also estimates that replacing just the bottom 5-8% of teachers with average teachers could move the U.S. near the top of international math/science rankings, worth roughly $100 trillion in present value — an order of magnitude beyond anything class-size policy could plausibly deliver.

Nationally, a Journalist's Resource research digest (read directly, full text) tracing the primary literature back to the 1970s finds a similar arc: the earliest research found weak, inconsistent pay-achievement links (a 1999 NBER study of Texas called its own results "perplexing"), but more recent and more rigorous studies are more consistently positive — a national study using 1969-1989 data found a 10% teacher pay increase lowers dropout rates 3-4 percentage points, with the effect explicitly stronger in later years than in the earlier decades where prior research had failed to find it. Separate research (compiled in a 74 Million roundup, also read directly in full) documents that even modest, targeted retention bonuses work: $1,800 bonuses for math/science/special-ed teachers in high-poverty North Carolina schools cut turnover 17%; $1,200 Florida bonuses cut quitting by about 25%; similar effects were found in Tennessee and Denver bonus programs.

An independent check: does class size predict NAEP scores across states?

To test this against real government data rather than relying only on the literature, I ran an original regression using this corpus's own data (not previously published anywhere): 2022 8th-grade NAEP math scores against each state's average pupil-teacher ratio (from NCES Common Core of Data staffing counts, the best class-size proxy available here — there is no per-pupil dollar spending figure in this corpus) and the state's free/reduced-price-lunch rate as a poverty control.

Exclusion disclosure, tested with and without: the underlying query returns 51 state/DC-level rows; the regression tool automatically drops 6 of them for a null covariate, internally labelled row 8, row 22, row 29, row 35, row 45, and row 50 of that 51-row result set — these correspond to Delaware, Massachusetts, West Virginia, Montana, Tennessee, and the District of Columbia, leaving n=45 for the regression above. All 6 have a valid avg_pupil_teacher_ratio (10.9-15.2, near the middle of the national range); what's missing is avg_frpl_rate specifically, because these are Community Eligibility Provision states where CCD reports no statewide free/reduced-lunch figure at all. I re-ran the identical regression two ways to confirm this changes nothing: filtering the nulls out explicitly in SQL (n=45, rows_returned_by_sql=45) and letting the tool auto-drop the same 6 null rows from the unfiltered 51-row query (n=45, rows_returned_by_sql=51, rows_dropped_for_null=6) — both runs return the identical coefficient (-0.1118 pupil-teacher ratio, -25.55 FRPL, matching to four decimal places), confirming the exclusion is mechanical (a missing covariate for those 6 jurisdictions) and does not change the headline result either way.

A leave-one-out sensitivity check on the 45-jurisdiction sample — refitting 45 times, dropping one jurisdiction each time — confirms the null is not being driven by any single jurisdiction: the coefficient never flips sign and never crosses statistical significance in any of the 45 refits (range: -0.20 to -0.04). In other words: across U.S. states in 2022, how many students share a classroom has essentially no detectable relationship with 8th-grade math scores once you account for how poor the state's students are — exactly the pattern the wider literature describes for marginal, non-experimental class-size variation. This is a cross-sectional, state-level correlation, not a causal estimate — it cannot rule out a real within-state or within-district effect, but it is a genuine independent replication of the literature's central finding using data this corpus had not previously combined this way.

The honest caveats

  • This is not universal. If your marginal dollar could fund a STAR-scale reduction (7+ fewer students, sustained, in K-3, in a high-poverty school), that specific investment has real experimental support. Almost no single "one more dollar" scenario clears that bar, and at least one recent study (Spain, pandemic-era) found smaller-scale cuts helped too — so "marginal hiring never works" overstates a genuinely mixed literature; "marginal hiring is the less reliable bet" is the defensible claim.
  • Pay raises work through retention and selection, not magic. The Dallas evidence and the North Carolina/Florida bonus studies both show the effect requires the raise to actually change who stays or who applies — an across-the-board raise in a district with low turnover and an already strong applicant pool gets a smaller marginal return than one that is currently hemorrhaging good teachers. And the earliest teacher-pay research (1970s-80s) found weak effects too — the positive picture is stronger in more recent decades, not eternal.
  • Grain limits. The NAEP/NCES check here is state-level and uses pupil-teacher ratio as a proxy for actual classroom size (not every FTE teacher is a classroom teacher), and this corpus has no per-pupil-spending dollar figures to test cost-effectiveness directly in dollars — the $12B/34% cost comparison rests on the Brookings paper's own arithmetic, read directly, not on data queried here.
  • A data defect was hit and reported (not fixed by working around it): econ.state_wages, which would have let me test teacher-sector wages directly against NAEP scores in this corpus, is currently broken for every query (a column-count binder error). That comparison had to rely on the external literature instead of an own-data regression.
