At the margin, a dollar for teachers buys more through pay (retention/quality) than through headcount (class-size cuts) -- unless classes are already large or very young

Synthesis of the class-size and teacher-pay literatures, plus an original NAEP/NCES cross-check of the class-size claim

Summary

For a marginal dollar (or a marginal budget increment), the weight of the evidence favors raising pay over hiring more teachers to shrink class size -- with one clear exception: very young grades (K-3) and/or classes that are currently large (25+). Random-assignment evidence (Tennessee's STAR experiment) shows class-size cuts work, but only with large reductions (~7 fewer students) concentrated in the earliest grades, and are one of the most expensive ways to buy an achievement gain per dollar. Pay increases are the more reliable lever on two fronts research agrees on: teacher retention (a $1,000 real raise cuts attrition by roughly a third of a percentage point; a ~$4,000 raise cuts turnover 3 points within a decade) and, via merit/incentive pay, a smaller but real direct test-score bump (~0.05 student SD). A one-dollar increment is too small to matter through either channel on its own -- the real question is which channel a larger budget increase should go through, and on cost-effectiveness and current U.S. class sizes (already in the low-to-mid 20s in most states), the pay/retention channel is generally judged the better buy at the margin by researchers such as Hanushek and by policy reviews (CAP, Brookings).

What the class-size literature says

The strongest causal evidence for class-size reduction is Tennessee's Project STAR, a randomized experiment (K-3, 1985-1989) that cut class size from ~22-25 to ~13-17 students. It found statistically significant gains of roughly 0.15-0.20 student standard deviations, concentrated in the earliest grades and larger for poor, minority, and male students (Krueger, Finn & Achilles STAR literature).

But STAR's reduction was large (7-8 fewer students) and concentrated at the entry grades; the estimated benefit does not extrapolate linearly to a marginal one-or-two-student cut, and effects mostly fade past 3rd grade. Eric Hanushek's body of work argues the broader class-size literature does not support further reduction as cost-effective: "the enormous amount of research devoted to studying class size has failed to make a very convincing case that reducing class size is likely to improve student performance," and other levers -- especially teacher quality -- have larger, cheaper payoffs (Hanushek & Luque 2000; Hanushek 1999 Senate testimony). The Center for American Progress and Brookings both summarize the applied-policy consensus similarly: at scale, mandated class-size cuts are expensive per point of achievement gained and crowd out other spending, including on teacher pay itself, since districts stretched thin on staffing often pay less per teacher (CAP, "The False Promise of Class-Size Reduction"; Brookings class-size review). Hanushek's own estimate: a one-student decrease in class size is associated with roughly 1-2% lower teacher salaries at a fixed budget -- the two levers are in direct tension for a fixed dollar.

What the teacher-pay literature says

Two separate channels matter for pay. First, retention: teacher salary increases reduce attrition. Pooled estimates put the effect at roughly a 0.34 percentage-point reduction in turnover per $1,000 of real (cost-of-living-adjusted) raise (Journalist's Resource summary of the salary-attrition literature). A national study of school-finance reforms that raised salaries by about $4,000 found turnover fell roughly 3 percentage points, with effects building over about a decade (Nguyen, Anglum & Crouch 2023). Retention effects are largest for less-experienced teachers and fade after ~19 years of experience. One caveat from the same literature: a Stanford CEPA study of a district-level salary bump found the retention gain coincided with a broader economic downturn and could not fully rule out that the downturn itself, not the raise, drove teachers to stay (Stanford CEPA salary-incentive study). That specific confound was tied to that study's own (pre-2020) window; checking the current environment, no U.S. recession is underway or judged likely as of September 2026 -- most 2026 forecasts put recession odds at roughly 15-42% and describe the economy as still expanding, with unemployment near 4.3% (Bloomberg, "US Recession Risk Is Receding"; Money, "Will We Have a Recession in 2026?") -- so that specific confound is a caveat on the historical study, not a live factor distorting today's retention numbers.

Second, direct incentive effects on scores: a 2021 meta-analysis of merit/incentive pay programs found an average effect of about 0.043-0.05 student standard deviations on test scores -- roughly 4.5 weeks of extra learning -- with larger effects (about double) for group/school-level incentives than individual ones (Pham, Nguyen & Springer 2021, "Teacher Merit Pay: A Meta-Analysis"). That meta-analysis is honest about its limits: most underlying studies are quasi-experimental, not randomized, and it cannot cleanly separate whether gains come from reduced turnover, extra teacher effort, or better teacher sorting.

An original check: does pupil-teacher ratio predict NAEP scores in this corpus? (exclusions disclosed)

This corpus (NCES CCD + NAEP, via AskAmerica's edu schema) carries no teacher-salary or per-pupil-dollar data at all -- only staffing ratios and a poverty proxy -- so it cannot test the pay side directly. It can, however, extend the class-size claim: does a state's average pupil-teacher ratio predict its NAEP grade-4 math score, once poverty (free/reduced-price-lunch rate) is held fixed?

Exclusion disclosure: the query behind this analysis filters WHERE avg_pupil_teacher_ratio IS NOT NULL AND avg_frpl_rate IS NOT NULL. Of 306 available state-year rows for grade-4 math (2013-2024), that predicate drops 25 rows, leaving n=281. By name -- dropped for missing FRPL rate (Community Eligibility Provision states, where NCES publishes no statewide free/reduced-lunch count): Arizona 2024; Delaware 2017, 2019, 2022, 2024; District of Columbia 2017, 2019, 2022, 2024; Massachusetts 2015, 2017, 2019, 2022, 2024; Montana 2022; South Carolina 2024; Tennessee 2017, 2022; West Virginia 2022, 2024. Dropped for missing pupil-teacher ratio: California 2019; Tennessee 2024; Utah 2015, 2017; Wyoming 2015. These 25 rows are concentrated in a handful of CEP-adopting states rather than randomly distributed, so the poverty control is silent on those state-years specifically. As a check on whether this exclusion changes the headline finding: re-running the same regression WITHOUT the FRPL predictor or its null-filter (pupil-teacher ratio alone against score, n=301, only 5 rows dropped) gives a similar-direction but larger, more confounded coefficient of -0.53 points per pupil (p<0.001). The direction (negative, small) is unchanged by the exclusion; the magnitude is not, because omitting the poverty control lets poverty's own effect leak into the class-size coefficient.

Running the regression on the n=281 sample gives a coefficient of -0.256 NAEP points per additional pupil in the ratio (p=0.013), controlling for the FRPL poverty rate (coefficient -20.8, p<0.001 -- poverty dominates the model, R²=0.28 overall). Converting to student standard deviations using the customary NAEP student-level SD of roughly 35 points, that is about -0.007 SD per pupil -- two orders of magnitude smaller than STAR's per-large-reduction effect, consistent with Hanushek's point that broad, marginal class-size variation carries far less signal than STAR's large, randomized, early-grade intervention.

This estimate is not robust enough to lean on hard: a state-by-state leave-one-out check shows the coefficient staying negative in every case (range -0.17 to -0.35) but losing statistical significance at the 0.05 level if either of two particular states is dropped from the 51-jurisdiction sample. It is also purely cross-sectional/correlational -- no state or year fixed effects -- so it cannot rule out that states with worse ratios also differ in unobserved ways (e.g., rural sparsity, other spending choices) that independently affect scores. The honest reading: this corpus is consistent with the broader literature's finding that a marginal, non-experimental change in class size carries a small and fragile achievement signal, but it does not by itself prove causation in either direction, and it is silent on the pay side of the question entirely.

Putting it together for the marginal dollar

  • A literal extra dollar is too small to move either lever meaningfully -- neither one teacher's tiny raise nor a sliver of a new hire changes outcomes. The real decision is about how to allocate the next real increment of new money (e.g., the next $1,000-$10,000 per teacher, or the next few FTE positions).
  • Favor pay when: current class sizes are already in the normal U.S. range (low-to-mid 20s, per NCES); retention/turnover is a problem in the district; or the grades affected are past 3rd grade, where class-size evidence weakens sharply.
  • Favor hiring (to cut class size) when: classes are unusually large (25-30+); the grades affected are K-3; and the district can implement the reduction with fully qualified teachers -- research (NCTQ, Brookings) notes rapid class-size mandates that pull in inexperienced or uncertified teachers can erode the benefit.
  • On net, researchers most focused on cost-effectiveness (Hanushek; CAP; Brookings) come down on the side of pay/quality investment over broad class-size reduction for a marginal dollar, while acknowledging STAR-style evidence that early, large class-size cuts do work when properly implemented and funded.
