No single dataset counts "counties that lost their last bank branch" — but the closest verified figures show 39 rural counties deeply affected 2012-2017, with credit unions, CDFIs, and the Postal Service filling the gap

FDIC Summary of Deposits data via Federal Reserve, NCRC, and Fed Communities/Philadelphia Fed, 2016-2024

Summary

There is no published national count of rural counties that went from having at least one bank branch to having exactly zero over the past ten years — no federal agency or research group tracks that specific threshold at the county level on a rolling ten-year basis. What does exist, and what is reported below, are two related but distinct measurements: the Federal Reserve's 2019 study found 44 U.S. counties were "deeply affected" by branch closures between 2012 and 2017 (defined as having 10 or fewer branches in 2012 and losing at least half by 2017) — 39 of those 44 (89%) were rural, though most still retained at least one branch, not zero. Separately, the National Community Reinvestment Coalition found 86 new rural "banking deserts" were created between 2008 and 2016. Where a rural community has actually lost its literal last branch — documented cases include Duncan, AZ (2016) — access has been replaced, unevenly, by: credit unions and Community Development Financial Institutions (CDFIs) opening small "microsite" branches, ATMs (often third-party, fee-charging), the U.S. Postal Service used informally for cash and money orders, and a shift to online/mobile banking that is adopted more slowly in rural areas. No systematic replacement (postal banking, guaranteed CDFI coverage) exists nationally, and Fed Communities data show only about 1% of 2023 "banking deserts" gained a new branch by 2024.

What the data actually measures — and the gap in the question

Three different, non-interchangeable metrics get used in this space, and none of them is "counties reduced from 1 branch to 0, tracked over a rolling 10 years":

  • Federal Reserve (2019), "Perspectives from Main Street": compared FDIC Summary of Deposits branch counts by county between 2012 and 2017 (a 5-year window). It defines a "deeply affected" county as one with 10 or fewer branches in 2012 that lost at least 50% of them by 2017 — this can mean going from 2 branches to 1, not necessarily to zero. 44 counties nationally met this bar; 39 (89%) were rural.
  • NCRC "banking desert" county/branch tracking (2008-2016): found 86 new rural banking deserts were created, using a broader access-based definition, not a strict branch-count-to-zero test.
  • Fed Communities / Philadelphia Fed Banking Deserts Dashboard (2019-2024): operates at the census tract level, not the county level, and defines a "banking desert" as a tract with no branch within 2/5/10 miles (urban/suburban/rural) of its population center — a tract can be a banking desert while its county still has branches elsewhere. This is the most current, most granular tracker available (updated through mid-2024: 3,629 banking deserts, up from roughly 3,618 a year earlier — an 11-tract net increase most recently, following a much larger post-pandemic surge), but it does not answer "how many counties have zero branches."

I checked the AskAmerica/FDIC connector directly for this: banking.locations (current branch snapshot), banking.sod (branch-level deposits) and banking.history (branch open/close events) are only loaded for the current year (2026), not for a full 10-year historical series. Computing "which counties had >=1 branch in 2016 and have 0 in 2026" would require historical FDIC Summary of Deposits snapshots for multiple years, which this connector does not currently carry — this is a genuine data-coverage gap, not a result that came back as zero.

None of these is a direct answer to "how many rural counties lost their literal last branch," but together they bound the scale: it is very likely fewer than a few hundred counties nationally hit true zero-branch status in the last decade (most "deeply affected" counties retained at least one branch), while several thousand smaller sub-county areas (census tracts) did lose all nearby physical banking access.

What has replaced branch access

  • Credit unions and CDFIs: Fed Communities' 2025 reporting on "the last branch standing" documents CDFI banks and credit unions (e.g., Southern Bancorp in Arkansas, HOPE Credit Union in Mississippi, Tongass Federal Credit Union in Alaska) deliberately keeping a single branch or opening small "microsite" locations — sometimes single-employee offices in borrowed space (a tribal building, a "canoe shed") — in communities mainstream banks have exited. These are the exception, not a systematic response: it is rare for a new branch to open in a banking desert (only about 1% of 2023 banking deserts gained one by 2024), and 96% of 2023 "potential" deserts remained potential deserts a year later.
  • ATMs, often third-party and fee-charging: In Duncan, AZ — whose last branch (Zions Bancorp/National Bank of Arizona) closed in July 2016 — the old branch building now houses only a third-party ATM charging $2.75 per withdrawal.
  • The U.S. Postal Service, informally: Duncan residents and even the local electric cooperative use the post office to buy stamps for cash back or purchase money orders in place of bank deposits — an ad hoc substitute, not an official postal-banking program (formal postal banking has been proposed in Congress but not enacted).
  • Online and mobile banking: nationally, the share of households using digital channels as their primary means of account access rose from about 39% (2013) to 66% (2021), per FDIC survey data, but adoption is significantly slower among rural, older, lower-income, and less-educated households — the same populations most likely to be in a newly branchless county — and mobile banking cannot handle cash deposits, in-person loan underwriting relationships, or business cash management the way a branch did.
  • Nothing, in many cases: Duncan's own experience is that local officials tried and failed to attract a replacement branch, a part-time branch, or even a banking kiosk. Small-business owners there report holding more cash on-site (raising robbery risk) and losing informal lending relationships; a 2017 University of Delaware study cited in this reporting found each branch closing associated with a roughly 20% drop in nearby small-business loan volume.

Bottom line

If pressed for a single number, the most defensible, sourced answer is: 39 rural counties were identified by the Federal Reserve as "deeply affected" by branch closures between 2012 and 2017 — the only federally-produced, county-level statistic that closely approximates the question — while acknowledging that this measures a >=50% loss from a small base, not confirmed reduction to zero, and covers a 2012-2017 window rather than a clean trailing ten years from today. No source, including this connector's own FDIC-sourced banking schema, currently supports computing the literal "reduced to zero branches" county count over a full rolling decade; that is a genuine, disclosed gap rather than an answer of zero.

What This Report Does Not Answer

  • Exact count of rural counties that went from having a bank branch to having zero, over the last 10 years: No FDIC, Federal Reserve, NCRC, or Fed Communities publication tracks this specific metric (county goes from >=1 branch to exactly 0) at a national, 10-year-rolling level. The AskAmerica connector's banking.locations/sod/history tables only carry the CURRENT snapshot year (2026) plus one partial event-year, not the multi-year historical series (e.g., 2016 vs. 2026 branch counts by county) needed to compute this directly. Published research uses adjacent but different metrics: (a) the Federal Reserve's 2012-2017 study of counties that lost >=50% of a small branch base (not necessarily to zero), and (b) Fed Communities' census-tract-level 'banking desert' metric (no branch within a driving radius), which is a smaller geography than a county and defines absence differently. Both are reported below as the closest available proxies, explicitly labeled as not answering the literal question.
