The US port supply-chain crisis peaked in early 2022 and had almost fully cleared by late 2022–2024

Ship queues, dwell times, and freight rates all show the acute crisis resolved; volumes never fell, they simply outran capacity

Summary

The 2021-2022 US port supply-chain crisis was severe by every operational measure but was not a crisis of falling cargo volume — container throughput at the top-10 US ports kept climbing through it (BTS data, connector table transport.bts_port_teu), rising from roughly 3.6-3.9 million TEUs/month pre-pandemic to a peak near 4.7 million TEUs/month in May 2022. The crisis was a capacity/logistics bottleneck: a record 109 container ships queued off the San Pedro Bay ports (LA/Long Beach) in January 2022, container dwell times hit 16.5 days for rail cargo (Aug 2022) versus a pre-pandemic norm of 1-2 days, and spot ocean freight rates (Drewry World Container Index) spiked to $10,377 per 40-foot container in September 2021, roughly 7x pre-pandemic levels. By essentially every one of these measures the acute crisis had cleared by late 2022 into 2024: the SoCal ship queue reached zero for the first time in 25 months in November 2022 and has stayed negligible since; dwell times returned to near pre-pandemic levels by 2024; and freight rates fell 79-86% from their peak by 2023-2025, though they remain somewhat above pre-pandemic averages. A resurgence of congestion risk appeared in 2024-2025 (East/Gulf Coast ILA strike threat, Red Sea/Suez diversions, tariff-driven import surges), but this has been episodic and far short of the 2021-2022 scale, even as US ports have gone on to set new all-time volume records (LA: 10.3M TEUs in 2024; Long Beach: a record 9.9M TEUs in 2025).

How big was the crisis — the numbers

  • Ship backlog: Vessels waiting at anchor off Los Angeles/Long Beach rose from an average of roughly 30 at mid-2021 to an all-time high of 109 on January 9, 2022 — versus essentially 0-1 pre-pandemic. Combined ships at anchor plus at berth hit 100+ on peak days, over 5x pre-COVID norms.
  • Dwell times: Truck-bound import containers averaged 8.4 days at their November 2021 peak; rail-bound containers peaked at 16.5 days in August 2022 (Pacific Merchant Shipping Association / gCaptain reporting), versus a historical norm close to 2 days.
  • Freight rates: The Drewry World Container Index (a composite of major trade lanes, most directly relevant to transpacific routes into US ports) peaked at $10,377 per 40-foot-equivalent container the week of September 2021 — roughly 7.3x the 2019 pre-pandemic average of about $1,420.
  • Economic cost: Global supply disruptions (of which US port congestion was a major contributor) are estimated by the IMF to have shaved about 0.5 percentage points off global growth in 2021; the NBER and Richmond Fed attribute roughly a quarter of 2021-2022 US inflation to supply-chain/international factors; sector estimates such as AlixPartners' put the global semiconductor-shortage cost to the auto industry alone at about $210 billion in lost 2022 revenue.
  • Volume did not fall: Top-10 US port container throughput (BTS Port Performance data, queried directly from the connector) rose from about 3.6-3.9 million TEUs/month in 2019 to a peak of 4.71 million TEUs in May 2022 — the crisis was demand outrunning available vessel, terminal, chassis, warehouse, trucking and rail capacity simultaneously, not a volume collapse.

How much has cleared

  • Ship queue: The SoCal backlog eased steadily after its January 2022 peak, was in the single digits by mid-September 2022, and reached zero for the first time in 25 months in November 2022 (Marine Exchange of Southern California data, reported by FreightWaves/Long Beach Business Journal). No significant vessel back-up recurred in 2024.
  • Dwell times: By April 2024, truck-bound dwell time averaged 2.50 days and rail-bound cargo 4.55 days — close to or at historic pre-pandemic norms, per the Pacific Merchant Shipping Association. A brief rail-congestion spike to a two-year high occurred around the October 2024 holiday-import surge (CNBC), but well below 2021-2022 severity.
  • Freight rates: The Drewry WCI fell 86% from its September 2021 peak by September 2023 ($1,479/FEU) and remained 79% below peak in March 2025 ($2,168/FEU) — still roughly 53% above the 2019 pre-pandemic average, so rates have normalized substantially but not completely.
  • Not fully back to "boring": Volume itself never receded and has since set new records (LA 10.3M TEUs in 2024, up ~20% year over year; Long Beach 9.6M TEUs in 2024 then a record 9.9M in 2025), and 2024-2025 brought renewed episodic stress from the October 2024 ILA East/Gulf Coast strike threat, Red Sea/Suez diversions forcing re-routing around Africa, and tariff-driven import front-loading — but none of these approached the scale, duration, or vessel-queue severity of the 2021-2022 crisis.

Data and methodology notes

Container-throughput figures for 2019-2022 are queried directly from the AskAmerica connector's transport.bts_port_teu table (BTS Port Performance Freight Statistics Program, Top-10 US container ports). This is the best freely available bulk time series found; the industry-standard NRF/Hackett Global Port Tracker publishes only point figures in press releases with no downloadable series, and this connector table's own source dataset has not been updated past October 2022 — so no connector-native series exists for 2023-2026 port volumes. All ship-queue, dwell-time, freight-rate, and 2023-2026 volume-record figures come from cited news and industry sources (Marine Exchange of Southern California via FreightWaves, Pacific Merchant Shipping Association/gCaptain, Drewry, Port of LA/Long Beach official releases) rather than the connector, and are labeled as such rather than presented as connector-derived.
