The 2021-22 US port crisis was historic, fully cleared by late 2022, but a new supply-chain shock hit in 2026

Vessel queues, freight rates, dwell times, and inflation impact — peak vs. current state

Summary

The 2021-2022 US port crisis was the worst in modern shipping history: at its Los Angeles/Long Beach peak in January 2022, 109 container ships sat waiting offshore (versus essentially zero in normal years), average import container dwell time roughly doubled, and global container freight rates spiked to $10,377 per 40-foot box (Drewry WCI), about 7x the pre-pandemic average. Economists at the San Francisco Fed estimate supply-chain pressure drove roughly 60% of the above-trend surge in US inflation in 2021-22. That crisis is fully cleared on the metric that defined it — the vessel backlog hit zero in mid-November 2022 for the first time in 25 months, and LA/Long Beach have since handled record cargo volumes (2024 was their second-busiest year ever, just 9,000 containers short of the 2021 record) with no congestion. Freight rates settled roughly 85% below peak by 2023-24. However, the system is not back to a fully calm baseline: a new, distinct disruption — the 2026 Iran war and the resulting collapse of Strait of Hormuz shipping traffic — pushed the NY Fed's Global Supply Chain Pressure Index back up to 1.82 in April 2026, the highest reading since July 2022, and container rates have climbed again toward $4,500/FEU. So: the pandemic-era port crisis, specifically, is over; broader global supply-chain pressure is not currently at a calm baseline, for a different and unrelated reason.

How big was it

  • Vessel backlog: the San Pedro Bay (LA/Long Beach) queue rose from ~9 ships in mid-2021 to 73 by September 2021, then to 101-109 ships by January 2022 — the historic peak.
  • Dwell time: rail-bound import dwell time at San Pedro Bay peaked around 16.5 days in August 2022, versus about 12.4 days in April 2021 and a few days pre-pandemic.
  • Freight rates: the Drewry World Container Index peaked at $10,377/FEU in September 2021, versus a 2019 pre-pandemic average of about $1,420 — roughly a 7x spike.
  • Volume: LA alone moved a then-record 10.7 million TEUs in 2021 as pandemic-era demand for goods (rather than services) overwhelmed capacity.
  • Macroeconomic cost: the San Francisco Fed attributes about 60% of the 2021-22 above-trend rise in US inflation to global supply-chain pressure, and estimates a one-standard-deviation GSCPI shock raises imported-goods inflation by up to 0.9 percentage points at peak.

How much has cleared

  • Vessel queue: fully cleared. The backlog hit zero for the first time in 25 months in mid-November 2022 and has stayed structurally low since — LA/Long Beach now report no congestion or disruption even while moving record cargo.
  • Dwell time: much improved. Comparing October 2024 to April 2021, the twin ports and their rail partners handled 12% more volume while cutting dwell times by roughly 20%.
  • Freight rates: mostly normalized, then re-elevated. Rates fell about 85-90% from the 2021 peak by 2023-24 (to roughly the low-$1,000s-$1,500 range), but by August 2026 the WCI was back up to about $4,526/FEU — still 56% below the 2021 peak, but well above pre-pandemic norms and rising, driven by the Iran war/Strait of Hormuz disruption rather than a repeat of the pandemic-specific port bottleneck.
  • Volume/capacity: cleared and then some. 2024 was LA/Long Beach's second-best year ever (roughly 10.1-10.3 million TEUs combined), and Long Beach was tracking a new all-time record through 2025, evidence the ports absorbed pandemic-era demand growth without recreating the 2021-22 bottleneck.
  • Labor risk: resolved through 2030. The October 2024 East/Gulf Coast ILA dockworker strike (which cost the economy an estimated $2 billion in three days) was settled with a new six-year master contract ratified in February 2025, running through September 30, 2030, removing that near-term disruption risk.

What has NOT cleared

A genuinely new and separate shock emerged in 2026: the Iran war and the resulting near-collapse of Strait of Hormuz shipping (daily crossings fell from 70-80 pre-conflict to single digits by March 2026), which forced major carriers (Maersk, MSC, CMA CGM, Hapag-Lloyd) to reroute around the Cape of Good Hope, adding 10-20 days of transit time and pushing freight rates up as much as 50% on affected lanes. This drove the NY Fed's Global Supply Chain Pressure Index to 1.82 in April 2026, its highest level since July 2022's pandemic-era 1.86 reading. This is a distinct, geopolitically-driven disruption — not a resurgence of the 2021-22 container-terminal congestion crisis — but it means that by the broadest supply-chain-pressure measure, the system is again under real strain in 2026, even though US container ports themselves are running efficiently.

Data notes

AskAmerica's econ.trade_imports table (Census monthly import data) was queried for a US import-value time series but returned usable world-total rows for only 2022 and 2025 within its declared 2013-2025 coverage window — evidently a coverage gap for the intervening years — so it was not used as a load-bearing figure here; the port-specific and freight-rate figures above come from the cited news/industry/Fed sources instead. This tool has no Python sandbox, so no independent recomputation of the WCI or GSCPI indices was possible; the reported values are taken directly from Drewry and the NY Fed as published.
