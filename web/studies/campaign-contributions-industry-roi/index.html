No consistent link between 2024 campaign giving and post-election industry performance

FEC direct PAC contributions to winning 2024 candidates, matched against BLS sector employment growth through mid-2026

Summary

No clean pattern emerges. Looking at the industries that gave the most direct PAC money to candidates who won the 2024 federal elections, and tracking one broad performance proxy — national employment — from just before the election (Nov 2024) through the most recent published month (Jul 2026), the top-giving sectors land on both sides of the economy-wide trend. Construction (helped by home-builder/realtor money) grew jobs +4.0% while the whole economy shrank -0.5%; but Trade/Transportation/Retail (helped by auto-dealer and beer-wholesaler money) fell -2.7 to -2.5%, and Information/telecom (AT&T, Comcast were large donors) fell -3.8%, both underperforming the overall economy. Financial services (banks, credit unions, realtors, insurers — a heavy donor bloc) essentially matched the economy-wide trend. There is no monotonic 'pay-to-play' payoff visible in this outcome measure over this window, though other outcome measures (stock returns, policy wins, lobbying access) could tell a different story than employment does, and published academic work on stock-market reactions to political giving does find abnormal returns around the election itself even where the real economy shows nothing so clean.

What "gave the most" measures here, and why that matters

This report measures direct PAC-to-candidate contributions (FEC transaction type 24K, from fec.committee_contributions) made during the 2023-2024 cycle to candidates who went on to win — the 119th Congress's House and Senate members (matched by name/state/district against officials.members, congress=119) plus the winning presidential candidate. That channel totals $394 million to eventual winners.

This is a minority of total industry political spending. Committees with no declared connected_org_name (leadership PACs, non-connected PACs, and — critically — the super PACs and outside-spending vehicles that carried the largest sums in 2024) account for roughly $84M of the $394M in this same 24K channel alone, and the true outside-spending channel (independent expenditures, electioneering communications) is not captured by this query at all. For example, published reporting (OpenSecrets, via Yale Climate Connections) puts oil & gas industry total 2024 election spending at $219 million — of which only about $26 million went to winning candidates as direct/PAC contributions comparable to what this report measures, with the other roughly $151 million flowing through outside-spending vehicles this warehouse's 24K query cannot see. So a ranking built only on 24K contributions is a real but partial picture, and the largest 2024 donor blocs by total spending (crypto industry via Fairshake-aligned super PACs, oil & gas outside spending) are effectively invisible in a 24K-only ranking.

Within that 24K channel, the largest named single-organization givers to winners were trade/membership groups and a mix of finance, real estate, healthcare, defense and telecom corporations: National Association of Realtors ($3.76M), National Beer Wholesalers Association ($2.48M), American Crystal Sugar ($2.40M), National Automobile Dealers Association ($2.29M), Credit Union National Association ($2.20M), American Bankers Association ($2.17M), and — among individual corporations — Home Depot, AT&T, Comcast, Honeywell, UBS Americas, UPS, Koch Inc., Boeing, Lockheed Martin, General Dynamics, Northrop Grumman and RTX (five defense primes alone gave roughly $5.0M combined to winners). By broad PAC category, corporate PACs (org_type='C') gave $112.6M to winners, trade associations $64.0M, labor unions $36.2M, and membership organizations $28.8M.

Measuring "did better afterwards"

There is no single government table of industry stock returns or profit in this warehouse with a post-2024 window long enough to be meaningful, so this report uses national employment by NAICS supersector (BLS State and Metro Area Employment series, econ.state_industry, summed across all 50 states + DC) as the outcome proxy, comparing November 2024 (the election month) to July 2026 (the latest month loaded). Employment growth is an imperfect stand-in for "industry performance" — it says nothing directly about profit margins, stock prices, or whether an industry got the specific policy outcome it lobbied for — but it is a broad, government-measured, industry-resolved series available for the full post-election window, and it is not subject to the volatility a two-day stock-price event study would carry.

Sectors carrying heavy donor money split roughly evenly around the -0.49% economy-wide baseline: construction, healthcare and (marginally) financial services outperformed it; retail, trade/transportation, telecom and public-sector employment (tied to the heavily-donating public employee unions) underperformed it, several by a wide margin. Durable-goods manufacturing — the segment carrying the big defense-contractor donors — grew a marginal +0.16%, essentially flat and slightly ahead of total manufacturing but not a standout.

What the published literature says

This is a genuinely studied question, and the research points in a more nuanced direction than a simple employment comparison can capture. Cochran & Ovtchinnikov (Public Choice, 2016) find that firms whose PACs gave a higher share of contributions to the eventual presidential winner, and gave more in total, earned significant abnormal stock returns in the days immediately following presidential elections (1992-2004) — a short-window financial-market effect, not a multi-year real-economy one. Post-2024, multiple studies (ScienceDirect, e.g. "How CEO donations drive stock prices: evidence from the Trump 2024 election") likewise found CEO-level political giving associated with abnormal stock price moves right around the election. Reporting also confirms fossil-fuel/"brown" industries and small-cap equities saw notably positive abnormal returns in the immediate post-election window. None of this is inconsistent with what this report finds: a short, sharp market reaction to an election result is a different phenomenon from a multi-year change in an industry's underlying employment or output, and academic commentary (e.g., E*TRADE's 2024 election research) explicitly cautions that the business cycle, not the election, dominates medium-run performance. In other words: donor-favored industries may well have gotten an immediate stock-price bump around the election, but that is not the same claim as "did better" in the broader, sustained sense this question could also mean, and on that broader employment-based measure, no consistent advantage shows up.

Caveats

  • Channel limitation: this only measures direct PAC-to-candidate giving (24K). Outside spending — which was several times larger for industries like oil & gas and decisive for others like crypto — is not attributable to a candidate by industry in this warehouse and is excluded, so the donor ranking above is necessarily partial.
  • Winner matching is a name/state/district match between FEC candidates and the 119th Congress roster (plus the winning presidential candidate), not an FEC-native "won" flag; it matched 463 of the ~469 expected winners and could contain a small number of mismatches.
  • Employment is not profit or stock return. An industry's payroll count can grow or shrink for reasons entirely unrelated to political favor (automation, interest rates, weather, tariffs), and this report does not attempt to isolate a causal donor effect — it reports a correlation check over one proxy outcome, not a regression-based causal estimate.
  • Window: Nov 2024 to Jul 2026 is roughly 20 months of a 4-year presidential term and about 8 months since the new Congress and administration were sworn in; a longer post-election window could show a different pattern.
  • The BLS employment series used (econ.state_industry) is not seasonally adjusted; comparing the same calendar-adjacent months (Nov and Jul are 8 months apart within the year) introduces some residual seasonal noise not fully controlled for here.
