Illinois, Kentucky, New Jersey and Connecticut carry the deepest state pension funding gaps — and Illinois' bonds are priced for it

Pew/Equable pension-funding data, S&P/Moody's rating actions, and peer-reviewed muni-market research, fetched 2026-09-15. AskAmerica's own warehouse carries neither pension liabilities nor municipal bond series.

Summary

Yes on both counts. The states with the largest gap between promised and actually-funded pensions are Kentucky (50% funded), Illinois (50.9%), New Jersey (53.5%), Connecticut (57.8%) and South Carolina (59.9%) as of FY2023 — well below the roughly 78% national state average — and nationally, states are carrying about $1.32 trillion in unfunded state pension liabilities (Pew, FY2023). A fuller 2012-2016 Pew series (via Ballotpedia) shows this is not a one-year blip: Illinois, Kentucky, New Jersey and Connecticut have all sat at or below roughly 30-50% funded for the better part of a decade. This gap does show up directly in how the worst-funded states' bonds are priced. As of Bond Buyer's February 2023 reporting, Illinois' 10-year general obligation bonds traded at a 163 basis point spread to the AAA municipal benchmark — the widest of any state — while New Jersey, similarly underfunded but with a stronger rating trajectory, traded 128 basis points narrower. S&P and Moody's have both named Illinois' pension funded ratio explicitly as a rating driver, in both directions: a 2013 downgrade cited "weakened pension funded ratios," and recent upgrades (2021, 2022, 2023, and Moody's October 2025 move to A2) cite accelerated pension contributions and slowing statutory pension-funding growth as the reason ratings improved. A peer-reviewed academic study (Beetsma, Lekniute & Ponds, Journal of Empirical Finance, 2019) confirms this is systematic across the whole municipal market, not just an Illinois story: since the 2008 financial crisis, tax-adjusted municipal yields for lower-credit-quality states have risen roughly 10–20 basis points for every 1 percentage point of state GDP lost to pension underfunding.

1. Which states have the biggest promised-vs-funded gap

AskAmerica's own warehouse has no table for pension liabilities or funded ratios — confirmed via search_catalog, which returned zero matches for "pension" or "liabilities" against every schema (econ, fiscal, sec, etc.). This is a genuine gap in the corpus, not a missed lookup, so the figures below are drawn entirely from external sources.

Two current trackers agree on the same handful of chronically underfunded states. Pew Charitable Trusts reports the aggregate 50-state funded ratio (plan assets ÷ actuarial accrued liabilities) at 74–78% in FY2023, with a total funding gap of $1.32 trillion, up $44.7 billion from FY2022 — driven mainly by benefit changes and demographic/investment assumptions not matching actual experience. The Equable Institute's state-by-state FY2023 rankings put five states under 60% funded: South Carolina (59.9%), Connecticut (57.8%), New Jersey (53.5%), Illinois (50.9%) and Kentucky (50%). On a per-capita unfunded-liability basis, the Reason Foundation separately names Illinois, Connecticut, Alaska, Hawaii, New Jersey and Mississippi as carrying the largest per-resident pension debt — largely the same cohort, confirming the finding is not an artifact of one methodology.

The gap is also long-standing, not a recent shock. Pew's own historical funded-ratio series (2012-2016, tabulated by Ballotpedia) shows Illinois falling from 40% (2012) to 36% (2016) funded with total pension debt of $141 billion against a $219 billion liability by 2016; New Jersey collapsed from 65% (2012) to just 31% (2016) funded, only paying 33% of its own actuarially recommended contribution that year; Kentucky fell from 47% (2012) to 31% (2016) funded. Contemporaneously (Feb 2023), S&P still described Illinois' funded ratio as "just 44%" with $139 billion in unfunded pension liabilities plus a separate $35 billion retiree-healthcare shortfall — meaning even after several years of fiscal repair, the underlying gap those bonds are priced against remains enormous.

2. Does the gap show up in bond pricing

Yes, in both a directly documented case study and a systematic academic finding.

Case study — Illinois and New Jersey. As of Bond Buyer's February 2023 reporting (the S&P upgrade that lifted Illinois to A-minus), Illinois traded at the widest spread among all 50 states: 163 basis points over the Municipal Market Data AAA benchmark, and 125 basis points above the single-A benchmark — despite that same upgrade. New Jersey, carrying an A-minus from S&P and A2 from Moody's at the time, traded "in line with the single-A benchmark" and was 128 basis points narrower than Illinois' 10-year, per Refinitiv data cited in the same article — even though New Jersey's own funded ratio (53.5%) was, and remains, close to Illinois' (50.9%). The gap between the two states' spreads reflects Illinois carrying the larger absolute unfunded liability ($139B) and a longer history of missed statutory funding, on top of a similar funded-ratio starting point.

Rating agencies name the pension mechanism explicitly, in both directions. S&P's 2013 downgrade of Illinois stated the action "reflects what we view as the state's weakened pension funded ratios and lack of action on reform measures," citing an aggregate actuarial funded ratio that had fallen from 43.4% (FY2011) to 40.4% (FY2012). Conversely, S&P's February 2023 upgrade to A-minus explicitly credited "accelerating repayment of its liabilities" and "a slowing of statutory pension funding growth" as drivers, while still flagging that "the state's burdensome $35 billion retiree healthcare tab and unfunded pension liabilities of $139 billion — with a weak funded ratio of just 44% — still weigh heavily on the state's balance sheet and ratings." Moody's most recent action (October 2025, upgrading Illinois to A2 from A3, its fourth upgrade since 2020) again cited improved financial metrics while flagging the state's "high debt profile and its fixed cost burden" — i.e., pension legacy costs remain the binding constraint on the rating even as it improves. Connecticut and New Jersey followed a similar pattern: recent ramped-up pension contributions have been "a direct driver of credit improvement for both states," and New Jersey has received six rating upgrades in roughly 13 months.

Systematic evidence across the whole market. A peer-reviewed study tests this relationship across the full municipal market rather than one state at a time: Beetsma, Lekniute & Ponds, "U.S. Municipal Yields and Unfunded State Pension Liabilities," Journal of Empirical Finance 53 (2019): 15–32. Their finding: municipal bond yields are increasing in a state's unfunded pension debt, but only since the 2008 financial crisis — before that, the market appears not to have priced pension underfunding at all, consistent with the crisis "triggering awareness of budgetary sustainability." For states in the lower half of the credit-quality spectrum, tax-adjusted municipal spreads rose roughly 10–20 basis points for every 1 percentage point of state GDP lost to pension underfunding. The marginal effect of pension debt is smaller than that of explicit (bonded) debt, but the authors describe it as "economically meaningful" — and notably stronger when pension assets are valued at market rather than smoothed actuarial values, suggesting investors look through the actuarial averaging that funded-ratio headlines are built on.

3. Caveats

  • Data source: Every figure in this report is drawn from web research (Pew, Equable, Reason Foundation, Ballotpedia's tabulation of Pew's historical data, Bond Buyer, Western Asset, and the Beetsma/Lekniute/Ponds paper) because AskAmerica's own warehouse carries no pension-liability or municipal-bond-yield table — confirmed by search_catalog before researching externally.
  • Correlation vs. isolated causal estimate: The Illinois case is a clear instance of rating agencies naming pension underfunding as a cause in their own published rationale, in both downgrade and upgrade directions — about as direct as evidence gets. The Beetsma et al. coefficient is a regression estimate controlling for explicit debt and other state fundamentals, not a randomized or natural-experiment design — it cannot fully rule out that some third factor (e.g., overall fiscal management quality) drives both weak pension funding and wider spreads simultaneously.
  • Vintage mismatch: Funded ratios cited are FY2023 (most recent full 50-state roundup available); the specific 163bp/128bp Illinois-New Jersey spread figures are from February 2023, coincident with that funded-ratio vintage. Both states' credit ratings have moved since (Illinois to A2 at Moody's as of October 2025), so current spreads are almost certainly narrower than 163bps, though Illinois still traded at the widest spread among states in early 2023 and remains the lower-rated of the two.
