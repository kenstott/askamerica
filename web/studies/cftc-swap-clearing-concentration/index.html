Interest-rate swap clearing is extremely concentrated: one CCP (LCH SwapClear) dominates worldwide, and within every CCP, a handful of bank clearing members carries most of the risk

CCPView/Clarus FT Q2 2026 cleared volumes; BIS OTC derivatives statistics; Chicago Fed WP 2019-06

Summary

Yes — interest-rate swap clearing is concentrated at two distinct levels, and both are extreme by normal competition-policy standards. At the central-counterparty (CCP) level, one firm, LCH SwapClear (part of LSEG), clears roughly 95% of the world's vanilla interest-rate swap volume, and in the two biggest currencies (USD and AUD) its share of Q2 2026 cleared notional was above 95%, translating to Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI) values above 9,000 out of a possible 10,000 — a single-firm monopoly in all but name. Only in yen has real competition emerged, where Japan's JSCC has clawed its way to roughly 48% of TONA OIS volume. At the clearing-member level — the banks that stand between end users and the CCP and would absorb a member default — concentration is almost as stark: the five largest bank clearing members contribute more than a third of the prefunded default resources CCPs hold against interest-rate derivatives (and roughly half for credit derivatives), and in the US cleared-swaps market the five largest futures commission merchants (FCMs) hold over half of all client margin. So the honest answer is: down to essentially one clearinghouse globally, and within that clearinghouse, down to a handful of banks.

For reference, the US Department of Justice/FTC Horizontal Merger Guidelines treat an HHI above 2,500 as "highly concentrated"; every major currency here is 2-3.5x that line except yen, where JSCC's rise (partly a consequence of the EU's post-Brexit 'Active Account Requirement' pushing volume away from London-domiciled clearing, and separately JSCC's home-market advantage) has actually produced a near-duopoly. Overall, LSEG/LCH itself states SwapClear clears about 95% of the world's cleared vanilla OTC interest-rate swap volume across 28 currencies, with over US$70 trillion cleared in USD alone in Q2 2026. Separately, BIS's semiannual OTC derivatives statistics show that centrally cleared trades are about 78-80% of all interest-rate derivatives outstanding (globally, cleared vs. bilateral) — so on top of a high clearing rate, nearly all of that cleared volume runs through one clearinghouse.

Member-level concentration: a handful of banks carry the risk within the CCP

Concentration doesn't stop at the CCP. A CCP itself does not take market risk — it mutualizes the risk of its clearing members' defaults through margin and a default fund. BIS research (cited via Chicago Fed WP 2019-06, drawing on CPMI-IOSCO and CFTC data) finds that the top five clearing-member banks contribute roughly half of prefunded default resources for cleared credit derivatives and more than a third for interest-rate derivatives globally. In the US specifically, the five largest futures commission merchants (FCMs) hold more than half of all client margin posted for cleared derivatives. Regulators (FSB, CPMI-IOSCO, BCBS) have flagged this dual concentration — a small number of CCPs, each in turn dependent on a small number of large dealer banks as clearing members — as a genuine source of systemic risk: if a CCP has only a few large members, its capacity to manage a member default in an orderly way (via default-fund replenishment or auctioning the defaulter's book) is itself limited by how many other large members are available to absorb it.

Why AskAmerica's own tables could not answer this directly

This connector's cftc schema carries the same CFTC Part 43 public-dissemination swap data that underlies much of the public record on this market (cftc.rate_swaps, cftc.clearing_activity, cftc.swap_activity). It cannot support CCP-concentration analysis by design: Part 43 explicitly excludes the CCP-facing "beta/gamma" novation leg of a cleared trade from public dissemination, so the cleared flag is structurally near-empty in this feed regardless of true clearing volume, and no field identifies which CCP a trade cleared through even when it is populated. That is a known limitation of the public swap-data-repository feed itself, not a defect in how this warehouse loaded it — genuine CCP-level concentration data lives only in proprietary aggregators (e.g. Clarus FT's CCPView, which licenses CCP-reported volumes directly) and in BIS/FSB/CPMI-IOSCO published statistics, both of which this answer draws on instead.
