No — over 1994-2023, immigrants' taxes exceeded the cost of the services they used; the exception is education-driven state/local costs from first-generation arrivals

Synthesis of NAS/NASEM 2017 methodology, Cato's 2024 update (1994-2023), CBO's 2024 immigration-surge estimate, and the FAIR/Cato/ITEP state-cost dispute

Summary

No, taken as a whole and over roughly the last ten to thirty years, immigrants have not cost more than they have added. Every peer-reviewed and government estimate of the FULL picture (all levels of government, all immigrants, taxes AND economic output) finds a net positive: the National Academies of Sciences' definitive 2017 study, updated by the Cato Institute through 2023, finds a cumulative fiscal SURPLUS of $14.5 trillion (2024 dollars) from 1994-2023 — taxes exceeded benefit costs in every single year. CBO similarly finds the 2021-2024 immigration surge REDUCES federal deficits by $0.9 trillion over 2024-2034 and adds $8.9 trillion to GDP. The one place the answer flips to a genuine net COST is state and local budgets, for first-generation immigrants specifically — driven almost entirely by the cost of educating their children in K-12 schools. Those children (the second generation), once grown, become net contributors themselves. Claims that immigration is a large net drain (e.g. FAIR's $150.7B/year figure) rest on contested assumptions that most other economists, including a detailed Cato rebuttal using FAIR's own inputs, do not accept.

What the research actually measures, and why estimates diverge so much

There is no single US government dataset that tags an individual tax return, Medicaid claim, or school enrollment record by immigration status — the AskAmerica connector's fiscal, census, and health schemas were checked directly (fiscal.govt_finance_by_unit, fiscal.ssa_benefits_by_geography, fiscal.snap_benefits_by_geography, census.acs_nativity, census.acs_income_by_nativity) and none carries that cross-tab at the tax/benefit-record level. Every dollar figure anyone cites, including the ones below, comes from a microsimulation: survey data on who is foreign-born (Current Population Survey or ACS) combined with government revenue/expenditure totals, allocated to individuals under modeling assumptions. That is why the numbers vary by 5-45x depending on the study — the disagreement is almost entirely about ASSUMPTIONS, not facts:

  • Unit of analysis: household head only (understates cost, since a household with a citizen head and undocumented spouse counts as 'native') vs. independent-person-plus-dependents (the NAS/Cato approach, generally considered the more careful standard).
  • Whether the extra economic activity immigrants generate raises OTHERS' tax payments too. Cato's critique of FAIR's 2023 estimate ($182B gross cost) is that FAIR counts 100% of enforcement/education costs as attributable to immigrants but doesn't count the added economic activity (higher wages/output for complementary native workers) that generates additional tax revenue elsewhere in the economy — by Cato's recalculation using FAIR's own inputs, correcting this reduces FAIR's claimed net cost from ~$116B to $3.3-15.6B.
  • Population base. FAIR uses its own estimate of 15.5 million undocumented immigrants rather than Census Bureau counts, which mechanically inflates every per-capita cost figure.
  • Time horizon. A first-generation adult immigrant with limited education is a net state/local cost over a static, single-year snapshot; the same person's US-born children, followed into adulthood, are a net contributor. Static, single-year studies (like FAIR's) capture only the cost side of that lifecycle; multi-generation studies (NAS, Cato) capture both.

Why the answer differs by government level, and by generation

The consistent pattern across every serious study, regardless of its politics, is: federal budget = net positive; state/local budget for first-generation arrivals specifically = net cost, driven by K-12 education; second-generation-and-beyond = net positive again. This is a mechanical, not ideological, result: federal programs (Social Security, Medicare, Medicaid at the federal share) are funded heavily by payroll and income taxes, which most immigrants (including a majority of undocumented workers, per ITEP) pay into; K-12 education, the largest state/local budget line item nationally, is funded overwhelmingly by state/local governments and its costs fall immediately when an immigrant family arrives with school-age children, while the tax contributions those children will make as adults arrive only 15-20 years later. A single-year or short-window snapshot of a high-immigration state will therefore show a state/local fiscal cost that a 20-30 year, multi-generation view does not.

State variation matters and the corpus data confirms it directly: California, Texas, New York, Florida and New Jersey hold the largest foreign-born populations and carry the largest absolute K-12 cost exposure from immigrant households, but ACS 2023 data (queried from census.acs_income_by_nativity) shows foreign-born households in these same states earn 82-94% of the native-born median household income — they are not a low-income population overall, which is part of why FAIR's assumption of heavy net benefit usage is contested. Nationally, noncitizen households grew from 7.45 million in 2013 to 7.91 million in 2023 (+6%) while naturalized-citizen households grew from 8.81 million to 11.96 million (+36%) over the same decade — the population is shifting toward the naturalized/longer-tenured status that the literature associates with strongly positive fiscal contribution, per census.acs_income_by_nativity.

Bottom line

Over the last ten to thirty years, taking all levels of government together, immigrants as a whole have added far more in taxes and economic output than they have cost in public services — a $14.5 trillion cumulative surplus since 1994 by the most comprehensive available estimate, reinforced by CBO's recent finding that the 2021-2024 immigration surge is reducing, not increasing, federal deficits. The genuine exception, found consistently across studies including ones favorable to immigration overall, is state and local government costs specifically for first-generation immigrants, driven by K-12 education — a cost that reverses once their US-born children reach working age. Claims of a large aggregate net cost (FAIR's $150.7B/year) rely on assumptions (no macroeconomic feedback, self-estimated population counts, 100%-attributed enforcement costs) that a rebuttal using the same underlying data, and the broader academic literature, does not support.
