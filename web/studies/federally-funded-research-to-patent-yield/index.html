Federal Research-Agency and University Invention Productivity: NSF and NIH's Biomedical Institutes Lead by Patents per Dollar; Carnegie Mellon, Purdue and Utah Lead Among Universities

Battelle/NIH federal-agency patent study (2000-2013) + AUTM FY2022 US Licensing Activity Survey + AskAmerica NSF NCSES / FedInvent cross-checks

Summary

By federal agency: NIH's disease-focused institutes vary enormously — NIBIB (biomedical imaging/bioengineering) and NIGMS (general medical sciences) are the most patent-productive federal R&D programs measured (24.9 and 13.4 patents per $100M respectively, nearly matching private-sector R&D efficiency), while NIH overall averages 5.9 patents/$100M. Across whole agencies, NSF (10.4/$100M) and DOE/NIST (8.7/$100M) out-produce NIH (5.9/$100M), and DOD trails all of them at 2.5 patents/$100M — roughly a quarter of NSF's rate, because DOD R&D funding is weighted toward late-stage development and testing rather than patentable basic/applied research. A rough current-day (FY2022 funding vs. 2025 patent-grant) cross-check built from AskAmerica's NSF and FedInvent data reproduces the DOD (2.5) and NIH (~6.0) rates almost exactly, and shows NSF's rate has if anything grown since 2013 (~16/$100M today vs. 10.4 historically).

By university: Using AUTM's FY2022 US Licensing Activity Survey (research expenditure and invention-disclosure counts for ~39 major research universities), Carnegie Mellon, Purdue and the University of Utah post the highest invention-disclosure rates per research dollar (6.2, 5.0 and 4.6 disclosures per $10M spent respectively) — 4-6x the rate of the lowest performers in the same size class (Boston University, UNC Chapel Hill, UC San Francisco, each near 1-1.5 per $10M). MIT and Harvard, despite huge absolute output (698 and 376 disclosures), rank mid-pack on a per-dollar basis (3.6 and 4.0 per $10M) once their much larger research budgets ($1.9B and $0.94B) are accounted for — size alone does not predict efficiency.

The pattern the study attributes this to: agencies/institutes whose funding is concentrated in fundamental, broadly-applicable research (materials science and engineering at NSF/DOE; enabling technologies like imaging/bioengineering at NIBIB) generate more patentable output per dollar than agencies whose R&D budgets are dominated by large-scale systems development, testing and evaluation (DOD) or by late-stage clinical/disease-specific research with a longer, riskier path to a patentable invention (NIH's disease institutes on average).

Independent cross-check with AskAmerica data. Pairing recent FedInvent tallies of 2025-issued patents citing each agency's funding (NSF 1,121; DOD 1,820; HHS/NIH 2,630) against FY2022 federal R&D obligations by agency (queried from research.nsf_federal_rd_obligations: DOD $72.35B, HHS $74.10B of which NIH alone is $43.59B, NSF $6.90B) reproduces the historical ranking almost exactly: DOD ≈ 2.5 patents/$100M (identical to the 2000-2013 figure), NIH ≈ 6.0/$100M (vs. 5.9 historically) — a genuine consistency check, not a coincidence, since both draw on the same iEdison/USPTO funding-acknowledgment mechanism Battelle used. NSF's implied current rate (~16/$100M) is noticeably higher than its 2000-2013 average, consistent with NSF's funding mix shifting further toward commercializable engineering and computer science over the last decade; because patents granted in a given year reflect grants funded years earlier, this ratio should be read as directional, not as this year's true marginal productivity.

University-level productivity — data and method

AUTM's annual US Licensing Activity Survey is the standard industry source for university technology-transfer output; the FY2022 edition (published 2023, based on ~200 responding institutions, cross-referenced to NSF's HERD research-expenditure survey) reports each institution's total research expenditures alongside its invention disclosures, patent applications and issued patents for the year. AskAmerica fetched the survey's full institution table directly and computed each university's disclosures per $10M of research spending — the standard denominator technology-transfer offices themselves use for peer benchmarking (AUTM's own report groups institutions into six research-expenditure "peer groups" for exactly this reason, since a $2B-budget campus and a $50M-budget campus are not comparable on raw counts).

Ranking by issued patents per $10M instead of disclosures (a stricter, later-stage output measure) largely confirms the same leaders — Carnegie Mellon (0.29/$10M), Arizona State (0.26), Harvard (0.25), University of Colorado (0.22) and Stanford/Caltech (0.21 each) top that list — with Colorado's high issued-patent rate (28 patents on $1.27B) standing out as a case where the disclosure-to-issued-patent conversion rate is unusually strong.

Two mechanisms plausibly explain why smaller, more engineering-concentrated research portfolios (Carnegie Mellon, Purdue, Colorado School of Mines-adjacent programs) out-produce some of the largest medical-research-heavy budgets (Boston University, UNC, UCSF, Vanderbilt) on a per-dollar basis: (1) medical/clinical and public-health research (a large share of BU, UNC and Vanderbilt's portfolios) is less often directly patentable than engineering/physical-science research, and (2) the same NIH-institute pattern found at the federal level — enabling/engineering-adjacent biomedical work (NIBIB) vastly out-patents disease-specific clinical research — likely repeats itself inside university budgets that are heavily clinical-trial-weighted.

Caveats

  • Vintage mismatch. The agency figures are a 2000-2013 average; the university figures are a single fiscal year (FY2022). Patents granted in a given year reflect research funded several years earlier in both cases, so none of these ratios are a clean same-year input/output measure — they are the standard proxy the field itself uses (Battelle, AUTM, FedInvent, NCSES all report it the same way), not a claim about instantaneous marginal returns.
  • Invention disclosures ≠ patents ≠ commercial value. A disclosure is an internal report of a possible invention, most of which never become issued patents, and issued patents vary enormously in commercial value; per-dollar rates measure activity, not value created. A separate strand of research (cited in general search: Sichel/Åstebro-style "full-cost accounting" studies) finds that most US research universities lose money net on patenting once legal costs are included, which is a different question from patent-output efficiency addressed here.
  • Sample coverage. The university table above uses ~39 of AUTM's ~200 FY2022 respondents (institutions with research spend roughly $350M+, chosen for name-recognition and data completeness), not the full survey population; AUTM's own official benchmarking table (Peer Group 1, n=64 institutions >$554M) reports very similar overall central tendencies (average 261 disclosures per institution in that top peer group) and is cited directly in the report as a cross-check on the sample used here.
  • AskAmerica connector limitation encountered. An attempt to compute university-level patents-per-R&D-dollar directly inside the connector — joining research.nsf_herd_by_institution's federal R&D dollars to patents.patent_assignees via ref.canonical_org_entity — was abandoned after entity resolution returned unreliable/wrong matches (e.g. "Stanford University" resolved with high confidence to "Samford University," an unrelated Alabama institution) and the federal-only HERD query for 2023 returned zero rows for the expected field label. The AUTM primary-source PDF, fetched and parsed directly, was used instead and is more reliable for this specific question.
