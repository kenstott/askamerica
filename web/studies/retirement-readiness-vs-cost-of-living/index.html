How far short of what living costs require are Americans approaching retirement, and for how many of them?

Summary

Two independent, well-established U.S. retirement-adequacy models converge on the same figure: roughly 39-40% of working-age households — disproportionately those in their 50s and early 60s, closest to retirement — are on a trajectory that will leave them unable to cover their living costs in retirement at their pre-retirement standard of living.

  • The Center for Retirement Research at Boston College's National Retirement Risk Index (NRRI) put the at-risk share at 39% in its 2024 update (based on the 2022 Survey of Consumer Finances), down from 47% previously — an improvement CRR itself flags as probably temporary, driven mainly by a run-up in home equity that most retirees never actually convert to spendable income.
  • EBRI's Retirement Security Projection Model independently finds a Retirement Readiness Rating of 59.9% — meaning 40.1% of simulated household life-paths run out of money before death — with an aggregate national shortfall (Retirement Savings Shortfall, RSS) of $3.48 trillion in present-value dollars (2022 data, published 2024).
  • Applied to the roughly 42 million Americans aged 55-64 (Census population estimate), that ~39-40% share implies on the order of 16-17 million people approaching retirement who are financially unprepared for it. This multiplication is this session's own arithmetic, not a jointly modeled figure — treat it as order-of-magnitude.

AskAmerica's government data corpus has no household-level wealth or retirement-account microdata (no Survey of Consumer Finances equivalent), so it cannot recompute the NRRI or EBRI figures directly. What it can add — and what neither cited study reports — is real government administrative/price data on the floor under these households: Social Security benefits have only barely kept pace with inflation, the same benefit buys very different amounts of security depending on the state, and the household saving rate that would need to rise to close the gap has instead been falling for three straight years.

How short: the size of the gap

EBRI's Retirement Savings Shortfall (RSS) measure is the more direct answer to "how far short." It is the present value, at retirement age, of the extra amount a household would need on hand to cover the basic-living-expenses-plus-uninsured-healthcare-costs gap in every year it is projected to run out of money. EBRI's most recent published cut (2022 data, published 2024) puts the aggregate national deficit at $3.48 trillion across households aged 35-64.

An earlier vintage of the same model (2015 data) broke this down by cohort: a per-household deficit (among those with any projected deficit) of roughly $71,000 - $105,000 for early Baby Boomers depending on marital status, and $17,000 - $78,000 for Gen X depending on years of remaining 401(k)/DC-plan eligibility — the deficit is worst for near-retirees with little time left to save and the least Social Security/pension coverage.

Separately (a different kind of evidence — self-reported, not model-simulated), a 2025 Schroders survey found Gen Xers expect to retire with $711,771 versus the $1,116,747 they believe they need — a $405,000 self-assessed gap — and Baby Boomers a $357,000 gap by the same self-report method. These figures are not directly comparable in magnitude to the EBRI model-simulated deficit, but they agree on direction and on which cohort is worst off (Gen X, now in their late 40s to 50s).

How many: the share and count of people affected

The NRRI's 39% and EBRI's 40.1% are independently derived — different models, different simulation methodology, but drawing on the same 2022 SCF-era underlying data — and land within a point of each other. That agreement is itself worth noting.

The NRRI's 39% is a marked improvement from 47% in the prior update, but CRR flags it as likely optimistic: it's driven mainly by a temporary run-up in home equity that most retirees do not actually spend down, and would move back up if home prices normalize.

Applying the ~39-40% share to the roughly 42 million Americans in the 55-64 age band yields approximately 16-17 million people approaching retirement age on an inadequate trajectory.

What AskAmerica's data adds: the floor is barely holding, and it holds very differently by state

Social Security, the near-universal floor under retirement income, has only just kept pace with inflation. Querying SSA's own beneficiary data (fiscal.ssa_benefits_by_geography), the average monthly benefit for a retired worker rose from $1,411 in 2017 to $1,914 in 2023, nominal. Deflating each year to 2023 dollars with the corpus's CPI-U series (econ.inflation_metrics via adjust_inflation) shows the real benefit only grew from $1,754 to $1,914 — a 9% real increase over six years, almost all of it from the outsized 8.7% COLA triggered by 2022's inflation spike. In an average year without that spike, the real benefit was essentially flat. A benefit that merely tracks CPI does not close any retirement-income gap — it only prevents the gap from widening due to inflation.

The same dollar benefit buys very different amounts of retirement security by state. The corpus's BEA Regional Price Parity index (econ.regional_price_parities) shows a 24-point cost-of-living spread across states — from about 87 (Arkansas, Mississippi) to about 111-112 (California), with Hawaii, DC, New Jersey and New York also above 107. Computed against each state's actual average 2023 benefit, a California retiree's nominal $1,866/month benefit is worth only about $1,663/month in national-average purchasing power — roughly 13% less than the $1,914 nominal figure usually cited for Social Security, and the worst-off of the states checked. A retiree drawing the same nominal benefit in Arkansas keeps essentially all of its nominal value. This means the NRRI/EBRI at-risk shares, which are national averages, understate the shortfall for near-retirees in high-cost coastal states and overstate it for those in low-cost states — a dimension neither cited study reports by geography.

The household saving rate that would need to rise to close this gap is instead falling. BEA/BLS's personal saving rate (econ.fred_indicators, series PSAVERT) has declined for three consecutive years: from roughly 5.6% in late 2023, to 4.3-4.9% through 2025, to just 2.7% by June 2026 — the lowest sustained rate in the loaded series outside pandemic-stimulus distortions. This is forward-looking context the NRRI/EBRI vintage data (2022 SCF, roughly 3-4 years old at time of writing) cannot show: whatever the at-risk share was measured at in 2022, the saving behavior needed to improve on it has moved the wrong direction since.

What this corpus could not verify directly

AskAmerica's government-data corpus does not contain household-level retirement account balances, net worth, or a Survey of Consumer Finances equivalent — the NRRI and EBRI RSS/RRR figures above are relayed from the cited literature, not reproduced or recomputed from raw microdata in this system. What the corpus does hold (SSA benefit administrative data, BLS CPI, BEA regional price parities, BEA/BLS personal saving rate) was used to extend those figures with real-dollar, geographic, and trend context the two headline studies do not themselves report. Reproducing the NRRI or EBRI RSS calculation directly would require SCF microdata, which is outside this corpus's coverage — a genuine coverage gap, not one worked around with a proxy.
