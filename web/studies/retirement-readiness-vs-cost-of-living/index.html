Most Americans nearing retirement fall roughly a quarter short of what their retirement will cost — about 60% of the youngest boomer cohort, a median gap of $9,000 a year

Vanguard Retirement Outlook (Oct 2025); Boston College Center for Retirement Research National Retirement Risk Index; EBRI Retirement Security Projection Model

Summary

Roughly 60% of Americans in the youngest baby-boomer cohort (ages 61-65) — the group closest to retirement — are not on track to cover their living costs once they stop working, according to Vanguard's October 2025 Retirement Outlook. The typical (median-income) person in that group faces an annual income shortfall of about $9,000, equal to roughly 24% of what their retirement spending will actually require. Applied to the roughly 41 million Americans currently aged 55-64, that points to somewhere in the neighborhood of 20-25 million near-retirees heading into retirement without enough income to maintain their current standard of living. A separate, broader measure — Boston College's National Retirement Risk Index — puts 39% of all working-age households (not just near-retirees) at risk, down from 47% in 2019 mostly because of a temporary run-up in home values.

How far short: the size of the gap

Vanguard's Retirement Outlook, released October 14, 2025 and built on its Vanguard Retirement Readiness Model (calibrated to the Federal Reserve's 2022 Survey of Consumer Finances, the Health and Retirement Study, and Society of Actuaries mortality tables), found that only 40% of workers age 61-65 — the youngest boomers — are financially on track to maintain their current lifestyle in retirement. The other 60% are projected to fall short. For the median-income person in that cohort, retirement income (Social Security plus savings) is projected to replace only about 56% of pre-retirement income, an annual dollar shortfall of $9,000, or 24% of their total funding needs — assuming retirement and Social Security claiming at 65. Gen X, the next cohort in line, faces a smaller but still real gap: a median shortfall of $6,000/year, or 18% of needs.

Two levers meaningfully narrow the gap for this specific group, per the same study: working two more years (retiring/claiming at 67 instead of 65) raises the on-track share for 61-65 year-olds from 40% to 47%; and tapping home equity (86% of boomers own homes, with average equity of about $113,000) would raise the on-track share to about 60% — but most retirees are reluctant to do this, and the CRR/NRRI research below treats home-equity annuitization as an optimistic upper bound rather than what people actually do.

A second, broader measure agrees on the scale of the problem

The Center for Retirement Research at Boston College's National Retirement Risk Index (NRRI), the longest-running academic measure of this question, found that 39% of all working-age U.S. households are at risk of being unable to maintain their pre-retirement standard of living, using 2022 Survey of Consumer Finances data — down sharply from 47% in 2019. The improvement was driven almost entirely by a temporary spike in home values and pandemic-era savings, not by any underlying gain in retirement preparedness; the NRRI's authors caution that because home prices may not stay elevated and it assumes every household annuitizes a reverse mortgage on their house (which few actually do), "the share of households deemed at risk by the NRRI can be viewed as a lower-bound estimate." A cited Vanguard study found at least 70% of households would fall short of their pre-retirement standard of living once home equity is excluded from the calculation. The NRRI also reports that the reduction in risk from 2019 to 2022 was largest, in relative terms, for the oldest (nearest-retirement) age group and for high-income households — meaning lower- and middle-income near-retirees remain more exposed.

How many people that represents

Neither Vanguard nor CRR publishes a national headcount tied to these percentages, so this report computes an approximation. Census Bureau/BLS data (via FRED, working-age population series, 2023) puts the U.S. population aged 55-64 at approximately 41.4 million people — the closest standard age band to Vanguard's 61-65 near-retiree cohort. Applying Vanguard's 60% not-on-track rate to that population implies roughly 25 million Americans in this broad near-retirement age band face a meaningful retirement income shortfall; narrowing to just the 61-65 sub-band (proportionally about 20.7 million people, a rough linear estimate since Census does not publish that exact 5-year band nationally) implies about 12 million people. Separately, EBRI's Retirement Security Projection Model estimated an aggregate national retirement deficit of $3.48 trillion (2022 dollars) for households aged 35-64 — a stock measure of the present-value gap rather than a headcount, but consistent in magnitude with a shortfall affecting tens of millions of households, concentrated among single women, renters, and those without an employer retirement plan.

All of these figures describe the same underlying reality from different angles: a majority — not a minority — of Americans in their early-to-mid 60s are approaching retirement with income streams that fall roughly a fifth to a quarter short of what maintaining their current living standard will actually cost, and the shortfall is concentrated among lower- and middle-income households without home equity or continued earnings to fall back on.
