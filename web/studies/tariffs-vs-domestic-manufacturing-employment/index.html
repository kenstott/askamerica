Tariff-protected industries gained a few percent of jobs short-run, then flattened or fell back — the net national effect was roughly zero to slightly negative

2018 Section 232/201 tariffs on steel, aluminum, washing machines, and solar panels; BLS QCEW employment, 2015-2025

Summary

The main U.S. tariff protection episode for manufacturing was the March 2018 Section 232 tariffs on steel and aluminum, plus the January 2018 Section 201 tariffs on washing machines and solar panels. In the directly protected industries, employment rose modestly in the two years right after the tariffs: iron and steel mills (NAICS 3311) employment grew +6.4% from 2017 to 2019 (about +5,000 jobs nationally), steel products from purchased steel (3312) grew +4.6%, and the broader primary metals category (331, includes aluminum) grew +4.9%. Household appliance manufacturing (3352, the closest NAICS proxy for washing machines) grew only +2.1%. But these gains mostly did not last: by 2025, primary metals employment was -1.5% below its 2017 level and household appliances was -9.0% below it, both underperforming total U.S. manufacturing employment, which was roughly flat (-0.9%) over the same span. The peer-reviewed literature (Flaaen & Pierce, Federal Reserve, 2019/2020) reconciles this: the protected mills did gain jobs, but the tariffs' input-cost increases caused larger job losses in downstream steel- and aluminum-using manufacturers, producing a net loss of about 75,000 manufacturing jobs (-0.6% of manufacturing employment) by mid-2019 even as the protected sector itself grew.

Which industries got tariff protection

  • Steel — Section 232 tariffs, 25% on imports, effective March 2018 (NAICS 3311 iron & steel mills; 3312 steel product manufacturing from purchased steel; broader 331 primary metals).
  • Aluminum — Section 232, 10% tariff, March 2018 (also within NAICS 331).
  • Washing machines — Section 201 tariff-rate quota, effective rate roughly 20-50% depending on volume, effective February 2018 (closest available NAICS proxy: 3352 household appliance manufacturing, which also includes refrigerators, dishwashers, etc. — washing machines are not separately coded in QCEW).
  • Solar panels/cells — Section 201, ~30% initial tariff, February 2018 (falls within NAICS 3344, but that code is dominated by semiconductors, so it is not a usable proxy and is omitted from the employment comparison below).

Window rationale: 2017 (pre-tariff baseline) to 2019 isolates the short-run response the tariffs were designed to produce, before COVID-19 employment shocks distort the series from 2020 onward; 2017 to 2025 (the latest full year in BLS QCEW) shows whether any protection-driven gain persisted through a full business cycle, including the 2025 escalation of tariffs under the second Trump administration. Household appliances' steepest drop is 2024-2025 (-13%), coinciding with that renewed tariff escalation rather than reversing it — consistent with cost-pass-through and demand effects working against the protected sector even while it is nominally more protected.

Why the protected sector's own gain does not equal the net effect

Flaaen and Pierce (Federal Reserve Board, 'Disentangling the Effects of the 2018-2019 Tariffs on a Globally Connected U.S. Manufacturing Sector,' 2019/2020) found that while the steel and aluminum tariffs raised output an estimated $2.8 billion in the protected industries, they raised input costs by more for downstream manufacturers that use steel and aluminum, cutting downstream production by an estimated $3.4 billion. Netting these effects, they estimate the tariffs were associated with about 75,000 fewer manufacturing jobs (-0.6%) nationally by mid-2019 — a net loss even though the directly protected mills themselves added jobs, matching the QCEW pattern above. Separately, Autor, Beshkar, Fajgelbaum, Khandelwal, and coauthors (evaluating the same 2018-2019 trade war using local labor-market data) found the tariffs had 'neither a sizable nor significant effect on U.S. employment in regions with newly-protected sectors,' while retaliatory tariffs from trading partners had clear negative employment effects, concentrated in agriculture. For the washing-machine tariffs specifically, a widely cited academic estimate (Flaaen, Hortaçsu, and Tintelnot, published in the American Economic Review) put the cost at roughly $800,000 per job created in domestic washer assembly (about 1,800 new jobs), because the tariff also raised dryer prices (sold as a pair with washers) and funded roughly $1.5 billion in added annual consumer costs.

Caveats

  • Washing machines and solar cells have no dedicated NAICS code in QCEW; 3352 (household appliances) and 3344 (semiconductors, dominated by non-solar chip production) are the closest available proxies, so those two figures understate the precision available for steel/aluminum.
  • QCEW year-over-year swings after 2019 reflect COVID-19 shutdowns and recovery as well as any tariff effect; the 2020-2021 dip in primary metals and appliances is a pandemic effect layered on top of, not purely caused by, the tariffs.
  • 2025 figures reflect a period of substantially broader and higher tariffs (the second Trump administration's expanded Section 232 and reciprocal tariff actions), so the 2017-2025 comparison captures a different and larger policy shock than the original 2018 episode, not a clean continuation of it.
  • All employment counts here are the protected industry's headcount only; the literature's net national estimate (-75,000 jobs) already prices in the downstream losses these industry-specific numbers do not show.
