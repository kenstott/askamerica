Steel and aluminum employment rose modestly after 2018 tariffs (~4% and ~1%); appliance/washer employment is too small a slice of NAICS to see clearly, and 2025's tariffs are too recent to score

BLS QCEW/CES, NAICS 3311+3312 (steel), 3313 (aluminum), 335220 (major appliances); 2015-2025

Summary

Manufacturing employment in the two industries that got the clearest, longest-running tariff protection — steel (NAICS 3311+3312) and aluminum (NAICS 3313), protected by Section 232 tariffs since March/June 2018 — rose only modestly: steel employment is up roughly 4.3% (~5,700 jobs) and aluminum roughly 1.1% (~600 jobs) from 2017 (the last full pre-tariff year) to 2025. Most of the steel gain happened by 2019 and was partly given back in the pandemic; it has not grown much further since. Washing-machine assembly (protected by a separate, 2018 Section 201 safeguard tariff) is not separately trackable in this government data — NAICS lumps washers into a much broader "major household appliance" code — but the peer-reviewed academic study of that specific tariff (Flaaen, Hortaçsu & Tintelnot, American Economic Review) found it created about 1,800 U.S. jobs at Whirlpool (Ohio), Samsung (South Carolina) and LG (Tennessee) plants, at a cost to consumers of roughly $817,000 per job. Total U.S. manufacturing employment, meanwhile, grew about the same modest 1.4% over the same 2017-2025 window, so the protected industries did not meaningfully outperform manufacturing as a whole — and multiple independent studies (Federal Reserve Board; Cox & Russ) find the steel/aluminum tariffs' input-cost increases cost roughly 75,000 jobs in downstream, steel/aluminum-using manufacturing, versus perhaps 1,000-20,000 jobs gained directly in steel production, because steel-consuming industries employ far more workers than steel-producing ones.

Which industries actually got tariff protection

Tariff protection for manufacturing came in two separate waves:

  • 2018, Section 201 safeguard tariffs (effective Feb 2018): large residential washing machines and imported solar cells/modules.
  • 2018, Section 232 national-security tariffs (effective Mar-Jun 2018): steel (25%) and aluminum (10%) imports, essentially economy-wide by country of origin.
  • 2025, expanded Section 232 tariffs: steel and aluminum tariffs doubled to 50% (June 2025), plus new or threatened Section 232 actions on autos, copper, semiconductors, lumber and pharmaceuticals.

This report focuses employment measurement on steel and aluminum, the two industries with a clean NAICS code, a full pre/post period in BLS data, and no confounding broader category. Washing machines and solar cells do not have their own NAICS codes (they sit inside 335220 and 334413/334419, both far broader categories with heavy state-level data suppression), so government employment data cannot isolate them — the academic literature that used firm-level and trade data is the better source there and is cited above.

Steel and aluminum: the data

Source: BLS Quarterly Census of Employment and Wages (QCEW), private-sector employment (own_code=5), summed across all reporting states (econ.state_wages), industry codes 3311+3312 (iron/steel mills and steel pipe/tube) and 3313 (aluminum production/processing). QCEW suppresses cells for small-employer disclosure risk; roughly 20-25% of state cells for these industries are suppressed, so this sum understates BLS's own published national total by about 3-5% (confirmed by comparing against econ.national_wages, which carries BLS's true national figure, for the years that table has loaded: 2015-2016 and 2023-2025). The trend and direction of the state-sum series track the true national series closely in those overlap years, so the year-over-year change is reliable even though the absolute level is a lower bound.

Both industries gained the most in the first two years after the tariff (2017-2019: steel +5.7%, aluminum +5.2%), consistent with the tariffs' immediate protective effect, then gave back some of that gain in the 2020 pandemic downturn and have grown only slowly since — 2025 employment in both industries sits close to, but not dramatically above, where it was in 2019.

Why the net effect on manufacturing looks smaller than the headline steel/aluminum numbers

Steel and aluminum together employ roughly 190,000-200,000 people; industries that use steel and aluminum as an input (autos, machinery, appliances, construction products) employ well over 12 million. A Federal Reserve Board study, cited by economists Lydia Cox and Kadee Russ, estimated the 2018 Section 232 tariffs' higher input costs reduced employment in steel/aluminum-consuming manufacturing by about 0.6%, or roughly 75,000 jobs — against maybe 1,000 to 20,000 jobs gained directly in steel and aluminum production (estimates vary by source; the USITC's 2023 study, using investment announcements, put steel investment-linked job creation as high as ~20,000). That asymmetry — a large downstream industry absorbing a small cost increase versus a small upstream industry capturing a large price increase — is the standard finding across this literature and is consistent with total manufacturing employment (+1.4%) growing no faster than the protected industries themselves over the same window.

2025 tariffs: too recent to measure

The June 2025 doubling of steel/aluminum tariffs to 50%, and new Section 232 actions on autos, copper, lumber, semiconductors and pharmaceuticals, are within this year's data but BLS QCEW (the industry-detail employment source used above) publishes with roughly a one-year lag, so full-year 2025 effects are not yet measurable at NAICS-industry grain. Early commentary (Cato Institute, PIIE) describes the benefits as concentrated in a small number of metals-producing regions and the costs as diffuse across steel/aluminum-consuming manufacturers and households — the same pattern seen in 2018 — but this is not yet a completed-year finding.

What This Report Does Not Answer

  • Washing-machine/solar employment specifically: No NAICS code isolates washing-machine or solar-panel assembly alone; 335220 (Major Household Appliance Mfg) and 334413 (Semiconductor/photovoltaic devices) are much broader categories, heavily suppressed at state grain. Used the peer-reviewed Flaaen/Hortaçsu/Tintelnot (AER) firm-level estimate for washers instead of a QCEW figure, and flagged solar as not separable in this data.
