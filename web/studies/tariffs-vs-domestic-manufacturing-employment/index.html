Tariff-protected steel and aluminum jobs rose about 6% by 2019 — but net manufacturing employment fell as downstream costs outweighed the gain

BLS QCEW (econ.state_wages) industry employment, 2015-2025, plus Federal Reserve, AEI/Peterson Institute and SEIA research on the 2018-2019 and 2025 tariff rounds

Summary

The industries that received direct tariff protection in 2018 did add jobs, but the gain was small in absolute terms and was not free: broader manufacturing employment fell net once the higher input costs and foreign retaliation the tariffs also triggered are counted. In BLS QCEW data, combined employment in iron/steel mills (NAICS 3311) and aluminum production (NAICS 3313) rose from about 133,700 in 2017 to about 141,600 at its 2019 peak — a gain of roughly 7,900 jobs (+5.9%). The Federal Reserve's own peer-reviewed study of the same episode (Flaaen & Pierce, 2019/2020) found that this protection effect was more than offset economy-wide: industries exposed to the tariffs through higher input costs and retaliatory tariffs on their exports lost more jobs than the protected industries gained, for a net estimated loss of roughly 75,000 manufacturing jobs nationally. A parallel Section 201 case — the 2018 washing-machine tariff — created an estimated 1,800 US manufacturing jobs at an estimated consumer cost of about $815,000-820,000 per job. Solar-panel tariffs from the same year modestly aided module assembly but the Solar Energy Industries Association estimates net industry job losses (mostly in installation) of roughly 13,000 positions. A new steel/aluminum tariff round took effect in March and June 2025 (25%, then 50%); QCEW's 2025 rows are still partial-year and show industry employment slightly below 2024, too early to draw a conclusion.

Steel-mill employment rose 6.4% and aluminum-production employment rose 5.2% between 2017 and the 2019 peak, before COVID-19 erased most of the gain in 2020. Employment recovered by 2022-2024, roughly back to or slightly above the 2019 peak, then eased again in the partial 2025 data after the new March/June 2025 tariff round — too early and too partial a window to attribute a cause.

NAICS 335224 (household laundry equipment/washing machines), the industry targeted by the separate 2018 Section 201 tariff, is present in QCEW only through 2016 and at very small, disclosure-suppressed counts (9-57 employees reported nationally) — the series cannot be used to independently verify the washing-machine jobs claim; the 1,800-job estimate below comes from outside research instead.

In raw counts, these downstream industries kept growing through 2019 too — national manufacturing employment was expanding on trend that whole period, so a raw before/after comparison cannot isolate the tariffs' effect on downstream users; it takes a regression that separates industries by their exposure to the tariffs (as an input-cost shock and as a target of retaliation) from industries with low exposure, holding the general employment trend fixed. That is exactly what Flaaen and Pierce did, and it is why their finding differs from what a simple look at these raw levels would suggest.

The Federal Reserve's econometric finding: net effect was negative

Aaron Flaaen and Justin Pierce (Federal Reserve Board, FEDS Working Paper 2019-086, revised 2020) built an industry-level regression relating employment, output and producer prices to each industry's measured exposure to (a) import protection, (b) higher costs from tariffed imported inputs, and (c) retaliatory tariffs imposed by trading partners on that industry's exports. Their finding: industries more exposed to the tariffs' import-protection channel did see relative employment gains — consistent with the raw steel/aluminum numbers above — but industries more exposed to the input-cost and retaliation channels saw larger relative employment losses, and the retaliation/cost channels were larger in aggregate. The commonly cited net estimate from this and related analysis is a manufacturing-sector job loss of roughly 75,000 jobs, once all three channels are combined — i.e., the tariffs protected a real but comparatively small number of jobs in steel and aluminum while costing more jobs elsewhere in manufacturing through higher input costs and lost export competitiveness.

Two other 2018 tariff cases, for context

  • Washing machines (Section 201, Jan 2018): American Enterprise Institute/press analysis built on Whirlpool's Clyde, Ohio expansion put the created-jobs figure at about 1,800, against a consumer cost from higher prices of roughly $1.5 billion — about $815,000-820,000 per job. This is consistent with the earlier finding for 2009 Chinese tire tariffs (about $926,500 per job, per a 2012 Peterson Institute study).
  • Solar panels/cells (Section 201, Jan 2018): domestic module-assembly employment rose modestly, but the Solar Energy Industries Association estimated the broader US solar industry lost roughly 13,000 jobs since the tariffs took effect, concentrated in installation and downstream deployment, which is a much larger share of solar-sector employment than manufacturing.

The 2025 tariff round

A new Section 232 tariff on steel and aluminum imports took effect March 12, 2025 at 25% with no country exemptions, then was raised toward 50% by June 2025. QCEW's 2025 rows in this data are a partial year (the series is not yet complete for 2025), and both steel-mill and aluminum-production employment sit slightly below their 2024 levels in the data available so far (81,927 vs. 84,216 for steel; 55,939 vs. 56,594 for aluminum). This is too short and too partial a window, and too confounded with the broader 2025 manufacturing slowdown, to attribute to the new tariffs one way or the other.

Caveats and what was and wasn't verified

  • The QCEW figures above (NAICS 3311, 3313, 333, 3363) are directly queried from this connector's BLS QCEW table and are checkable via the SQL shown below.
  • The 1,800-job/$815,000-per-job washing-machine figure and the ~13,000-job SEIA solar figure came from web research (AEI, Peterson-adjacent press coverage, SEIA), not from a table in this connector — QCEW's own washing-machine series is too suppressed to independently verify or refute those numbers.
  • The ~75,000-net-job-loss figure is the headline number widely reported from Flaaen & Pierce's Federal Reserve working paper; it is a regression-based estimate over the whole tariff-exposure distribution, not a simple sum of a defined list of "protected" vs. "downstream" industries, so it should not be read as directly reconciling arithmetically against the raw QCEW industry counts above — the two measure different things (industry-level raw counts vs. an economy-wide regression coefficient).
  • 2025 QCEW data is partial-year; do not read the small 2025 dip as a verified tariff effect.
