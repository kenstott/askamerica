Tariff-Protected Steel and Aluminum Added Jobs on Net, but Downstream Manufacturing — Especially Auto Parts — Lost More, Leaving Total Manufacturing Employment Slightly Down

BLS QCEW county data summed nationally (private ownership) by NAICS industry, 2017-2025, cross-checked against BLS CES monthly reporting

Summary

Manufacturing employment in the industries that received tariff protection did not grow the way the policy intended. Primary metal manufacturing (NAICS 331 - steel mills, aluminum, and other primary metals, the direct target of the March 2018 and 2025 Section 232 tariffs) employed about 235,600 workers in 2025, down 9.2% from 2019 and down 6.8% from 2017, the last full pre-tariff year. The one clear winner was the narrowest slice, alumina/aluminum production (NAICS 3313, ~11,000 workers), which grew roughly 6-15% depending on the baseline year - but off a tiny base. Meanwhile downstream and adjacent tariffed industries fared worse: motor vehicle parts manufacturing (NAICS 3363, also covered by 2025 Section 232 auto-parts tariffs) fell 16.5-17.9%, a loss of roughly 58,000-64,000 jobs. Total manufacturing employment (NAICS 31-33, 12.6 million workers) is down about 1.4% since 2017 and 4.3% since 2019. In short: a modest, narrowly concentrated employment gain in the protected metal-producing industries themselves, more than offset by losses in the much larger downstream manufacturing base that consumes steel, aluminum, and auto parts as inputs.

What counts as "tariff protection"

Two tariff waves protected specific manufacturing industries: (1) the March 2018 Section 232 tariffs (25% steel / 10% aluminum, later raised) and Section 301 tariffs on Chinese manufactured goods; and (2) the 2025 tariff escalation - steel and aluminum raised to 50% in June 2025 (elimination of country exemptions in March 2025), a 25% Section 232 tariff on passenger vehicles and light trucks (April 2025) and on most auto parts (May 2025), a 50% tariff on copper products (August 2025), and an August 2025 expansion taxing the steel/aluminum content of derivative goods (machinery, appliances, etc.). This analysis focuses on the industries that received direct tariff protection: primary metals (331, including steel and aluminum), aluminum specifically (3313), and motor vehicles/parts (336, 3361, 3363).

Note the internal inconsistency: NAICS 336 (all transportation equipment, mostly aircraft and vehicle assembly) rose 5.7% since 2017 while its own subcategory, motor vehicle parts (3363), fell 16.5% - the grain of aggregation matters, and the parts subsector specifically targeted by the 2025 auto-parts tariff is the one that shrank.

Corroboration from published research and current reporting

This pattern matches the academic and journalistic record on both tariff episodes. The Federal Reserve Board's Flaaen & Pierce (2019) study of the 2018 Section 232 tariffs found that the net effect on manufacturing employment was negative: a small positive effect in the protected industries was outweighed by larger negative effects from higher input costs passed to downstream producers and from foreign retaliatory tariffs on U.S. exporters - about 75,000 fewer manufacturing jobs nationally by mid-2019 than would otherwise have existed. For the 2025 tariff wave, reporting drawing on BLS Current Employment Statistics (CES, the seasonally-adjusted monthly payroll survey - a different, more current BLS product than the QCEW figures tabulated above) found manufacturing employment down roughly 59,000 since the April 2025 tariff announcement through September, with durable-goods producers (autos, appliances, electronics) bearing the brunt, while primary-metals producers were "among the few" manufacturing subsectors adding jobs in 2025 - the same divergence this analysis finds in the QCEW data.

Data and methodology caveats

  • Grain choice matters. Steel/aluminum (331) rose relative to 2017 through 2019 (small tariff-protection bump) before falling sharply by 2024-2025, while the broader manufacturing base kept shrinking - the sign of the effect depends on which year you compare against and which NAICS level you pick (331 vs. its narrow sub-industry 3313 move in opposite directions in some windows).
  • County-summed QCEW vs. official national totals. This corpus's national-level BLS QCEW table (econ.national_wages) currently loads only 2025, so a multi-year national comparison isn't directly available from it; the multi-year figures above are built by summing county-level QCEW records (econ.county_qcew), which BLS's own documentation shows can understate true national totals in narrower industries because of disclosure suppression in individual counties. The percentage changes should be read as directionally reliable, particularly for the larger industries (331, 336, 332, 31-33 total), but not to the last decimal; the 2025 total-manufacturing figure here (12.19M) is broadly consistent with, though not identical to, the ~12.7M figure reported from BLS's monthly CES series for the same period.
  • 3361 (motor vehicle assembly specifically) was excluded from the headline comparison after its QCEW county-summed series showed an implausible 37% jump between 2019 and 2024 consistent with a disclosure/suppression artifact rather than a real employment swing; 3363 (parts) and 336 (the full transportation-equipment aggregate) were used instead as more reliable cuts.
  • 2025 is a partial or early-vintage year in BLS's own publication lag; some of the steepest 2025 tariff actions (June 2025 50% steel/aluminum rate, August 2025 derivative-goods expansion) postdate much of the year QCEW summarizes, so the full employment effect of the 2025 escalation is not yet fully reflected in either this analysis or the CES monthly data cited above.

query — 40 rows — 1710 ms
SELECT year, industry_code, SUM(annual_avg_emplvl) AS emp
FROM econ.county_qcew
WHERE own_code = '5'
  AND industry_code IN ('31-33','331','3313','336','3361','3363','325','332')
  AND year IN ('2017','2018','2019','2024','2025')
GROUP BY year, industry_code
ORDER BY industry_code, year
