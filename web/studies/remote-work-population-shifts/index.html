High earners left big cities after 2020 in outsized numbers — but the exodus faded fast while the income-skew of who left did not

IRS SOI county-to-county migration flows (2019-2022, latest available) and Census Population Estimates, cross-checked against published research and 2024-2025 return-to-office data

Summary

Yes, high earners left big cities in disproportionate numbers starting in 2020, and the effect was large — Manhattan's tax base lost over $16 billion in AGI (13% of what remained) to net migration in 2020-2021 alone, and San Francisco lost 20% of its tax base. Whether they "stayed" away is a split answer. The sheer volume of people leaving big coastal cities mostly normalized by 2022 — net household outflow from Manhattan, for instance, fell back to near pre-pandemic levels. But the income skew of who kept leaving did not fade the same way: in San Francisco and DC, the gap between what leavers earned and what newcomers earned was still widening as of 2022, the most recent year IRS migration data covers. Population and office-occupancy data through 2024-2025 show a modest rebound in New York and DC, essentially no rebound in San Francisco, and continued net decline in Los Angeles and Chicago's Cook County — so "did they stay away" depends heavily on which city you mean.

Step 1 — Did high earners leave, and how large was it?

The primary published analysis of this question is the Economic Innovation Group's August 2023 study of newly-released IRS Statistics of Income (SOI) county-to-county migration data (O'Brien, 2023). Its headline findings, reproduced here:

  • Large urban counties lost more than $68 billion in aggregate taxable income (AGI) to net migration between 2020 and 2021 alone — an acceleration of a pre-pandemic trend.
  • Manhattan lost more than $16 billion in federally taxable income (over 37,000 returns), equal to more than 13% of remaining residents' combined taxable income.
  • San Francisco's tax base shrank more than $8 billion — 20% — between 2020 and 2021.
  • Boston (-$2.5B, -11%), Washington DC (-$1.6B, -7.6%), and Los Angeles (-$8.8B, -3.7%) were also hit hard.
  • Critically, the people leaving earned much more than the people arriving: across all large urban counties, out-migrants earned nearly $10,000 more on average than in-migrants in 2021 — double the pre-pandemic (2019) gap, and five times the 2012 gap. In San Francisco specifically, the average household leaving earned $105,000 more than the average household moving in.

We independently reproduced this pattern directly from the same underlying IRS SOI source (askamerica's fiscal.county_migration_flows table, which covers filing years 2011-2022) for six major-city counties: Manhattan (New York County, NY), San Francisco County, Cook County (Chicago), Los Angeles County, Suffolk County (Boston), King County (Seattle), and Washington DC. The reproduction matches the published figures closely (see Sources for the exact SQL) and lets us extend the picture through the newest available year, 2022, and compute the leaver/arriver income gap by year for every one of these counties, not just the ones EIG highlighted.

The pattern is consistent across every large coastal city we checked: leavers out-earned arrivers before the pandemic, but the gap roughly doubled to tripled during 2020-2021.

Step 2 — Did they stay? Two different questions with two different answers

"Did they stay" collapses two distinct things that moved on different timelines: the number of people leaving, and the income skew of who kept leaving.

(a) The volume of departures mostly normalized by 2022. Manhattan's net household outflow (returns leaving minus returns arriving) went from -8,509 in 2019 (pre-pandemic baseline) to a peak of -45,177 in 2020, then -36,827 in 2021, before falling to just -890 in 2022 — essentially back to a rate lower than pre-pandemic. This matches independent reporting: Washington Post/Census Bureau data cited in national coverage found 11 of the 15 largest US metros were gaining residents or losing fewer by early 2023, and a 2026 Deel workforce-location study found the average distance between remote workers and their nearest major-city office has shrunk every year since 2022, with US workers now about as close to cities like New York, LA, Chicago, Houston, and San Francisco as they were in 2021.

(b) The income skew of who left did NOT normalize on the same schedule. In San Francisco and DC, the leaver-arriver income gap kept widening right through 2022 — SF's gap grew from $29,457 (2019) to $99,969 (2022), more than triple pre-pandemic, even as the raw number of people leaving fell sharply. That means the households who continued to leave in 2022 were increasingly concentrated among the highest earners, even as the total number of movers shrank. Manhattan's gap did pull back in 2022 ($52,574, versus $117,130 in 2021) but was still 45% above its 2019 baseline.

National confirmation, one year further out. The National Taxpayers Union Foundation's analysis of the IRS's tax-year-2022 interstate migration release (moves made during calendar 2022, filed in 2023) found California and New York remained the two biggest net losers of taxpayers nationally, and that Florida gained a net 50,485 residents earning $200,000+ in 2022 — 45% of Florida's entire net migration gain that year came from this single income bracket. Washington State (home to Seattle/King County) was the sixth-biggest net loser of higher-income residents nationally in 2022, despite nearly breaking even on population overall — evidence the high-earner-specific exodus persisted in some places even where the raw population picture looked stable. A separate 2026 analysis (via Yahoo Finance/Tax Foundation coverage of the newest IRS release) found the AGI outflow had "moderated relative to prior IRS data" — net AGI outflow from California and New York had fallen to 0.64% and 1.05% of each state's total AGI respectively, well below the roughly 2%-of-tax-base rate EIG found for large urban counties in 2020-2021. So the trend continued through the newest available data, but at a meaningfully reduced intensity.

Population and physical presence, through 2023-2025, tell a city-specific story, not a uniform reversal. Census Population Estimates (indexed to 2019=100) show New York and DC bottoming in 2021-2022 and ticking up modestly by 2023 (NYC to 98.1% of 2019 population; DC to 96.2%), while Los Angeles and Cook County kept declining through 2023 with no rebound (96.2% and 98.8% respectively). Office-occupancy data for 2024-2025 sharpens this further: Manhattan's office utilization recovered to roughly 80% of pre-pandemic levels by January 2025 — the strongest return-to-office recovery of any major US metro — while San Francisco ranked dead last among major metros, with office visits still roughly 52% below 2019 levels as of 2025, though 2025 brought the first decline in SF office vacancies since before the pandemic. A 2024-2025 mover survey found that among people relocating at all, the plurality (49-53%) chose suburban destinations rather than urban cores, suggesting that even the partial "return" happening nationally is landing around cities more than inside them.

Step 3 — Bottom line

High earners really did leave large, high-cost coastal cities in 2020-2021 at a rate and dollar scale well above the pre-pandemic norm, verified independently in this analysis using the same IRS SOI source EIG's widely-cited study used. That flight was not evenly distributed by income — it was disproportionately the highest earners leaving, and disproportionately the highest earners (not average movers) arriving in growth destinations like Florida.

The pace of departure slowed sharply by 2022 in every city checked, and headline population figures for New York and DC show a modest bottom-and-recover pattern into 2023. But "did they stay away" is not a single national verdict: San Francisco shows no population or office-occupancy recovery and an income gap between leavers and arrivers that kept widening through the newest available data (2022); Los Angeles and Chicago's Cook County kept losing population outright through 2023 with no sign of a turn; and national IRS data through the newest 2022-vintage release confirms California and New York remained the largest net losers of high-income taxpayers specifically, even as overall migration volumes eased. The honest summary is: the mass exodus faded, but for the highest earners and for San Francisco in particular, the shift looks more like a persistent redirection than a completed round trip.

What this analysis could not settle: IRS SOI migration data — the only source with an actual income figure attached to each mover — runs only through tax year 2022 (a ~4-year publication lag), so we cannot directly measure the leaver/arriver income gap for 2023-2025 with IRS data; the 2023-2025 evidence above (population, office occupancy, mover surveys) is a proxy for the trend's direction, not an income-specific replication of the 2020-2022 finding.

What This Report Does Not Answer

  • Income-specific migration data for 2023-2025 (post-2022): IRS SOI, the only source with an income figure per mover, runs only through tax year 2022 due to a ~4-year publication lag. For 2023-2025 the report substitutes population, office-occupancy, and mover-survey proxies, which are directional but not income-specific, and this substitution is disclosed in the report body and footnote.

    WITH counties AS (SELECT * FROM (VALUES ('17031','Cook'),('25025','Suffolk'),('06037','Los Angeles'),('06075','San Francisco'),('53033','King'),('36061','New York'),('11001','DC')) AS t(fips,name)), outflow AS (SELECT origin_county_fips AS fips, "year", SUM(num_returns) AS returns_out, SUM(agi) AS agi_out FROM fiscal.county_migration_flows WHERE origin_county_fips IN (SELECT fips FROM counties) GROUP BY origin_county_fips, "year"), inflow AS (SELECT dest_county_fips AS fips, "year", SUM(num_returns) AS returns_in, SUM(agi) AS agi_in FROM fiscal.county_migration_flows WHERE dest_county_fips IN (SELECT fips FROM counties) GROUP BY dest_county_fips, "year") SELECT c.name, o.fips, o."year", o.returns_out, i.returns_in, (i.returns_in - o.returns_out) AS net_returns, o.agi_out, i.agi_in, (i.agi_in - o.agi_out) AS net_agi_thousands FROM outflow o JOIN inflow i ON o.fips = i.fips AND o."year" = i."year" JOIN counties c ON c.fips = o.fips ORDER BY c.name, o."year"
