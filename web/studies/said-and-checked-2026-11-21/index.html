2026-11-21 · 48 statements from 7 speakers

President Trump held a long White House news conference on Nov. 20 after returning from Asia, where he met Chinese President Xi Jinping and Russian President Vladimir Putin. He spoke about Putin, a reported Iranian dirty-bomb plot, a week of technology outages including the FAA's nationwide ground stop, AI data centers and an AI agreement with Xi. Secretary of State Marco Rubio spoke at the same event about the three countries' nuclear arsenals.

Pennsylvania Gov. Josh Shapiro, in a PBS NewsHour interview the same day, spoke about the Asia summit, the war with Iran, U.S. military aid to Israel, his state's election results and minimum wage, and measles deaths in Pennsylvania. One of his statements, that the president did not address AI on the trip, is paired with Trump's account of an AI agreement with Xi.

On the Senate floor, Sen. Chuck Grassley spoke about welfare and Medicaid work requirements, Sen. John Thune about South Dakota Guard and Reserve units, and Sens. Shelley Moore Capito and Sheldon Whitehouse about the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act of 2026, a permitting bill.

How to read this page
Supported
Warehouse or external primary data confirms the statement.
Unsettled
Evidence was found, but it does not decide the claim: it turns on how a law or a loose term is read, or the evidence does not speak to the specific point.
Unsupported
No evidence was found in the AskAmerica warehouse or any external factual source. This does not mean the statement is false.
Contradicted
A primary source, read directly, says something different on the specific point, but some question remains open: an inquiry is unfinished, or the source could be using a different definition or period.
False
A settled official record, read directly, rules the statement out: a published measurement, no ordinary reading of the statement that fits it, and nothing pending that could change it.
Citejacked
The speaker cites a source that does not say what the speaker claims.
Premised prediction
An opinion or prediction that rests on a factual premise. The label rates the premise only, never the opinion or prediction built on it.
Counter-statement
A link to a statement by another speaker, or to an official document, that directly addresses the same point and says otherwise. A link is not a finding about which is right, and a rating shown beside a counter-statement rates that statement only.
Said more than once
When a speaker makes the same claim several times in similar words, it is listed once, with the other wordings and where each was said shown beneath it. Versions that differ on a figure, date or cause stay separate.
Contradicts own statement
A link between two factual statements by the same speaker that cannot both be true on the same specific point. A link is not a finding about which one is right; each statement keeps its own label.
Not checkable
An opinion, prediction, promise or personal attack. No data can settle it.

Every statement is listed, whatever its kind. There are no scores. Statements are grouped two levels deep, by speaker and then topic or by topic and then speaker, and every group is collapsed. A topic with a single statement is listed under Other. The counts beside a name cover only that person's statements on this page, and are not a rating of the person. Statements that could not be checked are folded under each speaker. Selection: Every statement by an in-scope officeholder quoted in a story published on Nov. 20 or 21, 2026 in the NPR Politics and PBS NewsHour Politics feeds, as read on Nov. 21 at about 09:07 ET, less the stories earlier pages already used, plus every transcript from the fixed transcript sources dated in that window (House and Senate floor speeches from the Congressional Record of Nov. 20). Three NPR stories and two White House statements returned too little text to use. Transcripts from Nov. 14 to 19 were searched only to confirm quotes. PBS NewsHour carries Associated Press reporting. Quotes under five words were left out, as were commentators' accounts of what officials said, one official's account of what another said, and statements by officials outside the offices in scope. Statements are worded as the outlet reported them; entries beginning "Reported as" are the reporter's wording.
