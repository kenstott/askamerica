Roughly 43% of the U.S. banking system's deposits are uninsured — but the typical small bank is nowhere near that exposed

FDIC/Federal Reserve Call Report data, 2019-2025 · $250,000 per-depositor, per-bank insurance limit

Summary

Nationwide, about 43% of all deposits held at FDIC-insured commercial banks exceed the $250,000 per-depositor, per-bank insurance limit as of Q4 2025 — back near the pre-pandemic level (43.3% in Q4 2019) after falling to a low of 40.7% in mid-2024 and spiking to a 20-year peak of 47.4% in late 2021. That share has been rising again over roughly the last year and a half, though it remains well below the 2021 peak.

But that 43% figure is dollar-weighted across the whole banking system, so it is dominated by a handful of very large banks with enormous uninsured balances. It is NOT what the typical bank, by headcount, looks like. At the median bank in the smallest size class (under $100 million in assets) — which is most of the roughly 4,400 FDIC-insured institutions in the country — only about 16% of deposits are uninsured. The uninsured share rises steadily with bank size, so 'the average bank' gives a very different answer depending on whether 'average' means 'average dollar of deposits' (~43%, rising) or 'the bank a randomly picked institution resembles' (much lower, well under half of the industry figure, for the numerically dominant population of small community banks).

Why the aggregate share moves the way it does

The $250,000 insurance limit has been fixed in nominal dollars since 2010 (Dodd-Frank). Inflation since then has eroded its real value by roughly 32% — $250,000 set in 2010 would need to be about $369,000 today to buy the same protection (askengine adjust_inflation, CPI-U). With the cap fixed in nominal terms while nominal deposit balances and wealth grow, a larger share of any given depositor's balance drifts above the line over time — a structural upward pull on the uninsured share, independent of any single bank's behavior.

Layered on top of that structural drift are cyclical swings: the uninsured share spiked to a 20-year high (47.4%) in late 2021 amid pandemic-era stimulus and cash buildup, fell sharply after the March 2023 failures of Silicon Valley Bank and Signature Bank (both of which were 90%+ uninsured) as depositors and banks reacted — partly by banks pushing large depositors into reciprocal deposit networks, which split large balances across many banks to make them fully insured while the depositor still deals with one institution — and has been drifting back up since mid-2024, reaching 43.3% again in Q4 2025, per the FDIC's Q4 2024 and subsequent Quarterly Banking Profiles.

Why 'the average bank' is a genuinely different number from the industry aggregate

The Federal Reserve Bank of Cleveland's Call Report analysis (Prescott and Rosenberger, 2024) breaks the uninsured-deposit line item down by bank size class and reports each class's median institution. The pattern is stark: median uninsured share rises steadily with bank size. The median bank under $100 million in assets — a large fraction of all FDIC-insured institutions by count — has only about 16% of its deposits uninsured, roughly a third of the 43% industry-wide figure. A separate bank-level analysis of the 34 largest banks (over $65 billion in deposits) found their average and median uninsured concentrations both around 39-40% as of early 2024, and some custodial/wholesale banks (BNY Mellon, State Street, large card issuers) run at 50-100% uninsured because their customer base is institutional, not retail.

The reason: large, sophisticated depositors (corporations, institutional investors, wealthy individuals) routinely hold balances well above $250,000 and gravitate toward larger banks; small community banks serve mostly households and small businesses whose balances rarely clear the cap. So the deposit base of a typical, numerically-representative small bank is much more fully insured than the deposit-weighted national number suggests — a distinction the FDIC's own asset-size-group tables in the Quarterly Banking Profile are structured to surface, but that a single industry-wide ratio erases.

Data and methodology notes

  • AskAmerica's own banking.institutions, banking.financials, and banking.sod tables (FDIC BankFind/Call Report mirrors) carry total assets and total deposits per institution but do not carry the Call Report's 'estimated amount of uninsured deposits in domestic offices' line item, so the uninsured share could not be computed directly inside the warehouse. This is a genuine coverage gap in the connector, not a missing analysis step — confirmed by inspecting both tables' full column lists.
  • All uninsured-share figures above are drawn from primary sources fetched directly: the FDIC's own Quarterly Banking Profile press release (Q4 2024, covering the domestic-deposit growth driven by uninsured deposits) and the Federal Reserve Bank of Cleveland's Economic Commentary 2024-14, which analyzes the Call Report's uninsured-deposit line item directly, plus a St. Louis Fed banking-analytics note for the most recent (Q4 2025) aggregate ratio and its 2019-2025 path.
  • The 'industry aggregate' and 'median small bank' figures are not perfectly time-aligned (the aggregate series runs through Q4 2025; the median-by-size-class figures are as of 2023-24, the most recent published breakdown found) — the qualitative gap between the two (aggregate roughly 2.5-3x the smallest-bank median) is the durable finding, not a claim that both numbers are contemporaneous to the day.
