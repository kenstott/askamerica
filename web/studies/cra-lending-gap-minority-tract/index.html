No U.S. dataset currently reports small-business loan approval rates by census-tract racial composition — the honest answer is a confirmed data gap, bounded by the closest available proxies

CRA small-business data (FFIEC) records only loan originations, never applications or denials; Section 1071, the rule that would add this, does not begin reporting until 2029.

Summary

This cannot be answered with a real number, because the underlying data does not exist yet. No U.S. government or industry dataset currently reports small-business loan applications or denials at the census-tract level broken out by tract racial composition — which is what an "approval-rate gap between majority-minority and other tracts in the same metro" requires. The Community Reinvestment Act (CRA) small-business data that FFIEC and banks publish records only loan originations — never applications — so there is no denominator from which to compute an approval rate. The rule that will finally require this data, Section 1071 of Dodd-Frank, was only finalized May 1, 2026 after roughly 14 years of delay and litigation; mandatory collection starts January 1, 2028, and the first public small-business lending register is not due until June 1, 2029.

What follows are the closest available proxies, each clearly labeled for what it actually measures rather than presented as an answer to the question asked.

Why the exact question cannot be answered today

Two things would need to exist simultaneously for this question to have a real answer: (1) small-business loan application and denial counts, not just originations, and (2) those counts broken out by census-tract racial composition, so majority-minority tracts can be compared against other tracts inside the same metro area (MSA).

  • CRA small-business data (the only annual, mandatory, geography-coded small-business lending dataset in the U.S.) collects loan originations only. It was designed in 1977 to monitor lending to low- and moderate-income neighborhoods, not to applicant or neighborhood race, and it has never required denial reporting at all. This was checked directly against AskAmerica's own banking.cra_small_business_lending table, built from this FFIEC source: describe_table shows its columns are originated-loan counts and dollar amounts by loan-size bucket and income tier at tract/county/MSA grain — no application or denial column exists for any tract in any year.
  • Section 1071 of Dodd-Frank — which will require lenders to report small-business credit applications, denials, and applicant/tract demographics — was proposed in 2021, finalized in 2023, litigated, and finalized again May 1, 2026 with a narrowed scope (coverage now limited to lenders originating ≥1,000 covered loans/year, small-business definition tightened to ≤$1M revenue). Data collection begins January 1, 2028; the first public register of this data is due June 1, 2029. Until then, no comprehensive small-business approval/denial-by-tract dataset exists publicly, for anyone.

Closest available evidence (proxies, not the requested figure)

1. Owner race, not tract geography — small-business lending itself. The Federal Reserve's 2025 Small Business Credit Survey (fielded Sept–Nov 2025, ~6,500 employer firms, published as the 2026 Report on Employer Firms) found firms owned by people of color are less than half as likely as white-owned firms with comparable credit profiles to receive full financing approval. In 2024, Black-owned businesses faced a 39% denial rate on loan/credit-line/merchant-cash-advance applications versus 18% for white-owned firms — a 21-percentage-point gap. This measures the owner's race, not the census tract the business sits in, so it does not answer the geographic question, but it is the most direct small-business-lending racial-disparity figure that currently exists.

2. Nearest tract-level, within-MSA analog — mortgage lending, a different credit market. A different U.S. credit market — mortgage lending under HMDA — does require both denial reporting and tract-level racial composition, making it the closest structural analog to what the question asks for. In a 2021 first-lien home-purchase-application analysis, the Urban Institute found a denial rate of 14.2% in tracts where white residents are under 50% of the population ("neighborhoods of color") versus 9.4% in majority-white tracts — a 4.8-percentage-point (roughly 50% relative) gap. This gap persisted after controlling for debt-to-income ratio, loan-to-value ratio, income, and tract-to-MSA income ratio: Black applicants were 1.8–2.5 times as likely to be denied as white applicants with similar observable characteristics in all neighborhood types, and Latino applicants roughly 1.5 times as likely. Notably, Urban Institute's own finding complicates a purely place-based framing: among Black and Latino applicants specifically, denial rates differed by less than 2.2 percentage points between majority-white tracts and neighborhoods of color — meaning most of the racial gap in mortgage denials tracks the applicant's race, not the tract's racial composition, once you're comparing within the same racial group of applicant. This is mortgage credit, not small-business credit, and should not be quoted as a small-business figure, but it is the best evidence available anywhere in the U.S. credit system for what a genuine within-MSA, tract-racial-composition-based denial-rate gap looks like when application-level data exists.

3. A lending-volume proxy for small business specifically. NCRC's tract-level analysis (dividing tracts into quintiles by retail loans — including small-business loans — per housing unit and per business) found the average share of people of color in "Quintile 1" (lowest-lending) tracts was 56.8%, and identified 1,265 predominantly-minority (>50% people of color), non-low/moderate-income census tracts across 344 counties that were systematically underserved by lending volume. This shows majority-minority tracts are disproportionately concentrated among tracts that receive the least small-business (and mortgage) lending volume — consistent with, but not a measurement of, an approval-rate gap, since it says nothing about how many businesses in those tracts applied or were denied versus simply not applying ("discouraged borrowers").

What was checked in AskAmerica's own data

banking.cra_small_business_lending's schema was inspected directly this session via describe_table: its full column list is loan counts and dollar amounts by loan-size bucket and income-group tier, at tract/county/MSA grain for 2019–2020 — no application, denial, or minority-population-share column exists in this table, which structurally confirms an approval rate cannot be computed from AskAmerica's small-business data even in principle, independent of the broader national data gap described above.

What This Report Does Not Answer

  • A quantified 'approval rate' gap for small-business loans specifically: No public U.S. dataset currently publishes small-business loan APPLICATION or DENIAL counts at census-tract grain. FFIEC's CRA small-business aggregate data reports only loan ORIGINATIONS by tract and income bucket (confirmed via describe_table on banking.cra_small_business_lending) — there is no application denominator anywhere in the source, so no approval rate can be computed from it by anyone, not just this corpus. The Dodd-Frank Section 1071 rule, which will require lenders to report small-business applications/denials by applicant race and census-tract demographics, was finalized May 1, 2026, with mandatory data collection starting January 1, 2028 and the first public Small Business Lending Application Register not due until June 1, 2029. This is a genuine, confirmed, dataset-wide gap, not a limitation specific to askamerica's corpus.
  • Comparison must hold metro area (MSA) constant, i.e. majority-minority vs other tracts WITHIN the same metro: Because no small-business approval-rate-by-tract dataset exists at all, no within-MSA comparison could be computed for small-business loans. The one figure below that is genuinely at tract grain with an MSA-holding-constant design is from the MORTGAGE market (HMDA/Urban Institute), a different lending market, offered only as the closest structural analog and labeled as such throughout.
