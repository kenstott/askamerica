Subprime credit-card delinquency surged post-2022, has since eased, but remains above pre-pandemic levels on the measures that matter most

Synthesis of NY Fed (CCP/Equifax), Kansas City Fed, Federal Reserve Board, and TransUnion data, 2019-2026

Summary

Subprime credit-card delinquency followed a sharp V/frying-pan pattern since the pandemic: it fell to record lows in 2020-2021 on stimulus and forbearance, then rose steeply once the Fed began tightening in March 2022, overshooting pre-pandemic levels by 2023-2024, and has been easing gradually since late 2024. Whether it is 'back to pre-pandemic' depends on which measure you use. On borrower composition, subprime has essentially returned to its 2019 share of the credit population (14.4% in Q3 2025 vs. 14.5% in Q3 2019, TransUnion). On actual delinquency rates for that population, subprime borrowers remain more delinquent than before COVID: the Kansas City Fed describes the subprime credit-card delinquency rate as still 'elevated' even after two straight months of decline, and NY Fed data show the bottom-quintile 'serious' (90+ day) delinquency rate running near 11%, above its roughly 10% pre-pandemic peak. So: down from the 2023-2024 peak, but not fully back to pre-pandemic health.

The pandemic trough and the post-2022 surge

Credit-card delinquency (all borrowers, 30+ days past due, NY Fed Consumer Credit Panel/Equifax) fell to record-low levels in 2020-2021 as stimulus checks, expanded unemployment insurance, and loan forbearance let households pay down revolving debt. Federal Reserve Board researchers (Barnes, Bopst & Driscoll, FEDS Notes, Feb 2025) document that the aggregate rate climbed back to its pre-pandemic level by 2023:Q1, and then kept rising, reaching roughly 125 basis points above that pre-pandemic level by 2024:Q3. Subprime borrowers bore a disproportionate share of this increase. The Kansas City Fed reports the subprime credit-card delinquency rate rose 7.4 percentage points, trough to peak, over the monetary-tightening cycle that began in March 2022 — a much larger move than the market-wide increase, consistent with subprime borrowers being most exposed to higher APRs and tighter underwriting.

Where things stand now: falling, but from a bad peak

Multiple sources agree the trend has turned: the Kansas City Fed notes the subprime delinquency rate has fallen for consecutive months since November 2024, tracking a narrowing gap between subprime and prime borrowing costs (APRs) and a pullback in subprime credit-card purchase growth. TransUnion’s Q3 2025 Credit Industry Insights Report shows the subprime share of the overall credit-risk population back near its 2019 level (14.4% vs. 14.5%), after falling to roughly 11.8%-12.5% in 2020-2021 and climbing steadily back up since. That composition figure is the closest thing to a 'fully round-tripped to pre-pandemic' data point in this research.

But the delinquency rate itself — as opposed to the size of the subprime population — tells a less complete recovery story. The Kansas City Fed explicitly still characterizes the subprime rate as 'elevated' even after its recent declines. NY Fed data (cited via TrendForce, drawing on the NY Fed's Q2 2024 Household Debt and Credit Report) put the 'serious' delinquency rate for the lowest-scoring/lowest-income segment at roughly 11.1%-11.3% in Q3 2024, above a roughly 9.98%-10% level that stood as the pre-pandemic-era high — meaning that even at its post-peak, easing trajectory, subprime serious delinquency had not returned to, and in fact exceeded, its pre-pandemic ceiling.

A methodological wrinkle: 'stock' vs. 'flow' delinquency

A August 2026 Liberty Street Economics post (NY Fed) directly addresses why headline subprime/overall delinquency numbers look so alarming even as underlying repayment behavior has stabilized. The 'stock' delinquency rate — the share of all outstanding credit-card balances 90+ days past due on credit reports — rose from 7.6% (2022:Q3) to 12.8% (2026:Q1), a Great-Recession-era level that has driven most of the 'subprime crisis' headlines. But the NY Fed shows this rise is driven almost entirely by a growing pool of already charged-off balances that lenders now report to credit bureaus for much longer than they used to (80% of charged-off debt is still being reported a year later in 2024, versus about 40% in 2004-2012) — not by a fresh wave of borrowers falling behind. The 'flow' delinquency rate — the rate at which balances newly become 90+ days past due each quarter — has been essentially flat since early 2024, and matches the trend in the Federal Reserve Board's own bank Call Report data. When charged-off balances are excluded, all three measures (flow, stock ex-charge-offs, and Call Report 30+ day rates) line up and show delinquency elevated but stable, not still worsening. Wolf Street's independent tracking of the Fed's Call Report series corroborates this: the seasonally adjusted 30+ day delinquency rate across all bankcards (including subprime) fell to 2.98% in Q3 2025, describing a 'frying pan' pattern — down from pandemic-era lows, up sharply through 2023-2024, and now just below its pre-pandemic level for the market as a whole.

Bottom line

Is subprime credit-card delinquency 'back to where it was before the pandemic'? Partially, and it depends on the lens: (1) The size of the subprime borrower population has essentially round-tripped back to 2019 levels. (2) The pace of new delinquencies (the flow rate) has stabilized since early 2024 and now tracks lenders' own Call Report data reasonably closely, suggesting no fresh acceleration in financial stress. (3) But the subprime-specific serious-delinquency rate remains above its pre-pandemic level even after declining from its 2024 peak, and the aggregate stock of severely delinquent/charged-off balances sitting on credit reports is at its highest share since the Great Recession — a legacy of debts accumulated in 2022-2024 that have not yet cycled off borrowers' credit files. In short: the flow of new subprime distress has calmed and the subprime population share is normal, but the accumulated stock of subprime delinquency, and the delinquency rate itself, have not fully returned to pre-pandemic norms.
