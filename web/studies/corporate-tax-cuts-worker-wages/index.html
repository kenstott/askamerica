Ordinary workers' real pay rose after the 2017 corporate tax cut, but the aggregate data can't show the cut did it, and the best micro studies find little for the typical worker

BLS CES avg hourly earnings, production/nonsupervisory (econ.wage_growth) deflated by CPI-U (econ.inflation_metrics); annual averages

Summary

Yes in raw terms, but the tax cut is not shown to be the cause. Real (CPI-deflated) average hourly earnings of production/nonsupervisory workers rose about 1.1%/yr in the two years after 2017 versus about 0.7%/yr in the two years before. Growth was only 0.5% in 2018, the first year of the cut, and 1.7% in 2019 as the labor market tightened. Firm-level research (Kennedy et al., AER; Chodorow-Reich, Zidar and Zwick) finds essentially no gain for the median worker and gains concentrated among owners, executives and top earners.

The picture depends on window length: equal 2-year windows say acceleration (0.68 to 1.11), equal 3-year windows say acceleration only if 2020 is counted (1.11 to 1.98), while a 3-year pre against a 2-year post (1.11 vs 1.11) would say none. All are small, and the pre-period had a recovery already underway, so a pre-existing trend explains it as well as the tax cut. No window has a control group. Real hourly pay went from 8.99 to 9.19 (1982-84 dollars) 2017 to 2019, about 2%.

What the research says

Kennedy, Dobridge, Landefeld and Mortenson compare C- and S-corporations in matched tax records: no detectable change in median worker earnings, +1.1% at the 95th percentile, +2.3% for executives; they attribute about 60% of the gain to owners, 8% to executives, 32% to other top-decile workers and none to the bottom. Chodorow-Reich, Zidar and Zwick find labor income up by less than $1,000 per employee in the long run. Proponents' projections of $4,000 to $9,000 per household were not tested here.

Caveats and exclusions

The CPI query excluded rows with period = 'M13' (BLS's pre-computed annual-average row, which would double-count a year) and kept only M01-M12, so each year's CPI is the mean of 12 monthly values (10 for partial 2026). Without the exclusion, the annual average row would be mixed with monthly values and shift the mean slightly; all headline growth rates use the 2014-2020 years, which have 12 months. National averages mix compositional change; no median-worker series was available in the warehouse; 2026 is a partial year. The table's declared window (2008-2024) understates the loaded rows (2009-2026).
