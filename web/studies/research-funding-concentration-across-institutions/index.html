Federal research funding is highly concentrated in a small set of universities, and that concentration has barely moved in over two decades

FY2024 institution-level federal R&D concentration vs. NCSES's own multi-decade concentration benchmark

Summary

Federal research funding at universities is very concentrated, and that concentration has been essentially flat for at least two decades. In FY2024, the top 10 universities (of 674 receiving federal research money) took in 20.8% of the $64.6 billion in federally funded academic R&D; the top 30 took 45.7%; the top 100 took 81.2%. The Gini coefficient across all 674 recipients is 0.82 — comparable in shape to wealth (not income) concentration. Johns Hopkins University alone — driven heavily by its Applied Physics Laboratory, a federally funded R&D center — received 5.6% of all federal academic R&D nationally, more than 2.5x the next institution. Critically, NCSES's own published statistic for TOTAL academic R&D (all funding sources) shows the top 30 institutions have taken about 42% of the total in every year from FY2022 through FY2024, essentially the same figure reported for FY2021, and the roster of top institutions has barely turned over since 2001 (9 of the FY2001 top 10 and 24 of the FY2001 top 30 were still in their respective top tiers 20 years later, in FY2021). The honest answer to "how much has it changed" is: very little — this is one of the most persistent concentration patterns in the federal budget.

Step 1 — Measuring FY2024 concentration directly from the warehouse

askamerica's research.nsf_herd_by_institution table holds NSF NCSES's Higher Education R&D (HERD) survey at institution grain. Filtering to rd_field='All', funding_source='Federal', federal_agency='Total' for FY2024 gives 674 institutions with a federal R&D total, summing to $64,639,941 thousand ($64.6B) — matching NCSES's own published FY2024 figure ("federally funded R&D at universities surpassed $64 billion") almost exactly, which is a good sanity check on the connector data for the one year it holds. The query filters on rd_expenditure_usd_thousand IS NOT NULL; checked directly, this predicate excluded zero rows (674 of 674 rows matching the year/field/source/agency filter already carry a non-null value), so it changed nothing about the population or the headline figures below.

Step 2 — A real coverage gap: the trend could not be computed from the connector alone

The connector's schema declares research.nsf_herd_by_institution covers FY2006–2024, but a row-level scan (data_coverage) shows only FY2024 is actually loaded — all 18 other declared years (2006–2023) are missing entirely. That makes it impossible to compute this question's "how much has it changed" half directly from the warehouse, so per this connector's own guidance on coverage gaps, I fetched NCSES's own primary published data directly (NCSES Science & Engineering Indicators, "Patterns of Academic R&D Spending" and the FY2024 HERD InfoBrief, NSF 26-305) rather than settling for a single-year snapshot with no trend, or a literature-only guess.

Step 3 — The trend, from NCSES's own published series

NCSES tracks concentration of ALL academic R&D spending (not federal-only) at the top-30-institution level across FY2001, FY2011, FY2021, and every year since, and reports it as essentially flat:

  • FY2021: the top 30 institutions accounted for "over 42%" of all U.S. academic R&D.
  • FY2022, FY2023, FY2024: the top 30 accounted for 42% in each year — NCSES's own FY2024 InfoBrief explicitly states this is "consistent with fiscal years 2022 and 2023."
  • Institutional turnover, FY2001→FY2021 (20 years): 9 of the 10 top-ranked institutions in FY2001 were still in the top 10 in FY2021 (different order); 24 of the top 30 in FY2001 remained in the top 30 in FY2021.

NCSES states plainly: "this concentration of R&D spending in a relatively small number of institutions is a long-standing characteristic of U.S. academic R&D spending" that has not shifted significantly "over the past two decades." The number of institutions crossing the $1 billion/year R&D threshold has been rising (29 in FY2022, 33 in FY2023, 37 in FY2024) — the group of "very large" performers is slowly widening at the margin — but the top-30 SHARE of the pie has stayed flat at 42%, meaning growth is being distributed roughly proportionally rather than concentrating further or diffusing.

Two further structural facts from the same source, relevant to "how concentrated": (1) concentration is much sharper among private universities than public ones — in FY2021 the top 25 private universities took over 75% of all private-university R&D, versus 46% for the top 25 public universities; and (2) institutions with a medical school receive a disproportionate share of federal money specifically — 75% of all federally sourced academic R&D in FY2021 went to institutions with a medical school, and 89% of NIH's academic funding went to those same institutions. This is consistent with what the FY2024 warehouse data shows directly: Johns Hopkins, UCSF, Duke, Penn, and Columbia — all medical-school-having institutions — dominate the federal-only top 10.

Step 4 — Reconciling the two concentration numbers (federal-only vs. total)

The connector's FY2024 federal-only top-30 share (45.7%) is a few points higher than NCSES's published FY2024 top-30 share of TOTAL R&D (42%). That gap is expected, not an inconsistency: federal money — especially NIH funding, which is 55% of all federal academic R&D — concentrates even more heavily in the largest medical-research universities than institutional, state, or business funding does, so slicing to federal-only should and does push concentration up slightly relative to the all-sources figure. Both numbers point to the same underlying reality of a top-heavy distribution that has held steady for two decades.
