Federal research funding is heavily but only mildly-more concentrated across institutions: Gini ~0.82, rising slowly since 2012, sharpest in Life Sciences and at HHS/DOD

NSF NCSES Higher Education R&D (HERD) Survey, institution grain, federal funding_source, FY2010-2024, USD thousands

Summary

Using NSF NCSES HERD survey data on federal R&D expenditures reported by the receiving institution (2010-2024, n=629-896 institutions/year), the Gini coefficient of federal research funding across institutions is 0.80-0.85 — very high concentration, comparable to wealth (not income) inequality. Comparing the first stable post-2011 year (2012: Gini 0.804) to the latest (2024: Gini 0.823), concentration has risen modestly (+0.019, about +2.4%), not dramatically. The top 10 institutions' share of federal R&D dollars has stayed roughly flat, ~20-21% across the period (19.7% in 2010, 21.2% in 2017, 20.8% in 2024) — corroborating the Gini trend: concentration is high and essentially stable-to-slightly-rising, not on a sharp trajectory in either direction. Concentration differs materially by agency (HHS/NIH 0.85-0.86, DOD rising fastest from 0.80 to 0.84, NASA highest at 0.85-0.87, vs. NSF and DOE consistently lowest at 0.75-0.79) and by field (Life Sciences highest at 0.84-0.86 and also the largest dollar category at 57% of the federal total; Psychology and Non-S&E lowest at 0.73-0.74).

Step 1 — Total federal research funding by institution, and the denominator

Total federal R&D expenditure reported across all HERD institutions rose from $37.5B (2010) to $64.6B (2024) nominal (not inflation-adjusted here; this is a levels/concentration analysis, not a real-growth claim). The top 10 recipients in FY2024 were Johns Hopkins University ($3.62B — more than 3x the #2 institution), Georgia Tech ($1.21B), Michigan ($1.17B), UC San Diego ($1.15B), Washington ($1.14B), Columbia ($1.08B), Duke ($1.04B), UCSF ($1.04B), Penn ($1.02B), and Stanford ($1.01B). This matches independently-reported HERD rankings (Johns Hopkins ranked #1 per NSF NCSES/Forbes coverage) — an external corroboration of the figures used here.

Denominator, stated explicitly: this analysis uses NSF NCSES HERD survey data, which is R&D expenditure reported by the performing/receiving institution, not USAspending prime-award obligations. This matters directly for the question's own caveat about subawards: HERD asks each institution how much federal-funded R&D it actually performed/spent, so a subrecipient university that receives a subaward from a prime awardee reports that money in its own HERD federal total — the flow-through is captured at the point of use, not attributed entirely to the prime awardee as USAspending's prime-award table would. This is a genuinely different denominator than 'USAspending prime obligations by recipient,' which the AskAmerica connector's fiscal.usaspending_by_state/by_agency views do not break out to the institution grain at all (no recipient-level federal grants table exists in this corpus outside HERD) — so a prime-vs-subaward comparison for the same population could not be constructed from this warehouse; HERD's own-institution-reported expenditure is the only recipient-grain federal R&D denominator available here, and it is used throughout.

2011 is a coverage anomaly, not a real spike: n jumps to 896 (vs. ~630-660 in surrounding years) and Gini jumps to 0.855, its highest value in the whole series — consistent with a broader set of institutions with small/marginal federal amounts being captured that year, which mechanically inflates Gini by adding low-value observations. Excluding 2011, the honest trend reading is: Gini was ~0.80-0.81 through the mid-2010s, then drifted up to ~0.82 by 2020 and has held there through 2024 — a real but modest rise of about +0.019 (2012→2024), not a dramatic concentration surge. This is corroborated by the top-10 institutional share, which moved from 19.7% (2010) to 21.2% (2017) to 20.8% (2024) — essentially flat, matching independent reporting that the top 10 universities account for roughly a fifth of HERD-type R&D spending nationally.

Step 3 — Breakdown by field and by agency

By agency (Gini of federal R&D across institutions, within each agency's own funding stream, 2012 → 2024): HHS (overwhelmingly NIH) is both the largest funder — $35.5B in 2024, 55% of all federal R&D at universities — and consistently one of the most concentrated, Gini 0.846→0.856. NASA is the single most concentrated agency (0.846→0.873). DOD shows the sharpest rise in concentration of any agency, 0.802→0.844 (+0.042, +5.3%). USDA is also high and rising (0.833→0.844). By contrast, NSF (0.798→0.792, essentially flat) and DOE (0.753→0.789) remain the least concentrated federal funders, consistent with NSF's broader, more distributed grant-making mandate relative to the NIH's flagship-medical-center-heavy portfolio.

By field: Life Sciences (biomedical/health) is both the largest field — $36.6B in 2024, 57% of the federal total — and the most concentrated (Gini 0.839→0.857), tracking HHS/NIH's dominance and rise. Engineering shows the largest field-level increase in concentration, 0.763→0.803 (+0.039, +5.2%), a bigger relative jump than the overall federal average despite Engineering being a mid-size category ($12.2B, 19% of federal total in 2024). Physical Sciences and Computer/Information Sciences sit in between (0.795→0.814 and 0.816→0.832 respectively). Psychology and Non-S&E fields (measured for 2024 only here, not a full trend) are the least concentrated at 0.727 and 0.740 — smaller-dollar fields spread across more institutions.

The pattern across both cuts is consistent: concentration tracks agency/field size. The single largest funding stream (HHS/NIH, feeding mostly Life Sciences) is also among the most concentrated and is not where the rise is fastest; the sharpest RISES in concentration are in DOD funding and Engineering research — smaller streams than HHS/Life-Sciences but consolidating faster, a pattern a snapshot top-N ranking would miss entirely.

Step 4 — What this measure does and does not imply

A Gini around 0.80-0.85 means federal research money is distributed roughly the way wealth (not income) is distributed in the US economy — a small number of institutions hold a large share, while several hundred more each receive comparatively little. This is a description of dollar distribution, not a normative or productivity claim. It does NOT by itself imply:

  • Unfairness or favoritism: concentration is expected in a peer-reviewed, competitive grant system where research capacity (labs, PIs, prior track record, medical-center infrastructure for HHS/NIH work) is itself concentrated. A merit-based system operating exactly as designed can still produce a high Gini.
  • Productivity or output: this measure counts dollars in, not discoveries, publications, or patents out. Whether concentrated funding produces disproportionately more or less research output per dollar is a separate, unanswered empirical question this analysis does not address — the literature reviewed (MIT Press Quantitative Science Studies) is actively split on whether concentrating funding raises or lowers aggregate scientific output.
  • A single true number: Gini over institutions treats a large research university and a small college with one federal grant as equally-weighted units being compared by dollars — it says nothing about per-capita, per-faculty, or per-proposal concentration, which could show a different picture.

What it DOES support: a defensible, denominator-explicit answer to how concentrated federal research funding is and whether it is changing — highly concentrated, essentially stable with a modest recent uptick, most pronounced in HHS/Life-Sciences (the largest stream) and rising fastest in DOD/Engineering (a smaller but consolidating stream) — a materially more complete picture than a static top-10 list, which cannot show the direction of change at all.

What This Report Does Not Answer

  • Report the denominator carefully: prime awards vs. total dollars including subawards, and that institutions differ in how much they subaward: USAspending in this corpus has no institution-grain (recipient) federal grants/prime-award table — fiscal.usaspending_by_state/by_agency/by_county are state/agency/county grain only, so a direct prime-award-only vs. HERD-institution-expenditure comparison for the same population could not be run from this warehouse. HERD institution-reported federal expenditure (which captures subaward flow-through at the recipient/performer level) is used as the sole available recipient-grain denominator, and this substitution is stated explicitly in Step 1 rather than silently treated as equivalent to a prime-award figure.
