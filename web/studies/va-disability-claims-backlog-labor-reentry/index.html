This corpus has no VA claim-processing-time data, but the closest causal evidence (SSDI examiner-delay study) says longer waits do delay return to steady work

A genuine coverage gap in this connector, addressed with primary literature: SSDI random-examiner-assignment delay study, the VA Agent Orange benefit-receipt study, and VBA's own backlog/processing-time reporting

Summary

The most honest answer is: almost certainly yes, but no study directly measures it for VA disability claims specifically — the causal evidence for that exact link comes from a sibling federal program (Social Security Disability Insurance), not the VA. Searching this connector's full catalog found no table carrying VA disability-claim processing time, appeal wait time, or a claim-level return-to-work outcome, and an extensive literature search found no published study that isolates the effect of VA claim-processing delay (as distinct from the eventual award/denial decision) on veterans' employment. What exists instead is the closest methodologically rigorous analogue: a study of the twin federal disability-compensation program, Social Security Disability Insurance (SSDI), that uses random assignment of applicants to examiners with different average processing speeds as a natural experiment isolating delay itself. That study finds delay has a real, independent, negative effect on subsequent employment — separate from and additional to the effect of getting benefits. Whether that finding transfers one-for-one to VA claims is an inference, not a measurement, but the underlying mechanism (extended time out of the labor force, employer skepticism about a long employment gap, skill atrophy, and the disincentive built into waiting for a compensation decision) applies to any comparable administrative disability-determination process, VA included.

What this connector could and could not answer

search_catalog was run against this corpus's ~25 government-data schemas for VA disability claims, claim processing time, and veteran return-to-work outcomes. The corpus carries veteran population counts (census.acs_veterans), VA medical-facility locations (health.va_medical_facilities), and BLS veteran-status labor force statistics (econ.employment_statistics, series LNU04049526 for veteran unemployment) — but nothing on VA Benefits Administration claim processing times, claim inventories, or claim-level outcomes. The word "processing" matched zero columns or table descriptions anywhere in the catalog. This is a genuine sourcing gap in the connector, not a case of the data existing under an unexpected name — VA's own claims-processing statistics live at benefits.va.gov/reports and are not yet ingested here.

Because of that gap, the descriptive veteran-vs-nonveteran unemployment-rate series shown on the dashboard (BLS CPS, not seasonally adjusted) is background context only. It shows veterans have consistently had a somewhat lower unemployment rate than nonveterans since 2020, with both groups tracking the same national cycle — but there is no VA processing-time series in this corpus to line up against it, so no claim about delay driving that trend can be made from it.

The closest causal evidence: SSDI examiner-assigned delay (Autor, Maestas, Mullen & Strand)

The single most directly relevant piece of research is "Does Delay Cause Decay? The Effect of Administrative Decision Time on the Labor Force Participation and Earnings of Disability Applicants" (Autor, Maestas, Mullen & Strand, NBER Working Paper 20840, published in the Journal of Labor Economics). It studies SSDI, the Social Security disability program — not VA — but its design is exactly on point for this question: applicants are effectively randomly assigned to disability examiners who differ in how fast they work, which creates variation in decision-wait time that has nothing to do with the applicant's own case strength. That randomization lets the authors isolate the pure effect of waiting itself, separately from the effect of the eventual award or denial decision — the exact distinction this question is asking about (delay vs. whether/when return to work happens).

Their finding, quoted directly from the abstract: "longer processing times reduce the employment and earnings of SSDI applicants for multiple years following application, with the effects concentrated among applicants awarded benefits during their initial application. A one standard deviation (2.1 month) increase in initial processing time reduces long-run 'substantial gainful activity' rates by 0.36 percentage points (3.5%) and long-run annual earnings by $178 (5.1%)." "Substantial gainful activity" (SGA) is the government's own working definition of steady, above-threshold work — functionally the same concept the question asks about ("whether and when they return to steady work"). The paper further finds that because initially-denied applicants spend an average of 15+ additional months in appeals, prior research comparing awarded vs. denied applicants had been confounding the delay effect with the award effect — once separated, the true award effect is at least 50% larger than earlier estimates, and the combined process (delay + award) suppresses employment roughly twice as much as prior literature suggested.

VA-specific evidence that exists — on award, not delay

The VA-specific causal literature that does exist studies the effect of getting a disability rating and receiving compensation, not the effect of the wait. Autor, Duggan, Greenberg & Lyle (American Economic Journal: Applied Economics, 2016) use the VA's 2001 Agent Orange decision — which expanded eligibility for Vietnam veterans who served in-theatre to conditions like type-2 diabetes, while leaving other-era veterans as an untreated comparison group — as a natural experiment. They find VA disability-compensation receipt reduced labor force participation by 18 percentage points, alongside a rise in transfer income and increased downstream qualification for SSDI. That is a study of the award decision's effect, structurally the opposite half of the SSDI paper's split (delay vs. award) — it says nothing about whether a faster or slower path to that award changes the outcome.

Separately, a BLS Monthly Labor Review article, "The Labor Supply of Veterans with Disabilities, 1995-2014", documents that the share of veterans who worked while receiving VA disability compensation fell from 62% to 49% over that period — a long-run descriptive trend, not a causal delay estimate, but consistent with a disability-compensation population that has grown steadily more detached from steady work over the same decades that VA's claims backlog and processing times were also rising (GAO documented average processing time rising from 161 days in FY2009 to 260 days in FY2012).

Current VA processing-time and backlog context

For scale: the VA's disability-claims backlog fell from roughly 264,700 pending claims in January 2025 to roughly 83,000-100,000 by mid-2026, a decline of about two-thirds, while average decision time fell from roughly 141 days to roughly 75-130 days over a similar span (VBA public reporting; figures vary slightly by the specific VBA report cited). Historically, average processing time rose from 161 days (FY2009) to 260 days (FY2012) per GAO. A DAV survey found over 60% of respondents said navigating the claims/appeals process caused financial or emotional hardship, and veterans with conditions like PTSD or service-connected injuries have specifically reported being unable to work while a claim is pending — consistent, directionally, with the SSDI mechanism above, though these are qualitative and survey accounts rather than a causal estimate.

Bottom line

Given the strength of the SSDI evidence — a well-identified, peer-reviewed causal estimate showing that decision delay alone (holding the eventual award decision fixed) measurably reduces both the rate of return to steady work and subsequent earnings, in a federal disability-determination process structurally very similar to VA's — it is reasonable to expect the same mechanism operates for VA disability claims: veterans whose claims take longer to process likely do see delayed and reduced return to steady work, over and above whatever effect the ultimate award decision has. But this is an inference from an analogous program, not a direct VA measurement, because (a) this connector carries no VA claims-processing data and (b) no published study was found that runs the SSDI-style examiner/regional-office delay design directly on VA claims. That is the honest limit of what can be said here, and it is a specific, nameable gap — a VA-claims equivalent of the Autor/Maestas/Mullen/Strand design, using VA regional-office or rating-specialist processing-speed variation as the delay instrument — rather than a general absence of relevant research.
