Port import volumes lead retail inventory build-ups by about 3-4 months, roughly double the raw shipping-pipeline time

Census container-import and international-trade data; peer-reviewed lag study (Transportation Research Record, 2022); ocean-freight transit benchmarks

Summary

Port import volumes lead retail inventory build-ups by roughly 3 to 4 months — not by the shorter physical shipping time most engineering-style estimates would suggest. The best available measurement comes from a peer-reviewed cross-correlation study (Smith, Bingham, Hackett & Smith, Transportation Research Record, 2022) that found a strong negative correlation between a retail-inventory index and an import-TEU index at a 4-month lag, using data from January 2018 through July 2021, with the relationship even stronger (still 4 months) in the 2020-2021 pandemic surge. The negative sign means the timing runs from inventory drawdown to import surge: when retail inventories fall sharply and non-seasonally, an import surge follows about a quarter later, and that surge continues until target inventory levels are rebuilt.

This measured lag (3-4 months) is roughly double the 5-9 week (about 1.5-2 month) figure you would get by simply summing the physical stages of the pipeline — ocean transit (2-5 weeks), U.S. customs clearance, and inland drayage/trucking (5-10 days combined). The gap between the two figures is real and important: the extra 1.5-2 months reflects the parts of the process a stage-by-stage transit estimate misses — the lead time retailers build into placing orders before a container ever leaves port, safety-stock/reorder-point policy, and periodic (not continuous) replenishment cycles — not shipping-pipeline friction itself.

What was measured, and by whom

The only rigorous, publicly documented measurement of this specific lag is Smith, D., Bingham, P., Hackett, D., & Smith, J. (2022), "Multiperspective Analysis of Pandemic Impacts on U.S. Import Trade: What Happened, and Why?", Transportation Research Record 2677(2), pp. 50-61 (authors are affiliated with Hackett Associates, the firm that compiles the NRF/Hackett Global Port Tracker). Its abstract states directly: "there was a relatively strong negative correlation between retail inventory- and import TEU indices with a 4-month lag (corresponding roughly to the time between import orders and arrival). In the 2020 to 2021 pandemic period the negative correlation was stronger, again with a 4-month lag." The paper frames this as a practically useful leading-indicator relationship: a marked, non-seasonal drop in retail inventory presages an import surge about a quarter later, and that surge persists until inventories are rebuilt.

No other primary source found in this search (Federal Reserve working papers, Census advance economic indicator releases, NRF Global Port Tracker press materials) publishes its own fitted lag figure for this specific pair of series — they report levels and short-term forecasts, not a cross-correlation. The Federal Reserve's own related research (the IFDP paper "Retail inventories and inflation dynamics: The price margin channel") studies a different relationship (inventory levels and price margins), not the import-to-inventory lag, and is cited here only to show it does not duplicate or contradict this finding.

Why the plausible shortcut (summing transit time) understates it

A natural way to estimate this lag is to add up the physical stages of the pipeline: freight-forwarder guides put ocean transit from China at roughly 12-38 days depending on origin/destination coast (about 14-20 days to the West Coast, 30-35 days to the East Coast), plus 5-10 days for U.S. customs clearance and port drayage/inland trucking, for a total of roughly 5-9 weeks (about 1.5-2 months) door-to-door once a container is booked. That estimate is a reasonable description of shipping logistics, but it is not the same quantity as the measured lag between the two aggregate economic series, because it starts the clock only once an order is already placed and a container is moving — it excludes the order-placement lead time before the physical pipeline even begins, and it says nothing about how retailers' inventory-policy and replenishment cadence translate an import wave into a change in the inventory figure itself. The measured 3-4 month lag captures all of that; the summed-stages estimate captures only the shipping leg. Reporting the shipping-time figure alone as "the lag" would understate the true, measured relationship by roughly half.

Current import-volume context (AskAmerica data)

For context, the chart above shows total U.S. goods import value (Census International Trade timeseries, world-total row, monthly, 2019-2025), computed directly from the AskAmerica connector (econ.trade_imports, filtered to geo_level='world' to avoid double-counting bloc/continent aggregate rows the source interleaves in the same feed). It shows the same broad pattern the literature describes: a 2020 dip, the 2021 pandemic-era surge, a 2022 peak, and renewed growth into 2025 — the same import series whose lagged relationship to retail inventory the cited study measures. This chart is descriptive background, not a re-derivation of the lag itself; see the data-gap note below for why the lag could not be independently recomputed in-corpus this session.

Data-gap disclosure

AskAmerica does carry a nominal retail-inventory series (econ.fred_indicators, series RETAILIMSA — Census Monthly Trade in Sales, retailers' inventories, seasonally adjusted — and its sibling RETAILIRSA, the inventory-to-sales ratio) that would, in principle, let this lag be recomputed directly against the connector's own bts_port_teu or trade_imports series. In this session that series was found to hold only 6 rows, all dated January-June 2026, despite data_coverage reporting a full 2009-2026 declared and observed window for the table as a whole (the aggregate scan across all 50 curated FRED series masked that these two specific series are almost entirely unloaded). This was logged as a data-quality issue via report_issue. Because of that gap, the lag reported here is the literature's own measured figure, not an in-corpus reproduction — a materially different provenance that the reader should weigh accordingly.

What This Report Does Not Answer

  • Reproduce or verify the lag using the connector's own data where possible: econ.fred_indicators RETAILIMSA/RETAILIRSA is only loaded for 6 months (Jan-Jun 2026), not the multi-year history needed for a cross-correlation; this was discovered, logged via report_issue, and disclosed in its own section rather than silently omitted or patched with a proxy variable.
