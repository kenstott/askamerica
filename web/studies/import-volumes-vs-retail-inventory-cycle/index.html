How much do port import volumes lead retail inventory build-ups, and by how long?

askamerica run · 2026-09-11

## Answer

The best directly measured estimate: **port import volumes lead retail inventory levels by about 4 months**, with a moderately-to-strongly negative correlation (rising imports precede rising retail inventories by roughly that lag, and vice versa). This is a *fitted/measured* lag from a peer-reviewed cross-correlation analysis of actual U.S. import-TEU and retail-inventory index data — not a summed-pipeline guess (ocean transit + customs + trucking), which alone would understate the true relationship because it ignores order-placement lead time and inventory-replenishment cadence sitting on top of the physical transit chain.

## Primary source and finding

**Smith, D., Bingham, P., Hackett, D., & Smith, J. (2023), "Multiperspective Analysis of Pandemic Impacts on U.S. Import Trade: What Happened, and Why?"** *Transportation Research Record*, 2677(2), 50–61 (published online 2022-06-23; DOI 10.1177/03611981221098663; PMCID PMC10076166). I fetched and read the article's own text directly (not a secondary summary):

- Comparing monthly U.S. import-TEU indices (Global Port Tracker / Hackett Associates) against U.S. Census retail-inventory indices, the authors found that **from January 2018 through July 2021, there was a "relatively strong negative correlation between retail inventory- and import TEU indices with a 4-month lag (corresponding roughly to the time between import orders and arrival)."**
- **During the 2020–2021 pandemic surge specifically, that negative correlation was even stronger, again at a 4-month lag.**
- Interpretation: a drop in retail inventories today predicts an import surge roughly 4 months later, and that import surge tends to continue until retailers' target inventory levels are restored. The authors explicitly tested and rejected two more "obvious" leading indicators — credit-card spending and consumer sentiment — because neither showed a consistent, well-timed lead relationship; retail inventory was "the strongest and most useful" leading relationship they found.
- The paper frames the 4-month figure as "corresponding roughly to the time between import orders and arrival" — i.e., it captures not just ocean transit (typically 3–5 weeks from Asia) but the full order-placement-to-shelf cycle: manufacturing lead time, booking/transit, port dwell, inland drayage, warehouse receipt, and putaway before the goods register as inventory.

## What I could and couldn't verify independently in this session

I attempted to reproduce this cross-correlation directly against a live government time series rather than relying on the article alone:

- `transport.bts_port_teu` in this connector carries actual monthly container-TEU throughput for the top-10 U.S. ports, Jan 2019–Oct 2022 (46 months) — a real, government-sourced (BTS Port Performance Freight Statistics Program) proxy for port import volume. I pulled and charted this series (see dashboard/chart delivered alongside this answer): it shows the same broad shape the paper describes — a 2019 plateau, a sharp COVID dip in April 2020, and a sustained 2020–2022 surge to new highs.
- I could **not** independently recompute the lag itself, because this connector has no usable historical retail-inventory series overlapping that window: `econ.fred_indicators` (which carries the FRED series `RETAILIMSA`, Census retail inventories) is only loaded for 2026 (6 rows), and `econ.business_indicators` is similarly current-year-only. I also tried fetching FRED's own CSV/data endpoints directly (`fred.stlouisfed.org`) and the Census MTIS timeseries API; the former timed out consistently (confirmed with an unrelated series too, so this is a general connectivity limitation, not specific to retail inventories) and the latter requires a Census API key I don't have. So the 4-month figure above is the published, peer-reviewed, primary-source result — not a number I recomputed in-engine — and I'm stating that distinction explicitly rather than implying I re-derived it.

## Caveats on generalizing this figure

- The 4-month lag was fitted over Jan 2018–Jul 2021, a window dominated by the pre-pandemic steady state and the 2020–2021 pandemic demand shock. The paper itself doesn't claim the lag is a structural constant — it's an empirical average over that period, and the authors' own within-sample comparison (pandemic sub-period vs. full period) shows the *strength* of the correlation, not the *lag length*, changed across regimes (strength rose, lag stayed at 4 months in both cuts they report).
- Container throughput (TEU) and retail inventory dollars are two different units on two different indices; the "4 months" describes when the two series' fluctuations align best, not a fixed physical transit time for any single shipment.
- The relationship is a leading-indicator correlation for the *aggregate* retail sector, not a guarantee for any individual retailer or product category — the paper notes fast-moving categories (electronics, apparel) behave differently from bulky/seasonal categories (furniture) within the same aggregate.
