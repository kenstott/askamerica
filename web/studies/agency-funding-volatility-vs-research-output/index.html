q117 · askamerica

How much does year-to-year volatility in an agency's budget show up in what its funded researchers produce?

Answer

A little, in the expected (negative) direction, but the connection is weak, hard to pin down precisely, and the naive/simple version of the relationship is not statistically robust.

Using HHS (NIH's parent, the dominant and most-studied federal research funder) as the case agency, and USPTO patents as the feasible output metric in this corpus, a two-way (institution + year) fixed-effects panel across 202 of the largest HHS-funded U.S. research institutions (2010-2022, patents lagged 2 years) finds:

  • Preferred specification (institution + year fixed effects, cluster-robust SEs): a 1-unit increase in |year-over-year %Δ HHS funding| is associated with -0.051 fewer patents 2 years later (SE 0.030, p = 0.091, n = 2,141 observations, 202 institutions, 12 years). Directionally consistent with the literature (funding instability crowds out research time with grant-writing), but only marginally significant — not below the conventional p<0.05 threshold.
  • Robustness check (mandatory leave-one-out on the same relationship without fixed effects, top 150 institutions by cumulative funding): the pooled/no-FE correlation is null and fragile — coefficient +0.025, p = 0.97, and the sign flips when either Johns Hopkins or Northwestern is dropped from the sample (robust: false, coefficient range across 149 leave-one-out refits: -0.019 to +0.39). This is an important finding in its own right: a single naive correlation on this question would be actively misleading, and the fixed-effects specification is materially more defensible.
  • National aggregate context (not causal): total HHS funding to these 202 institutions dipped in 2013-2015 (the sequestration era) before resuming a steady nominal climb through 2024, while total patents granted to the same institutions rose steadily throughout, including through the sequestration dip — consistent with the estimated effect being small.

What the published literature already establishes (read before querying)

  • NIH's 1998-2003 budget doubling produced a well-documented "boom-bust": success rates collapsed after 2003 without funding falling, producing chronic hypercompetition — Freeman & Van Reenen, Issues in Science and Technology.
  • The 2013 sequestration is linked to a delayed, measurable decline in NIH-supported basic-science publications, especially non-clinical work — bioRxiv 2025.07.15.664939.
  • Azoulay & Li and Azoulay/Graff Zivin/Manso show funding structure (renewal-cycle length, tolerance for early failure) shapes what researchers attempt, not just funding level — NBER w26889, HHMI-vs-NIH study.
  • A 2022 review of European funding: the field is shifting "from excellence to luck and timing" as funding becomes more volatile — PMC9639839.
  • Direct researcher/university-level evidence is genuinely mixed: Lawson et al. (2021) found no significant funding→productivity effect in social sciences/economics (Scientometrics 2025); other NIH work finds a positive but diminishing-returns effect of funding level (bioRxiv 367847).
  • The 2025-2026 NSF/NIH funding disruptions are already producing self-reported productivity effects — researchers report spending more time on grant-writing and less on research, with junior researchers reporting career-threatening uncertainty — STAT News, Aug 2025, STAT News, Mar 2026.
  • None of this literature isolates year-to-year volatility (as distinct from funding level or a one-time cut) with a clean output elasticity — that gap is what the computed analysis above tries to fill.

Method (what was computed vs. relayed)

Computed via the AskAmerica connector (not relayed from any source): for each of 202 institutions with both an NSF HERD federal-funding record and a matched USPTO patent-assignee ID (joined through ref.canonical_org_entity), the absolute year-over-year % change in HHS-sourced federal R&D expenditure (research.nsf_herd_by_institution) was computed per year 2010-2022 and matched to that institution's granted-patent count 2 years later (patents.patent_grants / patents.patent_assignees). Ran panel_fixed_effects (institution + year FE, cluster-robust SEs) as the primary specification, then a mandatory sensitivity_analysis leave-one-out check on a comparable pooled (no-FE) specification, restricted to the top 150 institutions to satisfy the tool's 200-group cap.

Caveats (disclosed, not smoothed over)

  • Patents are a weak proxy for HHS/NIH output specifically. Biomedical basic science overwhelmingly produces publications, not patents; askamerica has no publication-count table, so the literature's strongest volatility→output evidence (sequestration → declining basic-science publications) could not be directly replicated here — a genuine coverage gap.
  • 2-year patent lag is a simplification; grant-to-patent lags vary by field. Not separately tested at 1-year/3-year lags given time budget.
  • 2023 patents.patent_grants coverage returned null in the national aggregate — flagged per the source's declared coverage lag, not filled in as a zero.
  • HHS stands in for "an agency" — the largest, most-litigated case (NIH). NSF/DOD/other agencies were not separately modeled.
