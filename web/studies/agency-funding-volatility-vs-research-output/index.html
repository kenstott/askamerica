NIH budget volatility does show up in what funded researchers produce — but mostly at the margin, amplified 30-70x, not proportionally across the whole portfolio

Case study: NIH appropriations vs. research-grant output, FY1996-FY2026 (nominal $)

Summary

Using NIH as the case study (the one agency where both budget history and grant-level output data are publicly detailed), year-to-year budget volatility shows up in what funded researchers produce very unevenly: the total funded portfolio (dollars committed, total number of active awards) is remarkably stable year to year, but the margin — new/competing grants, and the success rate applicants face — absorbs almost all of the volatility, often wildly amplified relative to the dollar change. In the one episode with exact matching primary-source figures, a budget change of -0.58% (FY2024 to FY2025) coincided with a -39.6% drop in new competing research grants funded, roughly a 68x amplification. A parallel, less precisely dated episode (the 2003-2006 post-doubling slowdown) shows the same pattern: NIH held average award value flat and absorbed the funding deceleration almost entirely by cutting the number of new awards and letting the success rate fall. The true 'output' economists and NIH itself care about most — papers, citations, trained researchers — lags this by roughly the multi-year length of a grant, and the literature confirms real hits there too (publication declines after the 2013 sequester; researcher-reported disruption after 2025's funding instability) but that output is not something we could measure directly from government microdata; it comes from academic literature and surveys, cited below.

What 'agency budget volatility' means here, precisely

NIH's total program-level appropriation (nominal dollars, from CRS Report R43341) has gone through four distinct volatility regimes since FY1996: (1) FY1999-FY2003, the 'budget doubling' era, +14.0% to +16.5% every single year; (2) FY2003-FY2015, much slower nominal growth punctuated by outright nominal cuts in FY2006, FY2011, and FY2013 (the FY2013 sequestration cut was -5.0%); (3) FY2016-FY2023, steady increases of 3%-9% a year; and (4) FY2024-FY2026, essentially flat (-0.8%, -0.6%, +1.0%). In inflation-adjusted (BRDPI) terms, NIH's purchasing power peaked in FY2003 and, per CRS, the FY2026 level remains about 10.3% below that 2003 peak even after two decades of subsequent nominal increases — a reminder that 'volatility' in nominal appropriations understates real volatility once biomedical-specific inflation is netted out.

What 'what funded researchers produce' means here, and the gap in this corpus

The AskAmerica connector has no table of publications, citations, or patents attributable to a specific funding agency's grantees — confirmed via search_catalog and describe_table across the research, patents, and sec schemas. The closest AskAmerica-native proxy is research.nih_award_projects (NIH RePORTER award microdata), which covers only FY2022-2024 and FY2026 (FY2025 is an interior gap in the table) and only measures award count/dollars, not output. Querying it directly shows the funded-award count for the whole NIH portfolio is nearly flat across the years it does cover: 79,708 (FY2022), 80,843 (FY2023), 79,245 (FY2024), and 51,142 for the partial FY2026 in progress, at roughly $47-48B/year — consistent with the CRS budget figures for the same years. That flatness is itself informative: it shows the *existing* funded-researcher population barely moves year to year, because most of a year's award total is continuing (already-committed) grants, not new ones. It is the marginal, competitively-awarded slice of the portfolio, and the resulting success rate, where volatility actually lands — a distinction this analysis had to go outside the AskAmerica corpus to establish, using CRS's report of new *competing* grants specifically (10,086 in FY2024 vs. 6,095 in FY2025, a distinction the connector's own table does not carry).

The sharpest documented episode: FY2024 to FY2025

This is the one episode where an exact, same-source, same-methodology pair of before/after numbers exists. Per CRS R43341 (citing NIH's own FY2026 budget justification, p.23): NIH's program-level budget fell from $47.311B (FY2024) to $47.035B (FY2025), a change of -0.58%. Over the same one-year span, the number of new competing research grants funded fell from 10,086 to 6,095, a change of -39.57% — roughly 68 times the proportional size of the budget change. This was not purely a dollar story: CRS and GAO (B-337203, Aug. 2025) attribute the disproportionate drop to two mechanisms layered on top of the small budget cut — (1) a new FY2025 policy reserving half of competing-grant funds for grants paid in a single multi-year lump sum rather than year-by-year, which mechanically reduces the number of new starts per dollar, and (2) a documented slowdown in NIH's pace of obligating funds (GAO found NIH had obligated about $8 billion less between February and June 2025 than in the same months of FY2024, though CRS's later analysis found NIH ultimately obligated nearly all of its FY2025 budget authority by year-end). The lesson: the size of the output swing an agency's researchers experience is not simply a readout of the size of its budget swing — administrative and policy choices made *in response to* a small budget change can amplify it by an order of magnitude or more in what gets funded that year.

A parallel historical episode: the 2003-2006 post-doubling slowdown

The pattern recurs, though the primary-source figures for this earlier episode are less precisely dated in what could be retrieved here. Following NIH's five-year budget-doubling period (which ended in FY2003 at $27.167B), nominal budget growth decelerated sharply and the agency actually cut its FY2006 appropriation slightly (-0.1%, the first NIH nominal decrease since FY1970, per CRS). The R01-equivalent success rate — the share of grant applications that get funded — fell from roughly 30-31% during 1997-2003 (the doubling era, when application volume also surged) to 25% in FY2004 and into the low-to-mid 20s through the rest of the decade (NIH Data Book / RePORT series, compiled at drugmonkey.wordpress.com 2025-07-30). NIH's own budget-planning approach in this period, as documented in NIGMS and other IC funding-trend writeups, was to protect the real value of individual awards and the continuation of existing grants first, absorbing the funding slowdown mainly by reducing the number of *new* awards and letting the success rate fall — the same triage pattern seen again in FY2025.

Where the true output — publications, citations, trained scientists — is affected (literature, not computed here)

Because no government microdata table in this corpus links a funding agency's year-to-year budget to its grantees' publication or citation output, this link rests on the published literature rather than a number computed here. Findings worth weighing: after the 2013 Budget Control Act sequestration, the count of NIH-funded 'fundamental' and 'mixed mostly fundamental' papers declined while human-focused paper output plateaued (bioRxiv, 'Absolute and Relative Declines in NIH-Funded Basic Science Publications', 2025). A PNAS analysis found the 2025 wave of NIH grant terminations directly removed $2.45 billion from active research and disproportionately affected early-career researchers and women scientists (as summarized in STAT News, Aug. 26 2025, drawing on a survey of hundreds of biomedical researchers reporting funding instability's effects on their work). The Association of American Universities separately documented the FY2025 grantmaking slowdown from the recipient-institution side, corroborating the CRS/GAO figures above. None of these are re-derived from primary tables here; they are reported as literature findings, distinct from the CRS/GAO/AskAmerica-sourced numbers above.

Bottom line and caveats

Year-to-year NIH budget volatility shows up in researcher output disproportionately at the margin — new grants and success rates swing far more than the dollar totals that drive them, sometimes by an order of magnitude or more, because NIH's own triage behavior and administrative/policy responses amplify small budget moves into large changes in what gets newly funded. The stable core of the funded-researcher population (continuing grants) is comparatively insulated. What could not be established quantitatively here, for lack of any publications/citations-by-agency table anywhere in this corpus, is the size of the resulting effect on actual scientific output (papers, citations) a year or two downstream — that link is real and documented in the literature cited above, but not something this analysis computed directly. This is a single-agency (NIH) case study; the mechanism (a buffer of continuing grants, discretion over new-award count and size) is common to other large granting agencies (NSF, DOE Office of Science) but the specific FY2025 elasticity is NIH-specific and should not be assumed to transfer at the same magnitude to other agencies without their own primary data.
