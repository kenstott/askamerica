Federal research-funding volatility shows up in output composition (fewer/riskier publications, more patents), but the year-to-year transmission at state grain is only weakly, not significantly, measurable in this corpus

NSF HERD federal R&D dollars vs. USPTO patent output by state, FY2010-2024, plus the peer-reviewed literature on funding shocks

Summary

The direct answer this corpus can actually verify: yes, some -- but it is not a clean, single-agency, dollar-for-dollar transmission, and the honest number is small and noisy. No table here carries one named federal research-funding agency's own year-to-year appropriations at multi-year grain (see the data-gap note below), so this analysis uses the best available proxy: total federal R&D dollars flowing to universities across all funding agencies combined (NSF's HERD survey), against USPTO patent output, both at state grain, FY2010-2024.

Two independent tests both point the same direction, weakly: (1) a same-year, state-and-year fixed-effects panel regression finds patent-output growth moves roughly 1-for-1 with funding growth, but the estimate is not statistically significant (p=0.17) once state and year trends are absorbed; (2) across the 50 states + DC, the ones whose federal R&D dollars swing more from year to year also have more volatile patent output (r=0.43, R²=0.19, n=51). That second result says volatility itself -- not just the direction of funding change -- correlates with instability in what gets produced, but a correlation of 0.43 across 51 units explains under a fifth of the variance and cannot be read causally.

The published economics literature adds real substance this corpus's own tables cannot: it finds that when federal funding to a research area contracts, output does not just shrink uniformly -- it changes composition. Publications fall (especially the highest-quality, most basic ones); patents actually rise, but they are lower-quality, less general, and more often privately assigned, because labs shift toward private funding that has different incentives. That is the more precise and more literature-grounded answer to "how does budget volatility show up in output": less through overall output level and more through what kind of output researchers produce.

Step 1 -- What this corpus does and does not carry on agency budgets over time

A genuine, confirmed gap: this warehouse has no table giving one named federal research-funding agency's own budget across multiple years at fine grain. research.nsf_federal_rd_obligations (federal R&D obligations by funding agency) was queried directly and holds exactly one fiscal year -- 707 rows, all year=2024 -- a snapshot, not a series. research.nih_award_projects, which does carry an agency_ic column (NIH Institute/Center) and was explicitly built, per its own table description, to make an 'agency-funding-volatility-vs-research-output' analysis possible, is declared for FY2022-2026 but on live inspection holds 65,600 rows, every one of them fiscal_year=2026 -- FY2022-2025 are entirely missing from the table despite being in its declared window. This is an ingestion gap, not a design limit, and has been reported via this session's issue log.

research.nih_publications (funded-project-to-PMID publication links) does cover FY2022-2026 cleanly, but without the matching multi-year award/agency table it cannot be tied back to a specific IC's budget swings for anything before FY2026.

Given that, the best multi-year, agency-adjacent proxy available is research.research_herd_rd_by_state_year: federal R&D dollars (summed across every funding agency -- NIH, NSF, DOD, DOE, USDA, etc.) that flow to universities, at state x year grain, FY2010-2024 (15 years, contiguous). Paired with USPTO patent grants (patents.patent_grants joined through patent_assignees/patent_locations to get the assignee's state), this gives a genuine multi-year, multi-state panel -- at the cost of being a national, cross-agency measure rather than one agency's own budget line.

Step 2 -- Same-year transmission: does a funding swing show up as an output swing the same year?

Built a state x year panel (2010-2024) of year-over-year percent change in federal R&D dollars and year-over-year percent change in in-state patent grants, then ran a two-way fixed-effects regression (state and year effects absorbed, cluster-robust standard errors by state) of patent growth on funding growth.

The point estimate for the same-year relationship (~1.0) says a 1 percentage-point swing in federal R&D funding growth is associated with roughly a 1 percentage-point swing in patent-output growth -- an economically large, near 1-for-1 transmission if it were real -- but the confidence interval is wide enough (SE 0.74) that zero, and even a negative relationship, cannot be ruled out. The 2-year-lag version, testing whether funding swings show up in output after the multi-year lag typical of grant-to-patent timelines, is even weaker and equally insignificant. This is not surprising: patent grant dates lag filing by 1-3+ years due to USPTO prosecution backlogs that have nothing to do with the underlying research's funding year, which mechanically blurs any same-year or short-lag signal.

What was excluded to build this panel: both the contemporaneous and lagged regressions above are computed only on rows where a year-over-year growth rate could actually be formed. Excluded from the SQL by construction (via funding_growth IS NOT NULL AND patent_growth IS NOT NULL): each state's very first year in the window (2010 has no 2009 to compute a growth rate from) and any state-year where either the prior-year funding figure or prior-year patent count was exactly zero (growth undefined, division by zero). This drops roughly one observation per state from a theoretical maximum of ~53x15=795 down to the 735 (contemporaneous) and 629 (2-lag) actually used -- an unavoidable mechanical consequence of computing a growth rate, not a selective drop of any state or year, and it does not change which states or years are represented, only that each series starts one (or two) years later.

Step 3 -- Cross-sectional volatility: do more-volatile-funding states have more-volatile output?

Separately from the year-to-year transmission question, computed each state's overall funding volatility (standard deviation of year-over-year % funding change, 2010-2024) and its overall patent-output volatility (same measure, on patent counts), then correlated the two.

Headline figure: r = 0.43, R² = 0.19, n = 51 (the 50 states + DC). States whose federal research dollars bounce around more from year to year do tend to have more volatile patent output -- a real, positive, moderate association. R² = 0.19 means funding volatility statistically accounts for under a fifth of the variation in output volatility across states; the rest is other factors (industry mix, university research portfolio, exposure to a small number of large grants or institutions, etc.).

Disclosed exclusion #1 -- five U.S. territories excluded from the headline n=51 figure: the SQL filter state_fips NOT IN ('60','66','69','72','78') excludes Puerto Rico, Guam, American Samoa, U.S. Virgin Islands and the Northern Mariana Islands from the headline correlation, to keep the comparison to states + DC, a coherent unit type. Re-running the identical correlation WITHOUT that filter (territories included, n=53) raises the correlation to r = 0.77 -- substantially stronger. That difference is not a data error; the territories have very small patent counts and very small research bases, so a handful of patents swings their year-over-year percentage change enormously, and Puerto Rico/Guam sit at the high end of both funding and patent volatility, pulling the fitted line toward them. We report r=0.43 (states + DC only, territories excluded) as the headline because territories are not comparable units to states on this measure; the r=0.77 figure (territories included) is reported here for transparency, not suppressed.

Disclosed exclusion #2 -- rows dropped by the null-value filters: the same funding_growth IS NOT NULL AND patent_growth IS NOT NULL filters used in Step 2 also apply here, dropping each state's first undefined growth-rate year from the volatility calculation (the same mechanical exclusion described in Step 2, not a selective one). Separately, the underlying patent-by-state query excludes 15 (grant_year, state) groups where pl.state_fips IS NOT NULL failed -- i.e. patents whose assignee location could not be resolved to a US state (foreign-located or unresolved assignees, pooled together rather than assigned to any state). 15 out of roughly 13,000 grouped rows is a small share and does not plausibly change either correlation's direction.

Step 4 -- What the published economics literature adds

The strongest, most precise evidence on this question does not come from this corpus's own tables -- it comes from a specific line of empirical economics research using exactly the right natural experiment: year-to-year swings in Congress's own research-funding priorities across narrow scientific subfields.

Babina, He, Howell, Perlman and Staudt's NBER working paper 'The Color of Money: Federal vs. Industry Funding of University Research' (NBER WP 28160) documents directly that 'changes in congressional funding priorities alter the distribution of funds across narrow research fields,' and that these 'aggregate changes are highly volatile and mean-reverting' -- i.e., agency/subfield-level research budgets genuinely do swing year to year in a way that is not tied to underlying technological opportunity. Their companion paper, 'Cutting the Innovation Engine: How Federal Funding Shocks Affect University Patenting, Entrepreneurship, and Publications' (Quarterly Journal of Economics, 2023), traces what those shocks do to output: funding cuts to a researcher's specific field reduce publications -- especially the highest-quality, most basic-science ones -- while simultaneously increasing patenting, because labs shift toward private funding sources with different incentives. Those additional patents are lower-quality, less general, and disproportionately assigned to the private funder rather than kept open.

This is a sharper and more causally credible answer than either of this corpus's own regressions can give: budget volatility does not mainly show up as researchers simply producing less (or more) of the same thing -- it shows up as a shift in the KIND of output, away from open, citation-generating publications and toward narrower, privately-captured patents, as labs substitute private for federal support during a downturn.

Bottom line

Year-to-year federal research-funding volatility does show up in what funded researchers produce, but the transmission this corpus can directly measure at state grain is weak and not statistically significant on its own (contemporaneous elasticity ~1.0, p=0.17; cross-state volatility correlation r=0.43, sensitive to whether small territories are included). The published field-specific, subfield-grain literature finds a real and larger effect than either of these state-level results capture, but the effect is compositional rather than a simple volume effect: funding contractions push output away from open publications and toward patents, and toward private funding sources whose incentives differ from federal ones. The state-level, cross-agency proxy used here is a genuine second-best; the multi-year, agency-specific award data (research.nih_award_projects) that would let this be answered precisely for a single named agency is a confirmed gap in this corpus as of 2026-09-26.

What This Report Does Not Answer

  • Identify a specific federal agency's year-to-year budget volatility: No table in this warehouse carries a single named federal research-funding agency's own appropriations at multi-year grain. research.nsf_federal_rd_obligations (federal R&D obligations by funding agency) is single-year only -- confirmed live, 707 rows, all dated 2024. research.nih_award_projects (NIH award-level, which carries agency_ic) is declared FY2022-2026 but confirmed live to hold rows for FY2026 only (65,600 rows; zero rows for FY2022-2025). Substituted the broadest available multi-year proxy instead: total federal R&D dollars flowing to universities across ALL funding agencies combined (NSF HERD survey, research.research_herd_rd_by_state_year, FY2010-2024), at state grain.
