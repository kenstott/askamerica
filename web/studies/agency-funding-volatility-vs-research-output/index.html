Budget volatility passes through to research output mainly via interruption-driven attrition, not smooth output elasticity — but this corpus cannot trace it end-to-end for one agency

USAspending obligations FY2017-2025; output effects from published economics-of-science literature

Summary

Year-to-year swings in a research agency's budget show up in researcher output mainly through discontinuities — gaps in grant funding that push lab personnel out of employment or out of the country — rather than through a smooth, proportional "less money in, less output out" relationship. A funding interruption of just 30+ days on a single-R01 lab raises the probability its personnel stop working in the US by about 3 percentage points (a 40% relative increase), and those pushed into nonemployment are about 90% less likely to publish that year (Tham & Staudt, 2024). Separately, grant-renewal cycles that are "unforgiving of failure" (the norm for NIH R01s, vs. HHMI's longer-horizon awards) measurably suppress high-risk, high-impact work (Azoulay, Graff Zivin & Manso, 2011). This connector's own data confirms that agency budgets genuinely do vary in volatility — NSF's obligations grew steadily (year-over-year std. dev. ±2.3 points, FY2018-2025) while HHS's (which include NIH) swung far more (±6.7 points, driven largely by COVID-era spending) — but the corpus has no multi-year, agency-linked output series (publications or patents attributable to a specific funder by year) to directly regress against that volatility, so the causal link itself is drawn from published research, not computed here.

What the warehouse data shows: budget volatility is real and agency-specific

Querying fiscal.usaspending_by_agency (USAspending Spending Explorer obligations, FY2017-2025, the only multi-year federal agency-budget series in this corpus) for the two clearest research-funding agencies available:

  • National Science Foundation — obligations grew from $7.75B (FY2017) to $9.89B (FY2025). Year-over-year growth ranged from -1.2% to +5.6%, with a standard deviation of ±2.3 percentage points — a comparatively steady funding path.
  • Department of Health and Human Services (NIH is not broken out separately in this table — it is folded into HHS's total, which is dominated by Medicare/Medicaid, not research) — obligations grew from $1.65T to $2.79T, with year-over-year growth ranging from +0.9% to +21.2% (the FY2019-2020 COVID jump) and a standard deviation of ±6.7 points. This is a much noisier series, but it reflects entitlement-spending volatility far more than research-budget volatility specifically, so it is not a clean proxy for NIH's research budget alone.

This corpus has no separate NIH-only budget series, and its two candidate output tables for NIH — research.nih_award_projects (NIH award-level grant microdata) and research.nsf_federal_rd_obligations/research.nsf_herd_by_institution — each carry only a single loaded fiscal year (nih_award_projects: FY2026 only, 59,813 awards; NSF federal R&D obligations by field: FY2022 only; HERD: FY2024 only), not the multi-year time series a volatility-to-output regression needs. patents.patent_grants has a real multi-year series (2010-2025) but carries no field attributing a patent to the federal agency that funded the underlying research, so it cannot be joined to agency budget swings either. This is a genuine coverage gap, not a result — the causal estimates below come from the published economics-of-science literature, not from a computation run in this session.

What published research shows: volatility bites through interruptions, not smooth elasticity

Funding gaps directly push researchers out of the workforce and out of publishing. Tham & Staudt (2024), "Scientific Talent Leaks Out of Funding Gaps," link IRS earnings records and university transaction data in a difference-in-differences design around NIH R01 grants. They find a funding interruption of more than 30 days on a single-grant lab raises the probability its personnel stop working in the US by about 3 percentage points — a 40% relative increase. About half of those pushed into nonemployment appear to permanently leave the US, and are about 90% less likely to publish in a given year, with the largest effects on postdocs and graduate students (trainees). Among personnel who keep working in the US, an interrupted funding history is associated with 20% lower earnings than continuously-funded peers. The authors estimate funding delays account for roughly 5% of US nonemployment in their sample — a national-scale effect, not a curiosity.

Renewal-cycle structure, not just funding level, shapes what gets produced. Azoulay, Graff Zivin & Manso (2011, RAND Journal of Economics), comparing HHMI investigators (long time horizons, tolerant of early failure) against similarly-accomplished NIH-funded scientists (R01's roughly 3-year cycle, renewal unforgiving of failure), find HHMI investigators produce high-impact papers at a much higher rate and pursue more novel research directions. The mechanism is the incentive structure imposed by funding instability/renewal risk, not simply dollar volume.

Simulation work on sustained budget disruption reaches the same qualitative conclusion at a system level. A 2026 BRIDGE model (published in npj Health Systems) linking NIH funding to drug approvals, workforce dynamics and scientific output across 47 longitudinal measures (1995-2024) finds that even temporary NIH funding disruptions create lasting setbacks to drug development, researcher training and basic science that later funding increases do not fully repair for decades — consistent with volatility having asymmetric, sticky effects rather than a reversible elastic response.

Funding level does buy some output, with diminishing returns. Separately from volatility, studies of funding level (not its year-to-year variability) find a positive but concave relationship: roughly 10-16 additional scientific articles per $1 million in university research funding, with returns diminishing at higher funding levels — a different, non-volatility question, included here to distinguish "more money helps somewhat" from "unstable money actively disrupts."

Bottom line

The honest answer has two parts. (1) Federal research-agency budgets genuinely do vary in year-to-year volatility — confirmed directly from USAspending obligations data, with NSF comparatively steady and HHS's (including NIH) far noisier, largely due to non-research entitlement swings. (2) The transmission from that volatility into researcher output is not a smooth elasticity visible in this corpus's data — it operates mainly through discrete funding-gap events that knock personnel out of employment (with ~90% publication drop-off for those affected) and through renewal-cycle incentive structures that discourage risk-taking, both documented in the published economics-of-science literature rather than computed here. A materially better answer would need an agency-specific, multi-year budget series (isolating NIH or NSF research obligations alone, not HHS's entitlement-dominated total) paired with a multi-year, funder-attributed output series (publications or patents tagged to the funding agency and year) — neither exists together in this connector today.

What This Report Does Not Answer

  • Show how that volatility shows up in what funded researchers produce (output): This corpus has no multi-year, agency-attributed research-output series (publications/patents tied to a specific funding agency by year) to compute this directly — nih_award_projects, nsf_federal_rd_obligations, and nsf_herd_by_institution each hold only a single loaded fiscal year. The output-side answer is instead drawn from named published economics-of-science studies (Tham & Staudt 2024; Azoulay, Graff Zivin & Manso 2011; the 2026 BRIDGE model) with explicit effect sizes, clearly labeled as literature-sourced rather than warehouse-computed.
