Corporate climate-risk disclosure tracks industry regulatory exposure far more than actual disaster history at HQ

14 large-cap 10-K filers, FEMA disaster declarations 2015-2024, sec.mda_sections + disasters.disaster_declarations (AskAmerica)

Summary

Across a sample of 14 large public companies, how heavily a firm discusses climate/weather risk in its 10-K bears essentially no measurable relationship to how disaster-prone its headquarters state actually is (Pearson r = 0.07, p = 0.81; Spearman rank correlation rho = 0.44, p = 0.12; n = 14). What predicts disclosure intensity instead is industry and regulatory exposure: rate-regulated electric utilities (Southern Company, Xcel Energy, PG&E, NextEra, Duke Energy) devote 3-21 climate/weather mentions per 100 MD&A paragraphs, while national retailers, banks, a hotel operator and a REIT (Walmart, JPMorgan, American Express, Marriott, Simon Property) devote essentially zero — even though several of those firms are headquartered in, or operate heavily in, disaster-prone states. This matches the peer-reviewed literature: recent research finds only a weak, marginally significant link between assessed physical climate risk and what firms actually disclose, and separate work documents disclosure that expands independent of ("decoupled" from) underlying physical exposure. The clearest positive case in the sample — PG&E — shows disclosure that IS specific and tied to named real events (the 2018 Camp Fire, the 2020 Zogg Fire, the Paradise rebuild), but even there, mention volume fell in the most recent filing (254 → 226 → 139 across three consecutive 10-Ks) while California's disaster count did not fall nearly as sharply, showing that even a well-matched case does not track cleanly year to year.

1. What the published literature already says

A 2026 Sustainability study using an ESRS E1 Climate Reporting Readiness Index found the association between identified physical climate risk and financial disclosure to be "weak and only marginally statistically significant," concluding physical risks remain harder to quantify and integrate into disclosure than transition risks. A 2022 Review of Accounting Studies paper ("The real effects of risk disclosures: evidence from climate change reporting in 10-Ks") documents that 10-K climate disclosure has real capital-allocation consequences, implying disclosure is not neutral boilerplate — but does not establish that disclosure volume scales with a firm's actual physical exposure. Separate 2026 research on greenwashing documents firms expanding symbolic environmental disclosure faster than substantive action, a decoupling that intensifies under supply-chain stress. Together these support a picture in which climate-risk disclosure is driven by regulatory and reputational pressure at least as much as by measured physical exposure — exactly what the AskAmerica data below reproduces at firm level.

2. Method and data limitation (read this before the numbers)

"The disaster history of the places a company operates" is not a single number recoverable from public data at scale — a national retailer or bank has thousands of stores/branches across every state, and no source in this corpus (or, to my knowledge, publicly) geocodes 10-K disclosure language to the company's actual facility footprint. As a workable, imperfect proxy I used each company's SEC-registered headquarters state (sec.filing_metadata.business_address) and summed FEMA major-disaster-declaration counts for that state, 2015-2024 (disasters.disasters_declarations_by_state_year). This proxy is reasonable for the electric utilities in the sample, whose regulated service territory is legally bound to one or two states, and it is weak-to-meaningless for Walmart (Bentonville, AR — but stores nationwide), JPMorgan/American Express (New York, NY — national/global operations), Marriott (Bethesda, MD — hotels worldwide), Simon Property and Public Storage (national real-estate portfolios) and Norfolk Southern (rail network spanning 22 states). This mismatch is itself a finding: for a diversified national company, HQ-state disaster history is nearly uninformative about its true exposure, so a finding of "no correlation" for those firms is partly a data-availability artifact, not proof the firms are under- or over-disclosing.

Disclosure intensity was measured as the share of Item 7 (MD&A) paragraphs in each company's most recent 10-K containing at least one of: climate change, wildfire, hurricane, flood, drought, severe weather, extreme weather, natural disaster (sec.mda_sections, section='Item 7'). This captures MD&A narrative discussion, not the Item 1A Risk Factors section specifically (that text is not separately tabulated in this corpus at the same grain), so it under-counts firms that confine climate discussion to Risk Factors and say little in MD&A.

Correlating the intensity column against the disaster column: Pearson r = 0.072 (p = 0.81), Spearman rho = 0.435 (p = 0.12), n = 14 — no statistically significant relationship. Notably PG&E and Public Storage share the same HQ state (California, 148 declarations, the highest in the sample) yet PG&E's disclosure intensity is roughly 6x Public Storage's — same location, radically different disclosure, because PG&E's business (overhead power lines through fire-prone terrain) makes wildfire a direct legal-liability driver (California's SB 901 wildfire fund, inverse-condemnation exposure) while Public Storage's self-storage facilities do not carry the same regulatory framework.

4. What actually predicts disclosure: sector/regulation, not location

The five regulated electric/gas utilities in the sample (Southern, Xcel, PG&E, NextEra, Duke) occupy the top five disclosure-intensity ranks regardless of HQ-state disaster count — Xcel Energy (Minnesota, only 13 FEMA declarations, one of the lower totals in the sample) discloses climate risk about as heavily as PG&E (California, 148 declarations, the highest). This is consistent with the accounting literature: utilities face storm-cost-recovery rate cases, wildfire liability statutes, and NERC/FERC reliability mandates that make weather risk a recurring, quantifiable MD&A topic independent of how often FEMA actually declares a disaster in their territory. By contrast, national retailers, card networks/banks and hospitality/REIT firms with globally or nationally distributed real estate show near-zero MD&A climate mentions even when headquartered in states with real disaster exposure (e.g., Walmart in Arkansas, 17 declarations, still 0 mentions) — for these firms, climate risk is diffused across thousands of sites and appears to get folded into generic "business interruption" language rather than climate-specific narrative, if it appears at all in MD&A.

5. A within-firm check: does disclosure track a company's own disaster trend over time?

For PG&E specifically, whose service territory is essentially all of Northern/Central California, wildfire mentions ARE grounded in real, named events — the sampled MD&A text explicitly cites the 2018 Camp Fire, the 2020 Zogg Fire, and the Paradise rebuild with dollar figures, not generic hedging language. That is a genuine match between disclosure and actual disaster history at the qualitative level. But the trend does not track cleanly: PG&E's climate/weather mention count fell across its three most recent 10-Ks (254 in the FY2023 filing, 226 in FY2024, 139 in FY2025) while California's FEMA disaster-declaration count over the same window (15 in 2024, 8 in 2025) also fell, but not proportionally — and 2018-2020, the years of the fires actually cited in the most recent filing, were among California's highest-disaster years (29 and 29 declarations respectively) years before the mention count peaked. Disclosure volume lags and smooths the underlying event history rather than moving in lockstep with it.

6. Bottom line

On this sample, the match between disclosed climate risk and actual disaster history is weak and not statistically distinguishable from zero when measured across companies by HQ-state disaster count. Where a real match does appear — PG&E's wildfire disclosures naming actual fires — it holds at the qualitative/sector level (regulated single-state utility, legally exposed to wildfire liability) rather than as a clean quantitative dose-response to disaster frequency. The stronger predictor of disclosure intensity in this data is a firm's sector and regulatory-liability structure, not the raw disaster count of the place it calls home — consistent with the academic finding that physical-risk disclosure is only weakly and inconsistently tied to measured physical exposure, and with parallel research on disclosure-action decoupling.

What This Report Does Not Answer

  • Cover 'the places it operates', not just headquarters: No source in this corpus (or found externally) geocodes a company's full multi-site facility footprint to join against disaster data at scale. I used HQ state as a proxy and explicitly flagged that this is a strong proxy only for single-state regulated utilities and a weak one for national retailers/banks/REITs/railroads — this limitation is stated in Section 2 and reflected in the 'no significant correlation' result, which is partly a proxy-quality artifact for the diversified firms in the sample.
