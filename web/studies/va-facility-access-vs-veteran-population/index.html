Most veterans are close to some VA facility, but distance to comprehensive or specialty VA care varies enormously — from minutes in cities to 200+ miles in rural states

Sources: RAND (2015, VA-commissioned), VA Office of Rural Health, VA MISSION Act access standards, GAO. AskAmerica's own geocoded VA-facility table (lands.va_facilities) was unavailable this run — see note below.

Summary

Most veterans live fairly close to some VA presence, but how far they are from care that can actually treat them varies hugely by geography. The last national assessment (RAND, 2015, done for the VA under the Veterans Choice Act) found 93% of veterans lived within 40 driving miles of any VHA facility (including small outpatient clinics and Vet Centers), but only 55% were within 40 miles of a full-service VA medical center, and just 26% were within 40 miles of a VA medical center offering comprehensive specialty care. The VA now manages this gap with drive-time standards rather than a flat mileage rule: veterans facing more than a 30-minute average drive for primary/mental health care, or more than 60 minutes for specialty care, can get VA-paid care from a private provider under the MISSION Act. The variation is geographic and stark — veterans in large rural states (Montana, Wyoming, Alaska) routinely face 100-200+ mile trips to a full-service VAMC, sometimes 1,000+ miles in Alaska's most remote communities, while veterans in metro areas often have a facility within a short drive. About one-third of enrolled veterans (roughly 3 of 9.1 million) are classified rural or highly rural, and this population enrolls in VA care at a notably higher rate (66%) than urban veterans (47%), which raises the average access burden nationally even though it is concentrated in specific regions.

How the VA measures and manages distance today

The VA replaced its old distance-based eligibility rule with average-drive-time standards under the 2018 MISSION Act (methodology refined in a 2020 Federal Register update): 30 minutes average drive time for primary care, mental health care, and non-institutional extended care, and 60 minutes for specialty care. A veteran whose nearest appropriate VA facility exceeds that standard is generally eligible to receive VA-paid care from an in-network community (private) provider instead of traveling to a VA site. This replaced the blunter 2014 Veterans Choice Act rule, which set eligibility at a flat 40 miles from the nearest VHA facility (a rule some veterans enrolled before June 2018 are still grandfathered under). Separately, for disability compensation and pension exam travel, the VA uses a coarser urban/rural/highly-rural drive-time schedule: roughly 45 minutes for urban veterans and up to 100 minutes for rural or highly rural veterans (reduced from a 180-minute highly-rural standard used before April 2022).

How much distance varies across the country

The RAND breakdown above is itself evidence of the variation: nearly all veterans are near a VA facility, but the share close to comprehensive or specialty care drops sharply, which is a proxy for how concentrated full-service VA medical centers are in and near metro areas. Qualitatively, the extremes are well documented: the VA's own rural-veteran materials and multiple GAO reviews (e.g. GAO-24-107559, GAO-23-105544 on rural mental health access) describe veterans in low-density states such as Montana traveling 200+ miles to reach a comprehensive VAMC (the Billings VAMC's service area is larger than several entire states), and the single most extreme documented case is Hollis, Alaska, where the nearest VA medical facility is reported at over 1,000 miles away. By contrast, veterans in dense metro areas (e.g. greater Atlanta, Phoenix, San Antonio, or the New York/New Jersey corridor) typically have a VA medical center or one of the VA's roughly 1,000+ outpatient clinics and Vet Centers within a short drive. This urban/rural split is why the VA's rural population — about 4.5 million of 17.5 million living veterans, and roughly a third of the 9.1 million currently enrolled for care — is the group these access standards and the community-care option are mainly built to serve.

What this connector could and could not verify directly

AskAmerica's catalog includes a table purpose-built to answer this question directly from primary data — lands.va_facilities, a geocoded roster of all ~1,046 VA medical centers, outpatient clinics, Vet Centers, nursing homes, and residential rehab facilities, which the catalog describes as answering "nearest VA facility to a given location" via latitude/longitude. Every attempt to query or describe it this run (including after a schema-catalog rebuild) failed with an S3 404 from the underlying data store, meaning its data file is currently missing or unpublished server-side. This was reported as a data-quality defect. Absent that table, this answer relies on the cited external literature (RAND's national drive-distance assessment, VA Office of Rural Health figures, and GAO reviews) rather than a first-party computed nearest-facility distance for every U.S. veteran or county — a materially weaker basis than the connector was built to provide, and the honest gap to disclose here.

What This Report Does Not Answer

  • Compute this directly from primary/first-party geocoded VA facility data for the current U.S. veteran population: AskAmerica's lands.va_facilities table, built for exactly this computation, returned an S3 404 on every query this run (confirmed after a schema rebuild) and could not be read. The answer instead relies on the most recent published national assessment (RAND 2015) and current VA/GAO policy documentation, which is qualitatively but not numerically identical to a live per-veteran distance computation.
