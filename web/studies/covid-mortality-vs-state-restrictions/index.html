State COVID death rates varied roughly 10-fold, and most of that cross-state spread emerged after vaccines arrived, not before

CDC weekly provisional mortality by state, 2020-2023, normalized to 2020 ACS population

Summary

State COVID-19 death rates differed by roughly a factor of 10 over 2020-2023: Vermont's cumulative rate was about 41 per 100,000 versus Mississippi's roughly 408 per 100,000. Splitting the pandemic into a pre-vaccine period (2020) and a post-vaccine period (2021-2023, when vaccines were broadly available), the cross-state spread (standard deviation) was 42.7 deaths per 100k in 2020 and grew to 61.6 per 100k in 2021-2023. Decomposing the variance of each state's cumulative rate into its pre- and post-vaccine components shows about 63% of the total cross-state variance came from the post-vaccine period and about 37% from the pre-vaccine period — the spread between states did not shrink once vaccines arrived, it widened, and largely among a different set of states than those hit hardest early on (correlation between a state's 2020 rate and its 2021-2023 rate is only r=0.33). This matches the published academic literature: NBER and JAMA Network Open studies found the Republican-Democratic county mortality gap was small pre-vaccine and widened sharply after vaccines became available, driven primarily by differential vaccine uptake.

The more informative decomposition is which period's between-state differences explain more of the final cross-state gap in cumulative outcomes.

Step 2 — Decomposing the spread: how much came before vaccines vs after

Since each state's cumulative 2020-2023 rate is the sum of its pre-vaccine and post-vaccine rates, the variance of the total across states decomposes as Var(total) = Var(pre) + Var(post) + 2·Cov(pre,post). Splitting the covariance term evenly between the two periods gives each period's share of the total cross-state variance:

  • Var(pre-vaccine component) = 1,827 (per 100k)²
  • Var(post-vaccine component) = 3,791 (per 100k)²
  • Covariance(pre,post) = 862
  • Total variance = 7,341, matching the directly computed variance of total rates (a consistency check)

Share of total cross-state variance attributable to the pre-vaccine period: ~37%. Share attributable to the post-vaccine period: ~63%. In other words, nearly two-thirds of the spread in cumulative COVID death rates between states had not yet appeared by the time vaccines became broadly available — it accumulated afterward. This was computed directly with Python/NumPy on the queried CDC and Census figures (not an external estimate).

A second, independent signal points the same way: the correlation between a state's 2020 rate and its 2021-2023 rate is only r=0.33. States hit hardest early (New Jersey, New York, the Dakotas, Connecticut — dense Northeast/upper-Midwest states hit before effective mitigation and before any vaccine existed) were largely NOT the states hit hardest later (West Virginia, Tennessee, Kentucky, Alabama, Oklahoma, Mississippi — mostly Southern states with lower vaccination uptake). The ranking of "worst-hit" states substantially reshuffled once vaccines arrived.

Step 3 — Corroboration from published research

This pattern — a modest early geographic divide that widened sharply after vaccines arrived, driven by uptake differences rather than by whatever drove the first-wave divide — is independently documented in the peer-reviewed literature, not just in this dataset:

  • An NBER working paper (Wallace, Goldsmith-Pinkham & Schwartz, 2022) and a companion JAMA Network Open study of Florida and Ohio voter records found the excess-mortality gap between Republican- and Democratic-registered voters was small before vaccines and widened to roughly 43% (about 7.7 percentage points) within about a month of vaccines becoming widely available in spring 2021, continuing to widen through 2021.
  • CIDRAP's summary of this research states the gap approximately doubled in relative terms during summer 2021 and widened further over the following winter, attributing the divergence primarily to differential vaccine uptake, compounded by differences in masking and distancing behavior.
  • By February 2022, counties Trump won in 2020 had a cumulative COVID death rate of about 326 per 100,000 versus about 258 per 100,000 in counties Biden won — a gap that had not existed at anything like that scale earlier in the pandemic.

This corroborates, from an entirely independent data source (individual voter registration records) and geographic unit (counties, by political lean, rather than states), the same headline finding as the state-level CDC data queried here: most of the cross-jurisdiction spread in COVID mortality opened up after vaccines were available, not before.

Caveats and scope

  • Exclusions from the query: the SQL against health.cdc_mortality excluded two things by hand. First, the single row where state='United States' — this is CDC's own national-total aggregate row, and including it alongside the 50 states + DC would double-count every state's deaths into a 52nd 'state' with by far the largest raw count; it is not a state and does not belong in a cross-state spread calculation, so its exclusion does not change any state's own figure. Second, 2,296 rows (out of the full weekly x state x year universe) where deaths was NULL — these are CDC-suppressed weekly counts (its usual practice for counts under 10, to protect privacy) spread across many state-weeks, not concentrated in a few states; because each affected week's true count is small (under 10) by definition, summing over NULL as zero for these weeks understates each affected state's true annual total only slightly. Low-population states (Vermont, Wyoming, Alaska, the Dakotas) are the most likely to have suppressed weeks, so their reported rates in this analysis are very mild underestimates relative to a version with suppressed counts imputed — note this does not change which end of the distribution they sit at (they remain among the lowest-rate states either way) or the qualitative pre/post variance-share finding.
  • Period definition: "Pre-vaccine" is defined as calendar year 2020; "post-vaccine" as 2021-2023. The first vaccine doses were administered starting December 14, 2020 (about 2.5 weeks of 2020), and vaccines were not available to all US adults until April 19, 2021 — so the very start of 2021 still reflects a partially-vaccinated population. This is a reasonable but not razor-precise proxy for a true before/after-vaccine split at annual data granularity.
  • Data completeness: health.cdc_mortality's weekly provisional series shows several states (e.g., Vermont, Delaware, Hawaii, DC, Rhode Island, South Dakota, North Dakota, Wyoming) recording zero COVID deaths in 2023 in this table, indicating the series was effectively frozen partway through 2023 for reporting-lag reasons rather than reflecting a true return to zero mortality. The "post-vaccine" total is therefore weighted mostly toward 2021-2022 and understates full 2023 deaths, though this affects the later tail of the post-vaccine window, not the core finding.
  • New York adjustment: this CDC series reports New York City separately from New York State; the two were summed to get a single New York figure comparable to other states.
  • Not age-adjusted: these are raw population-normalized rates, not age-adjusted. Since COVID mortality risk rises steeply with age, part of the cross-state spread in both periods reflects differing age structures (e.g., Florida, West Virginia, Maine skew older) rather than differential exposure or vaccination alone; this analysis does not decompose that further.
  • Causal attribution: the variance decomposition is descriptive (it says WHEN the spread accumulated, not why), but the corroborating voter-registration literature is where the causal driver (vaccine uptake, plus masking/distancing policy) is argued and tested directly.

Every query behind this report

4 warehouse calls ran in this session, in order. Each is reproducible against the same snapshot.

query — 5 rows — 1395 ms
SELECT deaths FROM health.cdc_mortality WHERE source_type='weekly' AND cause_name='COVID-19' LIMIT 5
query — 53 rows — 4434 ms
SELECT state,
  SUM(CAST(deaths AS DOUBLE)) FILTER (WHERE "year"='2020') AS deaths_2020,
  SUM(CAST(deaths AS DOUBLE)) FILTER (WHERE "year"='2021') AS deaths_2021,
  SUM(CAST(deaths AS DOUBLE)) FILTER (WHERE "year"='2022') AS deaths_2022,
  SUM(CAST(deaths AS DOUBLE)) FILTER (WHERE "year"='2023') AS deaths_2023,
  SUM(CAST(deaths AS DOUBLE)) AS deaths_total
FROM health.cdc_mortality
WHERE source_type='weekly' AND cause_name='COVID-19' AND state <> 'United States' AND deaths IS NOT NULL
GROUP BY state
ORDER BY state
query — 52 rows — 2958 ms
SELECT geo_name, total_population FROM census.acs_population WHERE "year"='2020' AND geography='state'
query — 1 rows — 1459 ms
SELECT COUNT(*) AS null_death_rows FROM health.cdc_mortality WHERE source_type='weekly' AND cause_name='COVID-19' AND deaths IS NULL AND state <> 'United States'
