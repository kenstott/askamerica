Federal research funding per U.S. doctorate averages $1.7 million, but ranges 12-fold across fields — from $460K in math/statistics to $5.5 million in health sciences

FY2023 NSF NCSES Survey of Federal Funds for R&D (research obligations, all agencies, all performers) vs. 2023 Survey of Earned Doctorates

Summary

In FY2023, the federal government obligated about $97.1 billion to basic and applied research across all agencies and all performers (universities, federal labs, industry, nonprofits), while U.S. universities awarded 57,862 research doctorates that same year. Dividing the two gives a national average of roughly $1.68 million in federal research funding for every doctorate the U.S. produces.

That average masks an enormous spread by field: at the high end, health sciences ($13.3B obligated, 2,435 PhDs) works out to about $5.45 million per doctorate, and geosciences/atmospheric/ocean sciences to about $4.78 million. At the low end, mathematics and statistics ($997M, 2,167 PhDs) is about $460,000 per doctorate and social sciences about $515,000 — roughly a 12-fold gap between the most and least funding-intensive fields. Fields that train the most doctorates (engineering, biology) are not the ones that receive the most federal dollars per graduate; funding intensity is driven far more by the cost structure of the research itself (wet-lab biomedical and health research, large geoscience field/instrumentation programs) than by how many people a field trains.

Data and method

Numerator — federal research obligations (basic + applied, excludes experimental development) by field of R&D, FY2023, all funding agencies and all performing sectors combined, from NSF's National Center for Science and Engineering Statistics (NCSES) Survey of Federal Funds for R&D, queried via the AskAmerica research.nsf_rd_by_field table.

Denominator — doctorates (PhD/research doctorates) awarded by broad field, 2023, from NCSES's Survey of Earned Doctorates, read directly from NCSES's own published report page (ncses.nsf.gov/pubs/nsf25300/report/field-of-doctorate). The 14 SED trend broad-field counts sum exactly to NCSES's published national total of 57,862 doctorates, confirming the breakdown is complete and internally consistent.

Field mapping. Seven of NCSES's nine level-1 R&D fields (Engineering, Physical sciences, Geosciences/atmospheric/ocean sciences, Computer & information sciences, Psychology, Social sciences, Mathematics & statistics) have a direct, identically-labeled counterpart in the SED broad-field taxonomy and were matched 1:1. NCSES's broader "Life sciences" R&D category was split using its own level-2 sub-fields, which do carry SED-matching labels: "Biological and biomedical sciences" ($24,791M) and "Health sciences" ($13,281M) were used directly; "Agricultural sciences" ($1,208M) plus "Natural resources and conservation" ($360M) were summed for "Agricultural sciences and natural resources." The remaining NCSES "Other fields" bucket ($9,491M — covering education research, business, law, communications, and other non-STEM research) was matched against the sum of SED's education, humanities/arts, business, and other non-S&E doctorate counts (12,329 combined), since NCSES's field-of-R&D taxonomy was historically built for STEM research and doesn't break out humanities/education/business as separately as SED does.

Caveat on interpretation. This is a scale comparison, not a literal "cost per graduate" — the numerator funds ALL federal research performers (national labs, industry, nonprofits, and universities), not just the training pipeline that produces doctorates, and a fiscal-year obligation is not tied to any one graduate's training cost. It is best read as "how much a field's federal research enterprise is funded, relative to how many doctorate-level researchers it turns out" — a proxy for how capital-and-equipment-intensive a field's research is (health sciences and geosciences involve expensive clinical trials, large instrumentation, and field/vessel/satellite infrastructure) versus how much of that field's activity is done by doctorate-trained researchers as opposed to master's-level or non-degreed staff, industry researchers, or federal lab personnel.

Provenance. The SED figures were read directly from NCSES's own published report table (not a secondary summary) via a direct fetch of ncses.nsf.gov. The federal-obligations figures come from AskAmerica's research.nsf_rd_by_field table, itself sourced from NCSES's Survey of Federal Funds for R&D XLSX publication.
