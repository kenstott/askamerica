No dataset reports outstanding mortgage debt inside newly FEMA-remapped flood zones by lender; the nearest public proxy — $2.22T of GSE-backed loan balances exposed to future flood risk — shows the risk concentrates at the GSEs, not at any single depository lender

AskAmerica corpus (disasters, housing schemas) + NY Fed, Richmond Fed, CFPB, HUD, and NBER research, checked 2026-09-15

Summary

No dataset — in the AskAmerica corpus or published anywhere publicly — reports a dollar figure for outstanding mortgage debt sitting specifically inside properties FEMA has newly remapped into a flood zone, broken out by lender. That precise figure would require joining FEMA's Letter/Physical Map Revision (LOMR/PMR) history to loan-level mortgage servicing records carrying current unpaid balance and servicer of record — a linkage that exists only inside proprietary datasets (CoreLogic/Cotality flood-determination feeds, or the confidential McDash servicing data Fed researchers use under restricted access), never released publicly at that grain.

The nearest public, quantified proxy: the Richmond Fed (Phan, 2024), matching Fannie Mae/Freddie Mac's full 1999-2023 loan-performance database (100M+ loans) to First Street Foundation flood projections, finds 27.2% of the $8.17 trillion outstanding GSE mortgage portfolio — about $2.22 trillion — sits in ZIP codes carrying meaningful future flood risk (roughly 23.3 million loans). That is exposure to flood risk broadly, not specifically to a FEMA remap event, but it is the most authoritative public dollar figure connecting flood risk to outstanding mortgage balances at scale.

On concentration by lender: the debt is not concentrated at large depository banks — the opposite pattern holds. NY Fed research (Blickle, Perry & Santos, 2024) shows very large banks cut origination roughly 1-2 percentage points when a property's flood exposure becomes visible, while small local banks and nonbank lenders keep lending but sell or securitize those loans 1.5-2.2 percentage points more often than comparable loans, pushing the balance sheet risk downstream. That downstream destination is concentrated: the GSEs (Fannie Mae/Freddie Mac) guaranteed $6.88 trillion in mortgage debt in 2019 without pricing flood risk into guarantee fees (Ouazad & Kahn, NBER w26322) — roughly 61% of the entire $11.2 trillion US residential mortgage market that year — making the GSEs, not any individual bank, the true concentration point for this risk.

What was checked in the AskAmerica corpus

Searched schemas disasters (FEMA National Risk Index tables, nfip_claims, nfip_policies — carries a flood_zone column but no loan balances or lender identifiers), housing (housing_mortgage_originations, an HMDA-derived view — carries loan purpose, state, year and a join to SEC mortgage-lender filings, but no property-level flood-zone designation and no current outstanding-balance/servicing data), and ref (the entity-resolution join layer — no FEMA-remap-to-mortgage bridge exists). None of these tables can be joined to answer the question at the property level. FEMA map-revision history (LOMRs/PMRs) is not ingested into the corpus at all, and no table anywhere carries current unpaid mortgage balance by property. This is a genuine data gap, not a query-construction problem.

What the published research does show

  • Richmond Fed, Phan (2024), "Assessing GSE Mortgage Portfolios' Exposure to Past and Future Flood Risk" — matched the full GSE loan-performance database (1999-2023, 100M+ loans) at the 3-digit ZIP level to First Street Foundation flood-factor projections through 2050. Of 89.58 million GSE loans outstanding in January 2023 ($8.17T total balance), 26.1% of loans / 27.2% of balance ($2.22T) sit in areas at meaningfully elevated flood risk; a narrower higher-risk cut is 5.7% of loans / 5.0% of balance ($0.41T). A companion event study found Hurricane Irma raised the six-quarter default rate in affected Florida ZIPs by 0.40 percentage points against a 0.80% baseline.
  • NY Fed, Blickle, Perry & Santos (2024), Liberty Street Economics + Staff Report SR1101 — matched geocoded HMDA originations (2018-2021) to CoreLogic property-level flood risk and digitized FEMA maps. $600B+ of 2021 originations sat in high-risk areas with NO FEMA flood-map coverage at all, versus ~$200B inside currently mapped zones. Of the highest-risk decile of properties nationally, 74% lack FEMA map coverage. When flood exposure becomes visible to a lender (with or without a formal remap), very large banks cut origination ~1-2 pp; small banks and nonbanks keep lending but sell/securitize 1.5-2.2 pp more.
  • Ouazad & Kahn, NBER Working Paper 26322, "Mortgage Finance and Climate Change: Securitization Dynamics in the Aftermath of Natural Disasters" — shows lenders use GSE conforming-loan-limit bunching to offload disaster-exposed mortgages onto Fannie Mae/Freddie Mac after storms. GSEs guaranteed $6.88T in mortgage debt in 2019 without pricing flood risk into guarantee fees, against $11.2T in total US residential mortgage debt outstanding (Goodman 2020) — roughly 61% of the whole market carries GSE flood-blind guarantees.
  • CFPB, "Flood Risk and the U.S. Mortgage Market" (Jan. 2025) — merged FEMA and First Street flood data with 2018-2022 HMDA records for the southeast and central southwest census regions. Found over 400,000 homes may be underinsured for flooding in those regions alone because FEMA maps rate risk highest along the coast while First Street's forward-looking, inland-inclusive model finds broader exposure; underinsured borrowers also had lower incomes and lower down payments, i.e., the least capacity to self-insure against a remap-triggered insurance mandate.
  • HUD Cityscape (Craig, 2022), "An Overview of Flood Risk to the Housing Finance Ecosystem" — a policy synthesis noting that flood risk transition costs (map revisions, abrupt insurance mandates, tightened lending) can propagate through neighborhoods with similar risk profiles even without an acute flood event, and that over 80% of presidential disaster declarations relate to floods or flood-related events.
  • Cotality (formerly CoreLogic), "What FEMA's upcoming flood map changes mean for lenders and servicers" (May 2026) — an industry operational account confirming the mechanics: when a property is newly mapped into a Special Flood Hazard Area, the servicer must notify the borrower and flood insurance must be obtained within 45 days (lender-placed coverage otherwise), and Fannie Mae/Freddie Mac require the policy in place within 120 days of the revised map — confirming that a remap changes carrying cost and compliance risk on the existing loan balance, not the loan balance itself.

Why the exact question can't be answered with a number

To compute "outstanding mortgage debt in newly remapped-into-a-flood-zone properties, by lender" requires: (1) FEMA's LOMR/PMR history identifying which specific parcels moved into a Special Flood Hazard Area and when — exists via FEMA's Map Service Center/OpenFEMA; (2) a property-to-mortgage match giving current lender/servicer and unpaid principal balance for each of those parcels — does not exist in any public dataset; HMDA discloses only origination-year loan amount and the originating (not necessarily current-servicing) lender, never an ongoing balance; and (3) a lender-entity join to aggregate that balance by institution. Component (2) is the blocking gap: the property-level servicing data (CoreLogic/Cotality flood-determination feeds, McDash servicing records) that would close it is proprietary and restricted to institutions like the Federal Reserve under confidentiality agreements. No government agency or research team appears to have published this specific cut at the national level.

What This Report Does Not Answer

  • A dollar figure for outstanding mortgage debt in properties newly mapped into a FEMA flood zone: No public or corpus dataset joins FEMA map-revision (LOMR/PMR) history to current outstanding mortgage balances at the property level. Reported the closest available quantified proxy instead: Richmond Fed's $2.22T GSE-backed balance exposed to future flood risk (broader risk exposure, not specifically post-remap), and explained precisely which missing data component (property-level current balance/servicer linked to remap history) makes the exact figure unobtainable from any public source.
  • Whether that debt is concentrated at particular lenders: Cannot be answered for the specific 'newly remapped' population for the same data-availability reason. Answered the closely adjacent, well-studied question instead: research on newly-revealed (unmapped) flood risk shows large banks retreat from lending while small banks/nonbanks keep lending and securitize more, concentrating the downstream credit risk at the GSEs (which guarantee $6.88T in flood-risk-blind mortgage debt) rather than at any single depository lender. Labeled this a proxy finding, not a direct measurement of lender concentration in remapped zones specifically.
