Workplace death rates vary roughly 30-fold across industries and up to 3x across states — the worst combinations pair resource-extraction/logging/fishing industries with the sparsely-populated states where they are concentrated (Wyoming, Alaska, North Dakota, West Virginia)

BLS Census of Fatal Occupational Injuries (CFOI), 2023 national/industry rates and 2021-2023 state averages. This AskAmerica corpus does not carry CFOI data — figures below are from BLS primary releases and one secondary tabulation, cited individually.

Summary

Workplace fatality rates differ enormously by industry — from 3.5 deaths per 100,000 full-time-equivalent (FTE) workers averaged across all U.S. jobs in 2023 up to 98.9 per 100,000 for logging workers and 86.9 for fishing and hunting workers, roughly a 25-30x spread. By state, the gap is smaller but still large: Wyoming's 2021-2023 average rate (10.7 per 100,000) is nearly 3x the national average and about 2.8x South Carolina's (4.65), the lowest among the ten highest-rate states. The worst COMBINATIONS are not evenly distributed — they cluster where a high-fatality industry is a large share of a small state's workforce: logging in the Pacific Northwest and Appalachia, oil/gas extraction and trucking in Wyoming and North Dakota, and commercial fishing in Alaska (historically 100+ deaths per 100,000 fishing workers per year, tens of times the national average). This corpus (AskAmerica/askengine) has no occupational-fatality table at all — no CFOI, no OSHA injury/illness data — so every figure below comes directly from BLS's own CFOI releases and one secondary analysis of BLS data, not from a warehouse query.

Data gap disclosure

search_catalog was queried for 'workplace fatalities,' 'occupational injury deaths,' and 'OSHA injury illness establishment' — no table in this corpus (health, crime, transport, census, econ schemas) carries occupational fatality counts or rates by industry or state. The only county-level mortality tables are cause-of-death (overdose, suicide, homicide, firearm) and premature-death (YPLL), which do not capture occupational cause of death. This is a genuine corpus coverage gap, not a query-construction problem. BLS's own Census of Fatal Occupational Injuries (CFOI) — the authoritative source — is a keyless public program but its site blocked automated fetches (HTTP 403) during this session, so the figures below rely on BLS's published headline numbers as reported through web search and one secondary source (H&P Law's tabulation of 2021-2023 BLS state data), each cited individually below. This is disclosed rather than presented as a warehouse-verified figure.

Wyoming's rate is 184% above the national average per this analysis; a separate single-year 2024 BLS release cited in a different secondary source put Wyoming even higher, at 13.9 per 100,000 — the two do not agree exactly because one is a 3-year average and the other a single year, and single-year state CFOI rates are volatile given small annual death counts (Wyoming has roughly 30 workplace deaths a year on a workforce of under 300,000).

Worst industry-state combinations

The worst combinations are where a high-fatality industry is a large share of a small state's employment base, concentrating both the numerator (dangerous work) and a small denominator (thin overall workforce), which also makes single-year rates noisy:

  • Wyoming / North Dakota — oil & gas extraction, mining, and long-haul trucking. Both states combine top-5 fatality-rate rankings with oil, gas, and mining as leading employers; national oil-and-gas-extraction rates have historically run 10-25 per 100,000 (9.8 in 2021; a 2003-2013 average of 25.0), several times the all-industry rate.
  • Alaska — commercial fishing. Alaska ranks 3rd-8th nationally on overall state rate depending on the year/window, but its commercial fishing industry specifically has had fatality rates in the 100-400+ per 100,000 range historically (CDC/NIOSH: 128/100,000 average 1992-2008; 414.6/100,000 in 1980-1988; more recent NIOSH figures around 140/100,000, still ~20x the all-industry national rate), even as the absolute count of deaths has fallen substantially since the 1990s due to Coast Guard and vessel-safety regulation.
  • West Virginia / Montana — logging and underground mining. Both are high-overall-rate, low-population states where logging (98.9 per 100,000 nationally, the single highest civilian occupation rate) and coal/underground mining (19.6 per 100,000 in 2021) are concentrated employers.

No table in this corpus lets us compute a true state x industry cross-tabulated rate (e.g., 'logging workers in Montana specifically'); the combinations above are inferred by pairing the national industry-level rates with each state's known industry mix and its own state-level overall rate, not from a single joined dataset — that is a real limitation of this analysis, not a finding to be read as precisely as the industry-only or state-only rates above.

What This Report Does Not Answer

This is disclosed explicitly in the report rather than presented as a precise ranking.
