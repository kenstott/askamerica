Industry Gap (33x) Dwarfs State Gap (13x) in Workplace Death Rates — Worst Combos Cluster in Extraction, Fishing, Logging

BLS Census of Fatal Occupational Injuries (CFOI), 2023-2024 vintage — rate per 100,000 full-time-equivalent workers

Summary

Workplace death rates differ enormously by both industry and state, but the industry gap is far larger. In 2024, the national fatal work-injury rate was 3.3 per 100,000 full-time-equivalent (FTE) workers. By industry sector, agriculture/forestry/fishing/hunting sat at ~20.9 per 100k (6.3x the national rate) versus 1.8 for retail — and by occupation the spread is even wider: logging workers faced 110.4 deaths per 100k (33.5x national) and fishing/hunting workers 88.8. By state, 2024 rates ranged from Wyoming's 13.9 per 100k down to Rhode Island's 1.1 — a 12.6x spread, still large but well short of the occupation-level gap. The worst combinations are not evenly distributed: they cluster where a high-risk industry is geographically concentrated in a sparsely populated state — commercial fishing in Alaska, oil/gas and mining extraction in Wyoming and North Dakota, and logging in the Pacific Northwest/Appalachia — so the state-level and industry-level dangers compound rather than acting independently.

Data availability — a genuine corpus gap

The askamerica corpus (this connector) does not carry BLS's Census of Fatal Occupational Injuries (CFOI) — the actual federal source for workplace death counts and rates. search_catalog was queried three separate ways ('workplace death rates by industry state', 'occupational injury illness OSHA workplace safety', 'census of fatal occupational injuries CFOI') and every run returned unmatched_terms including "fatalities," "occupational," "injuries," "cfoi," and "osha" with no covering table. The closest tables present (transport.fatal_crashes_by_state_year, health.cdc_county_injury_mortality) cover traffic deaths and overdose/suicide/homicide mortality respectively — neither is workplace-fatality data and neither was used as a substitute. This is a real sourcing gap, not a phrasing problem: it has been logged as such below. The entire quantitative answer here was built from BLS's own primary CFOI releases, fetched directly, plus one secondary analysis (H&P Law) that reprocessed BLS's own 2021-2023 state file for a 3-year averaged ranking, used only for corroboration.

Which combinations are worst

State rates are driven almost entirely by which high-risk industries dominate that state's employment mix, so the worst outcomes are combinations, not a state effect or an industry effect alone:

  • Wyoming — oil/gas extraction and mining. Roughly 30% of the state's workplace deaths occur in mining and natural-resource extraction, the single largest driver of its #1 national ranking.
  • North Dakota — natural resources/mining. Nearly half of workplace deaths fall in this category, tied to Bakken-region oil and gas activity.
  • Alaska — commercial fishing. Fishing sustains the most fatalities of any Alaska industry; combined with fishing/hunting's own occupational rate of 88.8 per 100k nationally, Alaska's commercial fishing fleet is plausibly the single most dangerous industry-state combination in the country, consistent with this occupation's long-documented extreme risk.
  • Mississippi — construction and long-haul trucking. These two sectors are named as the major contributors to the state's #2 2024 ranking.
  • Logging is not tied to one dominant state in the sources reviewed, but its 110.4 national rate is the highest of any occupation, and it concentrates in timber regions (Pacific Northwest, Appalachia, Maine) — a combination not separately quantified at state grain in the sources fetched here.

The general pattern: sparsely populated, resource-extraction-heavy states amplify already-dangerous industries, because a small workforce concentrated in agriculture, fishing, logging, or oil/gas extraction pulls the state average far above states with large, diversified, office-heavy economies.

Caveats

  • State-by-industry cross-tabulated rates (the exact combination cells) were not directly fetchable — BLS's own bls.gov domain returned HTTP 403 on every direct fetch attempt in this session (news-release pages, the dedicated state-by-industry table, and the underlying .xlsx file all blocked automated fetches), a known Akamai-edge block on this domain. The figures above come from BLS's published numbers as relayed through BLS's own Economics Daily article and independent secondary analyses that processed BLS's public data, not from an independently re-run calculation against BLS's raw microdata.
  • 2023 and 2024 vintages are mixed above (2024 for industry/state rates, 2023 industry figures cited once for context) because 2024 is BLS's most current released CFOI year; where a figure is 2023 it is labeled as such.
  • The worst-combination narrative (Wyoming/mining, Alaska/fishing, etc.) is drawn from secondary reporting that cites BLS regional releases naming the top contributing industry per state, not a joint industry-by-state rate table — treat this section as directionally reliable but not a precise combined rate.
