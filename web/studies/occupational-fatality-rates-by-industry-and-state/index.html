Workplace death rates vary about 30x across industries and 13x across states — with resource-extraction, transportation, agriculture, and construction driving the worst state-industry combinations

BLS Census of Fatal Occupational Injuries (CFOI), 2024 — 5,070 fatal work injuries, national rate 3.3 per 100,000 full-time-equivalent (FTE) workers

Summary

Workplace death rates differ far more by industry than by state, though both gaps are large. Across the 13 major private-sector industries BLS tracks, the 2024 fatal-injury rate ranges from 0.7 per 100,000 FTE workers in educational/health services to 20.9 in agriculture, forestry, fishing and hunting — roughly a 30-fold spread. Across the 50 states plus DC, the rate ranges from 1.1 in Rhode Island to 13.9 in Wyoming — a 12.6-fold spread, versus a national average of 3.3. The worst combinations are not a mystery: they are states whose economies are unusually concentrated in the highest-risk industries — Wyoming and North Dakota (oil/gas and mining), Alaska (fishing, mining, aviation), Mississippi and Arkansas (trucking/transportation and agriculture) — while the safest states (Rhode Island, New Hampshire, New Jersey, Massachusetts) have service-heavy economies with little exposure to mining, logging, fishing, or long-haul trucking.

Which state-industry combinations are worst — and why

BLS does not publish a full state x industry fatal-injury-rate cross-tabulation (small cell counts in many state-industry combinations would fail its disclosure/reliability thresholds), so this cannot be answered with a single BLS table. Instead, the worst combinations can be identified by asking which states have both a high overall rate and a workforce concentrated in the highest-risk national industries — a coverage-based inference, not a directly-tabulated statistic:

  • Wyoming — mining/oil & gas. Roughly 30% of Wyoming's 2024 workplace deaths occurred in natural resources and mining, the industry with the second-highest national rate (13.8). This is the largest single driver of Wyoming's 13.9 state rate, nearly 4x the national average.
  • North Dakota — mining/oil & gas. Natural resources and mining accounted for close to half of all North Dakota workplace fatalities in 2024, pushing its state rate to 6.8.
  • Mississippi and Arkansas — trucking/transportation. Trucking is described as central to Mississippi's economy and is that state's leading cause of workplace death; combined with agriculture exposure in Arkansas, both states post rates more than double the national average (8.0 and 6.2).
  • Alaska — fishing, mining, aviation. Commercial fishing and small-aircraft transport are both nationally high-risk niches concentrated in Alaska, contributing to its 7.1 rate.
  • California, by contrast, illustrates the reverse pattern at industry-count level (directly fetched from BLS's California state supplement): construction (81 deaths) and transportation/warehousing (70) were California's top fatality-count industries in 2024, but California's overall state rate (2.4) sits below the national average because its economy is large and service-heavy overall, diluting the concentrated risk of its construction/transportation sectors.

The general mechanism: a state's overall fatality rate is closer to a workforce-weighted average of its industry mix's individual rates. States whose employment is disproportionately in agriculture, mining/oil & gas, transportation, or construction will show elevated overall rates even if none of their individual workplaces is unusually unsafe by industry-specific standards; states with large service-sector, finance, or information-industry employment (Northeast corridor states) will show low overall rates for the same reason.

Data access note and limitations

The AskAmerica connector's askengine schemas were searched first (search_catalog across all schemas for CFOI/OSHA/occupational-fatality data) and returned no matching table — this is a genuine coverage gap in the corpus, not a query-construction issue. The analysis therefore relies entirely on BLS's own published CFOI releases, fetched directly where bls.gov's live site did not 403 the request (its edge network blocks most non-browser fetches; the Wayback Machine mirror of the same BLS pages was used as a workaround, consistent with how this connector's own recipes handle dol.gov's identical block). The national industry-rate table and the California state supplement were read directly from BLS's own archived pages. The full 51-state ranking table was not retrievable as a raw BLS table through this session's tools (BLS's per-state PDF/XLSX supplements are also blocked); it was instead read from Visual Capitalist's reproduction of BLS's 2024 CFOI data, which was cross-checked and matches the directly-fetched BLS national and California figures exactly (13.9/1.1/3.3/2.4). No state x industry cross-tab exists publicly at fine grain, so the "worst combinations" section is a reasoned inference from each state's dominant industry share of its own fatalities (as reported directly by BLS in state news releases), not a single queried statistic.
