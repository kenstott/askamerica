High-uninsured US counties are poorer, smaller, and concentrated in non-Medicaid-expansion Texas - but they are not simply the highest-mortality counties

Census SAHIE 2023 (county) vs CDC/NCHS mortality 2017 (state) - askamerica

Summary

The 100 US counties with the highest uninsured rates (2023 SAHIE, ranging 20.6%–33.5%) are overwhelmingly small, rural, lower-income, and concentrated in one state that never expanded Medicaid: 90 of the 100 are in Texas. The 100 lowest-uninsured counties (2.1%–4.5%) cluster in Massachusetts, Minnesota, Iowa, Michigan and the Upper Midwest/Northeast — states with either near-universal state coverage mandates (Massachusetts, pre-dating the ACA) or full Medicaid expansion. The high group averages 38% lower median household income ($60,225 vs $96,890) and is smaller/more rural (median county population ~12,000 vs ~94,000). At the only grain where uninsured rate and an age-adjusted mortality rate can both be measured here — state, 2017 — the raw correlation is weak (r=0.28, n=51) and NOT monotonic across deciles (trend p=0.078, dips at deciles 5 and 9); once median household income is controlled for, the partial correlation collapses to -0.05 (p=0.74). That collapse, plus the state-vs-county grain mismatch discussed below, is why this is a textbook ecological-fallacy case: county-level uninsured/mortality association here is largely explainable by income, not a demonstrated individual-level causal chain from being uninsured to dying.

1. The extremes, with actual rates

Highest uninsured rate (2023, county grain, n=3,144 counties with a non-null SAHIE estimate): Gaines County, TX 33.5%, Holmes County, OH 31.5%, Lagrange County, IN 31.3%, Presidio County, TX 29.6%, down to the 100th-ranked county, Wood County, TX, at 20.6%. Lowest: Norfolk County, MA 2.1%, Hampshire County, MA 2.4%, Middlesex County, MA 2.4%, Plymouth County, MA 2.7%, up to the 100th-ranked county, Union County, OH, at 4.5%. (Kalawao County, HI, the smallest county in the US at roughly 90 residents, has a suppressed/null SAHIE estimate and was excluded rather than treated as a true extreme.)

2. How the two groups differ

Population: the high-uninsured group is smaller and far more skewed — mean 104,361 but median only 11,977 (a few large metro counties like Harris/Dallas/Cameron TX pull the mean up); the low-uninsured group has mean 195,388, median 93,987. Income: mean median-household-income is $60,225 in the high group vs $96,890 in the low group, a 38% gap; median MHI $59,830 vs $93,528. State distribution: 90 of the top-100 uninsured counties are in Texas, with the rest scattered (FL 3, AK 2, IN, GA, ID, OH, OK 1 each). The bottom-100 is spread across MA (13), MN (12), IA (11), MI (10), NY (9), WI (9), plus RI, VT, MD, CO, IL, CA, VA, HI, PA. Geographic clustering: the high-uninsured extreme clusters almost entirely along the Texas–Mexico border and Texas Panhandle/South Plains (Webb, Hidalgo, Cameron, Starr, Maverick, the Panhandle wheat/cattle counties), consistent with prior research (Texas 2036) identifying the Rio Grande Valley, West Texas and Panhandle as the state's uninsured epicenters. This is not simply a rural-income story: Texas is one of the ten states that has not adopted ACA Medicaid expansion, which mechanically removes a coverage pathway available to low-income adults in expansion states. The low-uninsured extreme clusters in the Northeast/Upper Midwest, led by Massachusetts (its 2006 near-universal coverage law, the ACA's model, predates and exceeds ACA-driven gains elsewhere).

3. Decile analysis: is the uninsured-mortality relationship monotonic?

Because SAHIE has no direct mortality companion at county grain in this corpus, deciles were built at state grain (n=51 states+DC) for the one year (2017) where CDC/NCHS still published a state-level age-adjusted all-cause rate. Binning states into decile groups by 2017 uninsured rate and averaging each decile's age-adjusted mortality rate: bin 1 (mean 4.5% uninsured) averages 671 deaths/100k; the rate rises through the middle deciles to a peak of 826/100k at decile 8 (mean 11.2% uninsured), but bin 5 (8.5% uninsured) dips back to 672 — matching bin 1 — and bin 9 (13.0% uninsured) falls back to 754, below bin 8. The relationship is not monotonic (formal trend test p=0.078, monotonic=false) and it does not simply weaken or reverse at one tail — it is noisy throughout, with the worst-uninsured decile (16.1% mean) landing mid-pack at 794/100k, well below West Virginia (7.5% uninsured, 957/100k) or Kentucky (6.4% uninsured, 929.9/100k) individually. States with historically high mortality (KY, WV, MS, AR, AL, OK) are Appalachian/Deep South states with comparatively moderate uninsured rates but severe chronic-disease and overdose burdens; Texas, the single highest-uninsured state (19.4%), has middling mortality (735.7/100k) because its large young/working-age population is not the demographic driving age-adjusted mortality risk. The zero-order correlation (r=0.28) is weak to begin with; the fact that it fully collapses once median household income is controlled for (partial r=-0.05, p=0.74, see Section 4) confirms the raw pattern is not being carried by uninsurance itself.

4. The ecological fallacy, and how the grain mismatch was handled

What the county/state association does and does not show. Even a clean state-level or county-level correlation between uninsured rate and mortality is a claim about places, not people. A high-uninsured county's excess deaths could come disproportionately from the insured majority (via crowded emergency departments, understaffed rural hospitals that close because of high uncompensated-care burdens, or correlated risk factors like poverty and occupational hazard exposure that affect insured and uninsured residents alike) rather than from uninsured individuals dying at elevated rates themselves. Attributing an ecological (county-level) association to individual uninsured people requires individual-level linked data: e.g., death certificates or vital-statistics microdata linked to insurance-status records at time of death (or near it), of the kind used in the Oregon Health Insurance Experiment (randomized Medicaid lottery, individual-level mortality/hospitalization follow-up) or in NBER's 'Saved by Medicaid' study (universe of low-income adults, administrative linkage of enrollment to mortality records). Absent that individual linkage, this corpus's finding is properly read as: 'higher-uninsured places are not clearly higher-mortality places once income is accounted for' — a materially weaker and more defensible claim than 'being uninsured raises an individual's risk of dying,' which this data cannot test.

How the grain mismatch was handled. census.sahie_insurance carries uninsured rates for all roughly 3,144 US counties for 2023 (most recent year); health.cdc_mortality in this corpus carries an age-adjusted all-cause mortality rate only at the state level, and only through 2017 (2018-2023 mortality rows in this table drop the age_adjusted_rate field entirely, carrying only weekly COVID-era counts instead). Two mismatches therefore had to be resolved, not silently absorbed: (a) grain — rather than aggregating county uninsured rates up to a population-weighted state figure and calling that 'county-level,' the state-level SAHIE rows (a directly-published state estimate, not a derived aggregate) were joined to state mortality, and the analysis is reported at state grain throughout Section 3, explicitly flagged as coarser than the county-level extremes in Sections 1-2; and (b) vintage — rather than pairing 2023 uninsured rates with a stale 2017 mortality rate under a false appearance of currency, both series were pulled from the same year (2017) so the comparison is at least contemporaneous, at the cost of not reflecting post-2017 uninsured-rate levels. Both choices trade an inflated appearance of precision for an honest, matched comparison; a reader wanting a true county-grain mortality comparison would need CDC WONDER's restricted-use county mortality files, which are outside this corpus.
