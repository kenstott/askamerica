Big Populations, Then Medicaid Policy: What America's Most-Uninsured Counties Share

Census Bureau SAHIE 2023 (population under 65) — top 30 US counties by number of uninsured residents

Summary

The counties with the most uninsured people are, overwhelmingly, just America's biggest counties — Harris (Houston), Los Angeles, Dallas, Maricopa (Phoenix), Cook (Chicago), Tarrant (Fort Worth). Across all 3,143 US counties, county population explains most of the variation in uninsured count (r=0.90) but almost none of the variation in the uninsured rate (r=-0.05) — size drives the raw number, not the risk. The second, more diagnostic thing the top-30 counties share: 17 of the 30 (57%, holding 4.2 million of the combined 7.3 million uninsured) sit in states that had not adopted full ACA Medicaid expansion as of 2023 — Texas, Florida, and Georgia. Those 17 counties average a 16.7% uninsured rate versus 9.0% for the 13 top-30 counties in Medicaid-expansion states (California, Illinois, Arizona, Nevada, New York, North Carolina, Washington, Ohio) — despite being similarly large. So the shared traits are: (1) sheer population size, which mechanically produces a big raw uninsured count in any state, and (2) for roughly half of these counties, residence in a non-expansion state, which roughly doubles the local uninsured rate on top of that population effect.

Step 1 — What the top-30 list actually looks like

Ranking all US counties by number of uninsured residents (Census Bureau SAHIE, 2023, population under 65) surfaces almost exactly the list of the nation's most populous counties: Harris TX (922,000 uninsured), Los Angeles CA (703,000), Dallas TX (521,000), Maricopa AZ (447,000), Cook IL (372,000), Tarrant TX (331,000), Miami-Dade FL (321,000), Bexar TX (301,000), Clark NV (260,000), Hidalgo TX (212,000), Broward FL (204,000), Orange CA (182,000), San Diego CA (182,000), Riverside CA (180,000), Palm Beach FL (174,000), and so on through Denton TX (105,000) at rank 30. Every one of these is among the 30-40 most populous counties in the country. This matches independent reporting: Texas 2036 and ValuePenguin both report Harris, Dallas, Tarrant, and Bexar counties each with 300,000+ uninsured residents, consistent with the figures here.

Step 2 — Population size drives the count, not the rate

Querying census.sahie_insurance for all 3,144 US counties in 2023 and correlating uninsured count and uninsured rate against total population (insured+uninsured) gives:

  • corr(uninsured count, population) = 0.90 — county size is the dominant driver of the raw uninsured number.
  • corr(uninsured rate, population) = -0.05 — essentially zero. Big counties are not meaningfully more or less likely, per capita, to have uninsured residents than small ones.

This is the first and most basic thing the most-uninsured counties have in common: they show up on this list mechanically, because they are large, the same way New York and Los Angeles top most "most X" lists for any common condition. A ranking by raw count is largely a population ranking in disguise.

Step 3 — The second commonality: Medicaid non-expansion states are overrepresented and drive higher rates

Splitting the top-30 counties by whether their state had adopted full ACA Medicaid expansion as of 2023: 17 of the 30 counties (57%) are in Texas, Florida, or Georgia — the three most populous non-expansion states — while 13 are in Medicaid-expansion states (California, Illinois, Arizona, Nevada, New York, North Carolina, Washington, Ohio). The non-expansion group of top-30 counties totals 4.20 million uninsured against 3.12 million for the expansion-state group, despite the two groups having similar county sizes on average. The average uninsured RATE among the non-expansion counties is 16.7%, versus 9.0% for expansion-state counties — nearly double. Individual examples: Hidalgo County TX sits at 27.5% uninsured versus San Diego County CA at 6.8%, though both are large counties. This lines up with the broader literature: SHADAC's 2024 ACS county-uninsured tables and the Census Bureau's own 2026 SAHIE release note the South has the largest share of high-uninsured (>15%) counties, and Texas 2036's analysis of Texas specifically attributes much of the state's uninsured burden to non-expansion of Medicaid eligibility up to 138% of the poverty line.

Step 4 — What this does and doesn't establish

This is a descriptive comparison of 30 counties, not a causal estimate — it does not control for each county's poverty rate, share of undocumented immigrants (a population categorically ineligible for Medicaid/ACA subsidies and concentrated in border counties like Hidalgo and El Paso), employer-coverage rates, or age structure, all of which plausibly matter alongside expansion status. Rural/border Texas counties such as Hidalgo and El Paso post the highest rates in the sample (23-28%) and combine non-expansion status with a large noncitizen population, so the expansion effect likely overstates what a policy-only comparison would show. The literature (Texas 2036, ValuePenguin, UCLA Center for Health Policy Research) corroborates the same broad pattern — large urban counties carry the biggest raw numbers, while non-expansion-state and border/rural counties carry the highest rates — without claiming expansion status alone explains the full gap.
