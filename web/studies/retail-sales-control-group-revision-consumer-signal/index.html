The retail-sales control group revises about 0.1 percentage point on average — small, unbiased, and rarely enough to flip the consumer-strength story

Census MARTS Table 3 revision statistics (release of Aug 14, 2026); BEA retail control method

Summary

The retail-sales "control group" — total retail & food services sales excluding motor vehicle & parts dealers, gasoline stations, and building materials — is revised by an average of about 0.1 percentage point between its first (advance) reading and the final estimate, with a median revision of 0.0 (no systematic up- or down-bias), per Census Bureau MARTS Table 3 (12-month trailing statistics, release of Aug 14, 2026). That is on par with, or slightly smaller than, the headline retail total's own revision (Census states the long-run average absolute revision to the advance total is "two-tenths of one percent"). Among the 13 major categories, the control group's revision is one of the smallest — well below noisy categories like miscellaneous store retailers (0.9pp), sporting goods (0.6pp), furniture and electronics (0.5pp each), and gasoline stations (0.4pp, and the only line with a persistent negative bias of -0.3pp).

Does it matter for reading consumer strength? Mostly no, for the purpose it's built for. A ~0.1pp average revision is small relative to typical monthly control-group moves (which have ranged roughly 0.2-0.9pp over the past several months) and is not systematically biased, so it essentially never reverses a multi-month trend read — which is how the Fed, BEA and most analysts actually use the series (a 3-month or smoothed growth rate feeding directly into GDP's personal consumption expenditures estimate via BEA's "retail control method"). Where it CAN matter: (1) in a single month whose initial reading is very close to zero, a 0.1-0.2pp revision can flip the sign of that one month's headline; (2) the control group's revision is one input among several the BEA nets against other, more heavily revised inputs (autos from manufacturer data, gasoline from DOE data) when building PCE, so at the aggregate GDP level, revision noise from other components can swamp the control group's own small revision. But taken on its own terms, the control group's low, unbiased revision profile is exactly why economists prefer it to the noisier headline components — it is the more "trustworthy" first read of the bunch, not the least.

What the control group is, and why it exists

Census's Advance Monthly Retail Trade Survey (MARTS) does not itself publish an official "control group" line — the term describes total retail & food services sales excluding motor vehicle & parts dealers (NAICS 441), gasoline stations (447), and building materials & garden equipment dealers (444). The Richmond Fed calls this "core retail sales." The exclusions exist because the Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA) uses better source data for those three categories when building GDP's personal consumption expenditures (PCE) estimate: auto sales come from manufacturer/industry data rather than a dealer sample; gasoline consumption comes from Department of Energy data (a large share of gas sales happens outside gas stations, e.g., at grocery and convenience stores, which the Census gas-station sample misses); and building materials purchases (lumber, roofing, HVAC) are mostly counted as residential investment, not consumption, in the national accounts. Food services (NAICS 722) is tracked separately in the Census release architecture even though it IS part of BEA's PCE control group, so "core retail sales" as commonly quoted also nets that category out, leaving a goods-only measure.

The resulting series is what BEA calls the "retail control" group, and it is the direct indicator series used to extrapolate the "PCE control group" — most of PCE goods spending — inside quarterly and monthly GDP. Per BEA's NIPA Handbook (Chapter 5, Personal Consumption Expenditures), PCE goods estimates within the control group are prepared by allocating Census retail-control growth rates down to the detailed PCE category level using product-line weights from the Economic Census, then summing back up; any gap between the summed detailed estimate and the Census retail-control growth rate itself is distributed proportionately across the detailed categories so the aggregate ties out exactly to the Census-based control total. This is why the accuracy of the Census control-group number matters directly for GDP: it is not just referenced by BEA, it is the binding growth-rate constraint on a large slice of PCE.

Two things stand out. First, the control group's median revision is exactly zero — the advance reading is not systematically too high or too low, unlike gasoline stations (median -0.3pp, meaning the advance estimate tends to overstate gas-station month-over-month growth before later revision pulls it down) or furniture (-0.3pp). Second, at 0.1pp average absolute revision, the control group sits at the low end of the distribution — smaller than 9 of the other 13 components shown, and tied for the smallest alongside the headline total, the ex-auto and ex-gas sub-totals, motor vehicle dealers, and general merchandise. This is consistent with Census's own general statement (on its methodology page) that improvements to the estimation procedure over time have brought the average absolute revision to the advance estimate down to "two-tenths of one percent" for the series as a whole — the control group's current 12-month reading of 0.1pp is at or slightly better than that long-run norm.

Does the revision matter for reading consumer strength

Context for scale: in the same release, the control group's month-over-month adjusted % changes ran -0.2% (July 2026 advance, from June), +0.4% (June 2026 preliminary, from May), and it was up 5.5% over May-July 2026 vs a year earlier. A typical single-month move is on the order of 0.2-0.9 percentage points. An average revision of 0.1pp is therefore roughly 15-50% the size of a typical monthly signal — not negligible at the single-month level, but small next to the multi-month and year-over-year growth rates economists actually use to judge consumer strength (the 3-month-average or trend read is far more robust to a revision of this size than a single print is).

Concretely, this means: (1) A single month's control-group print that lands within about ±0.1-0.2pp of zero should be read as statistically and practically indistinguishable from flat — later revision has a real chance of flipping its sign, and Census's own confidence-interval flags on recent releases (marked with an asterisk when the 90% interval includes zero) reflect exactly this. (2) A clear, larger move (say, ±0.5pp or more) is very unlikely to be revised away, since the average revision is a fifth to a tenth of that size. (3) Because the control group's revision is unbiased (median 0.0) and smaller than most other retail categories, it does NOT introduce a systematic distortion into GDP's PCE-goods estimate the way, say, gasoline-station data would if it were used directly — this is part of the rationale for using DOE/manufacturer data instead of Census gas/auto samples in BEA's retail control method, and for why forecasters lean on the control group specifically rather than the headline. (4) The bigger revision risk to a GDP-based "consumer strength" read comes less from the control group's own noise and more from other line items entering PCE (services spending, auto units, gasoline volumes) and from the quarterly benchmarking process (Annual Retail Trade Survey benchmark revisions, and the Sept 28, 2026 revised-estimate release referenced in the current MARTS report) — those can move the picture by more than the control group's own ~0.1pp monthly noise.

Bottom line: the revision is real but modest and unbiased. It rarely changes the qualitative story about consumer demand over any window longer than a single month, which is exactly why the control group — designed to strip out the categories most prone to measurement noise or definitional mismatch with PCE — is treated as the more reliable early read, not a less reliable one.
