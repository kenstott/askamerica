## Does defense-appropriations timing (or stalling into a CR) show up in how defense-contractor-heavy stocks trade in the following weeks?

**Short answer: yes, but not as a simple "bill passes → stocks rise" rule.** The effect is real and mechanistically well-grounded (CRs concretely restrict what DOD can spend on, and full-year appropriations remove that restriction), but the sign and size of the market reaction depend on (1) whether the *shutdown/CR actually covers DOD itself* versus only the rest of government, (2) which defense subsector you're looking at (large diversified primes vs. smaller/cash-constrained subcontractors and staffing-heavy government-services firms), and (3) how much uncertainty is already priced in going into the event. This is a literature-and-reporting-based answer, not a computed one — see the data-limitation note below.

### The mechanism is real, not speculative
DOD has started the fiscal year under a continuing resolution in roughly 37 of the last 49 years, and GAO's own analysis (GAO-26-107065, "Defense Budget: Effects of Continuing Resolutions") documents that CRs mechanically bar new program starts, cap procurement quantities at the prior year's level, and force programs to be executed in a compressed window once full-year funding finally lands — a real operational/cash-flow effect, not just sentiment. That gives a concrete channel through which appropriations timing should show up in contractor revenue expectations and therefore in prices.

### What the finance literature actually finds
- The theoretical backbone is Pástor & Veronesi's "Uncertainty about Government Policy and Stock Prices" (NBER w16128): stock prices tend to fall as policy uncertainty *builds*, and rally when it *resolves* — even if the resolution itself is unfavorable (e.g., automatic cuts). That pattern shows up directly in defense stocks: contemporaneous coverage of the 2013 sequester noted defense-company stocks "soared" once the automatic across-the-board cuts actually took effect, because the multi-month uncertainty about *whether and how much* was worse for pricing than the cuts themselves ([The Hill, 2013](https://thehill.com/policy/defense/146456-defense-company-stocks-have-soared-since-sequesters-ax-fell/)).
- Dedicated event-study work exists on exactly this question. A Longwood University thesis ("Government Shutdown: A Test of Market Efficiency," digitalcommons.longwood.edu) and a Utah State thesis on government-contractor stock performance both run daily event-study/multifactor asset-pricing designs around shutdown announcements; a new 2026 ScienceDirect paper, "Do the Markets Care about the 2025 U.S. Government Shutdown?" (S1059056026006416), extends this to the most recent and longest shutdown. The consistent theme across these studies is that the reaction is *shutdown-specific*, not uniform: contemporaneous reporting on that same variation notes that during the full 2013 shutdown, defense stocks lagged the market (contracts and payments were actually paused), while during the 2018–19 shutdown defense stocks outperformed — because DOD had already been funded on time for FY2019 (signed Sept 28, 2018) and the shutdown that winter hit *other* agencies, not defense. That is the cleanest evidence that markets are pricing the *DOD-specific* funding status, not shutdown headlines in general.
- The most recent live test is the record 43-day shutdown of Oct–Nov 2025. Contemporaneous market-data coverage found that on the shutdown's first trading day, traditional hardware primes were essentially flat (Lockheed Martin −0.04%, Raytheon/RTX +0.04% — "remarkable stability"), while government-services and staffing-heavy contractors *rose* sharply the same day (CACI +3.3%, Booz Allen +2.7%, SAIC +1.8%, Leidos +1.4%). That divergence is itself informative: "defense-contractor-heavy" is not one trade — hardware primes with multi-year backlogs are largely insulated from a short funding gap, while services firms' stock reaction seemed to reflect relief/positioning rather than fear, since their revenue is billed against existing task orders regardless of a brief lapse. As the shutdown dragged past a month, Defense One's November 2025 reporting shows the picture splitting further: large primes (Leidos, Leonardo DRS, General Dynamics, RTX) posted strong Q3 earnings and kept guiding up, while GD's own CEO explicitly warned of downside "if the shutdown... stretches into the coming months," and B-21 bomber production-acceleration talks were reported paused during the lapse — i.e., the market-visible effect was concentrated in *forward guidance and contract-timing commentary* more than in the stock price itself over those weeks ([Defense One, Nov 7 2025](https://www.defenseone.com/business/2025/11/defense-tech-companies-will-weather-shutdown-what-happens-next/409390/)).
- A second, separate driver worth distinguishing from appropriations timing per se: actual conflict/war news. Reuters/Defense News reporting from April 2026 shows the NYSE Arca Defense index fell ~8% in March 2026 even as the Iran war continued, because "conflict premium" had already been priced in beforehand and the sector (32x forward earnings vs. ~20x for the S&P 500) was richly valued — and the same piece notes the sector's forward path was explicitly tied to *upcoming budget decisions* (a planned April 21, 2026 disclosure of FY2027 defense-budget details), underscoring that markets do treat scheduled appropriations/budget news as a distinct, forward-looking catalyst separate from combat headlines ([Defense News/Reuters, Apr 2 2026](https://www.defensenews.com/news/your-military/2026/04/02/us-defense-stocks-see-no-iran-war-lift-after-early-surge/)).

### Bottom line
Appropriations timing is a real, mechanically-grounded input to how defense-contractor stocks trade in the following weeks, and the finance literature's general policy-uncertainty framework (build-up → downward drag/volatility, resolution → relief rally, even on unfavorable terms) fits the defense-specific evidence reasonably well across the 2013, 2018–19, and 2025 episodes. But three qualifications matter for anyone using this as a trading or policy-analysis heuristic: (1) the effect is really about whether *DOD itself* is funded, not government-wide shutdown headlines generically; (2) large diversified hardware primes with multi-year backlogs show far less near-term price sensitivity than smaller, cash-flow-constrained subcontractors and than government-services/staffing firms, whose reaction can even run the *opposite* direction; and (3) how much uncertainty is already priced into valuations going in can swamp the appropriations-timing signal, as the 2026 Iran-war episode shows for a related but distinct catalyst.
