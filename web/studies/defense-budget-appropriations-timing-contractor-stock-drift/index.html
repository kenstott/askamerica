No statistically reliable signal: defense-heavy stocks show mixed, noisy moves in the weeks after DoD appropriations pass or CRs stall, not a consistent pattern

Event study, 11 fiscal-year funding events (FY2016-FY2026), iShares Aerospace & Defense ETF (ITA) vs S&P 500 (SPY), SEC/Stooq daily prices

Summary

No. Across the 11 fiscal-year defense-funding events from FY2016 through FY2026 (every year Congress either passed a Department of Defense appropriations act on time or let funding lapse into a continuing resolution, CR), the defense-heavy sector — measured with the iShares U.S. Aerospace & Defense ETF (ITA) against the S&P 500 (SPY) — showed no reliable, repeatable pattern in the four trading weeks following enactment. The excess return (ITA return minus SPY return) ranged from -5.1% to +8.2% and flipped sign in both directions regardless of whether funding was on-time, mildly delayed, or stalled for over six months. The average excess return across all 11 events was a statistically insignificant +0.98% (one-sample t-test run via the hypothesis_test tool: t=0.83, p=0.42), and the correlation between how many days late enactment was and the size of the post-enactment excess return was weak and not significant (r=0.50, p=0.118, n=11). Independent industry commentary corroborates this: reporting on shutdown-era defense-stock performance since 1995 finds the sector sometimes beat the market during a shutdown/CR stretch (5.2% vs 3% for the S&P) and sometimes lagged it (2013), consistent with no dependable directional signal.

Method

For each fiscal year 2016-2026, I identified the date the Department of Defense's appropriations act (or, for FY2025, the full-year continuing resolution that set DoD funding) was signed into law, and classified the year as on-time (enacted by Oct 1, the fiscal-year start) or delayed via CR (enacted after Oct 1, having operated under one or more continuing resolutions in the interim). FY2019 is the sole on-time year in the window — DoD appropriations were signed Sept 28, 2018, three days before FY2019 began, a genuinely rare event in this period. Every other year in the sample ran under a CR for anywhere from about 11 weeks (FY2020, FY2021, FY2023) to over seven months (FY2017: enacted May 5, 2017, 216 days into the fiscal year).

For each event I pulled ITA and SPY adjusted close prices from sec.stock_prices for the trading day at or immediately before enactment, and the trading day at or immediately before a point roughly 20 trading days (about 4 calendar weeks) later, and computed each ticker's simple return over that window. The reported 'excess return' is ITA's return minus SPY's return over the identical window — a simple market-adjusted (not beta-adjusted) event-study return, chosen because the small sample here does not support estimating a stable market beta for ITA per sub-period. Significance was tested with the hypothesis_test tool (one-sample t-test against zero) rather than by hand; with only one on-time observation (FY2019), a formal two-group significance test between on-time and delayed categories was not run — a single observation cannot support one.

Why the effect is not detectable here (and what would be needed to test it properly)

Three structural reasons this design cannot isolate a clean appropriations-timing effect, beyond the small sample: (1) Confounding macro news. Every ~4-week post-enactment window in this sample also contains unrelated market-moving events — e.g. FY2019's window captured the December 2018 market selloff; FY2026's window captured a rally driven by AI/tech and unrelated fiscal news; FY2025's window included the April 2025 tariff-driven selloff and snapback. None of the windows here are 'clean' of other news, and with only 11 non-overlapping events there is no way to average that noise away. (2) The appropriations act itself is rarely a surprise. Markets can price in expected enactment ahead of the actual signing — defense contractors' revenue guidance, backlog, and Congressional vote-counting are public well before a bill is signed, so an event study anchored on the signature date may be looking well after the information was already priced in. (3) An enactment during a CR is not "stalled" from the market's perspective — CRs typically continue prior-year funding levels for existing programs (new starts are what's frozen), so a CR does not necessarily mean zero revenue to incumbent contractors, which weakens the mechanical link between 'CR duration' and 'contractor stock stress' that the question's framing implies.

A more rigorous test would need: (a) many more independent events — e.g. individual continuing resolutions and full-year appropriations bills going back further, or a cross-sectional panel of many defense-heavy tickers rather than one ETF, to raise statistical power; (b) an event window anchored on the House/Senate passage vote rather than the President's signature, since passage is often the more decision-relevant date; (c) controlling for broader market beta and for concurrent macro news (Fed announcements, wars, tariffs) via a proper market-model abnormal-return regression rather than a simple SPY-relative return; and (d) separating the CR-stall period itself (return during the delay) from the post-passage period (return after resolution), since this analysis only tested the latter.

What the data supports as a defensible claim

Given the above, the defensible claim is narrower than the question as posed: in this event-study design, defense-sector stocks (ITA vs SPY) show no statistically significant, consistently-signed excess-return pattern in the month following DoD appropriations enactment, whether that enactment was on-time or badly delayed by continuing resolutions. This does not rule out a genuine effect too small or too diffuse for an 11-event univariate test to detect — it says this specific, tractable version of the question returns no signal, and that industry commentary on shutdown-era defense-stock performance independently reports the same inconsistency (beating the market in some shutdown/CR episodes, lagging in others).
