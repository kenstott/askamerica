Once You Normalize for Mileage, Energy Transfer/Sunoco Still Comes Out Among the Worst — But No Complete National Per-Mile Ranking Exists Publicly

PHMSA is the sole authoritative source for both incident counts and operator mileage; this session could not query PHMSA directly

Summary

PHMSA (the DOT Pipeline and Hazardous Materials Safety Administration) is the only body that holds both the incident data and the operator-by-operator pipeline mileage needed to compute a true, current, national "incidents per mile by operator" ranking. That merge does not exist as a single published table anywhere — not in this connector's data corpus (which carries no PHMSA pipeline table at all) and not as a ready-made PHMSA download I could fetch directly in this session (phmsa.dot.gov returned HTTP 403 to every automated fetch attempt). What follows is therefore built from analyses that independent parties (Pipeline Safety Trust, Reuters, Greenpeace/Waterkeeper) have already run directly against PHMSA's own incident and annual-mileage files, cross-checked against each other.

Bottom line: ranked by raw incident count, Sunoco/Energy Transfer, Enterprise Products Partners, and Plains All American are consistently the worst hazardous-liquids operators — but that ranking mostly just reflects that they run some of the largest networks. Once mileage is accounted for, the picture partially but not fully re-shuffles: in the one clean, sourced per-mile comparison available (Ohio, 2010-2021), Energy Transfer's liquids operations still ran at roughly 99.7 incidents per 1,000 miles — about 3.6x the average of other liquids operators in the state (28.0) — meaning its size does not explain away its bad record. A separate national three-year study found Sunoco ranks "8th-worst for volume spilled per mile," i.e. worse than most but not literally the single worst once normalized, even though it is the single worst by raw incident count. So the honest answer is: Energy Transfer/Sunoco and, to a lesser extent, Enterprise Products and Plains All American remain in the worst tier on a per-mile basis, not just a raw-count basis — but no one has publicly built the full national operator-by-operator per-mile league table this question is really asking for, and PHMSA's own site could not be reached in this session to build it fresh.

What "accounting for mileage" actually changes

Raw incident counts are dominated by network size. A 2016 Reuters analysis of PHMSA hazardous-liquids data found Sunoco Logistics (Energy Transfer's liquids arm) had the most crude-oil leaks of any operator since 2010 (203), ahead of Enterprise Products Partners (190) and Plains All American (167) — but Sunoco, Enterprise and Plains also operate some of the largest liquids networks in the country, so a naive count overstates how much worse they really are relative to smaller operators.

Two sourced pieces of evidence show what happens when mileage is actually divided in:

  • It partially, but does not fully, explain away the gap. A separate national study (cited via a Waterkeeper/press summary of PHMSA-derived analysis) found Sunoco ranks 8th-worst nationally for volume spilled per mile over a recent three-year window on hazardous-liquids lines — worse than roughly 90%+ of operators, but not literally #1 the way it is on raw incident count. Separately, that same analysis found Sunoco/Energy Transfer had more accidents harming people or the environment than any other operator over five years (38), even accounting for network size.
  • Where a full per-mile breakdown by operator exists (state level), Energy Transfer stands out sharply. Pipeline Safety Trust's Ohio "State of Safety" analysis (2010-2021) computed incidents per 1,000 miles by parent operator and system type. Energy Transfer's hazardous-liquids operations in Ohio ran at 99.69 incidents per 1,000 miles, against an average of 27.99 for other liquids operators in the state — roughly 3.6x worse, and well above the next-worst named operator, Buckeye (22.86). On the gas-transmission side, Kinder Morgan (5.71/1,000 mi) and Questar (5.90/1,000 mi) ran moderately above TransCanada (5.14/1,000 mi), though PST's own writeup flags that Ohio's "other" operator averages are noisy because they are dominated by companies with only a handful of miles of pipe (one incident on 9.8 miles of AK Steel pipe alone produced a 102-per-1,000-mile rate).

National-scale context on ETP/Sunoco specifically

Greenpeace's "Oil and Water" report, built directly from the PHMSA hazardous-liquids incident database, found that Energy Transfer Partners, Sunoco and their subsidiaries/joint ventures reported 527 of the 6,102 total U.S. hazardous-liquids incidents from 2002 through 2017 (about 8.6% of the national total over that span) — an incident roughly every 11 days from that one corporate family, releasing 87,273 barrels and causing an estimated $115 million in property damage. ETP's liquids-plus-gas network is described in the same report as roughly 71,000 miles nationally, among the largest of any operator, which is consistent with both a large raw share of incidents AND a genuinely elevated per-mile rate — the Ohio state data above is the clearest place those two explanations can be told apart, and there Energy Transfer's rate is high even relative to peers of comparable scale.

A confound that any single-operator ranking should control for and mostly doesn't: pipe age

Pipeline Safety Trust's own decade analysis (built on PHMSA's Gas Transmission mileage-by-decade data) found onshore gas-transmission significant-incident rates on pipe installed in the 2010s ran roughly 2.7x the rate of pipe installed in the 2000s (about 1.6 vs. 0.6 significant incidents per 1,000 miles for pipe-and-valve-site failures), and pipe of unknown installation age failed at nearly 3x the rate of the worst known-age cohort. A similar "burn-in" pattern shows up in hazardous-liquids pipe. This matters for an operator ranking because two operators with identical per-mile incident rates could differ enormously in how much of their pipe is old, unknown-age, or newly built — a factor that is not controlled for in any of the operator-level figures above, including the Ohio comparison. A truly rigorous "worst operator, adjusted for mileage" ranking would also need to adjust for each operator's pipe-age mix, which none of the available public analyses do at the operator level.

Data-access note (read before trusting any number above at face value)

This connector's data catalog (schemas: sec, geo, econ, census, crime, weather, fec, ref, fedregister, officials, cyber_vuln, cyber_threat, energy, health, edu, patents, lands, disasters, housing, cftc, ag, transport, environment, fiscal) was searched directly (search_catalog, list_tables on the transport and energy schemas) and carries no PHMSA pipeline incident or pipeline-mileage table at all — this is a genuine, confirmed coverage gap, not a query I failed to write correctly. I then attempted to fetch PHMSA's own annual-report and incident data pages and ZIP downloads directly (phmsa.dot.gov, several distinct URLs including the Related Downloads links, the National Pipeline Performance Measures page, and a Wayback Machine mirror) and every direct phmsa.dot.gov fetch returned HTTP 403 in this session. Every operator-level number in this report is therefore a figure I read from a secondary or tertiary source that itself analyzed PHMSA's raw data (Pipeline Safety Trust, Reuters, Greenpeace, a Waterkeeper-sourced summary), not a number I computed myself from PHMSA's source files. Where a source's own methodology or caveats are relevant (e.g., PST's note about noisy low-mileage "other" averages), I've included them inline rather than presenting the numbers as clean.

What This Report Does Not Answer

  • Produce a complete, current, national operator-by-operator per-mile ranking: No such ranking is publicly published anywhere, and this session could not build one from scratch: the askamerica corpus has no PHMSA pipeline table, and phmsa.dot.gov returned HTTP 403 on every direct fetch attempt for the raw incident and mileage files needed to compute it. This is disclosed explicitly rather than papered over with a fabricated or over-generalized ranking.
