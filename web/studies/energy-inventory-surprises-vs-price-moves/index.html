EIA weekly inventory reports move crude oil prices sharply for about 20-30 minutes, then the impact mostly holds rather than reversing

Literature review of intraday event studies plus an AskAmerica daily-close check, WTI crude, 2023-2025

Summary

The EIA's Weekly Petroleum Status Report, released every Wednesday at 10:30am ET, is the single most-watched scheduled event in the oil market. Peer-reviewed intraday event studies find a genuine but short and asymmetric price reaction: the immediate spike from a meaningful surprise runs roughly 30 cents to $1/bbl on WTI (a bit under 1% at recent prices), with volume and volatility burning out within about 20-30 minutes of the release. Prices react to the surprise relative to the analyst consensus, not the raw build/draw number -- a print that matches consensus moves almost nothing. Once that intraday window closes, the move is largely permanent rather than reversing: academic work (Ye & Karali 2016, Energy Economics) finds the EIA effect is both larger and longer-lived than the private API report's effect the night before, and Bu (2014) finds the inventory-surprise effect on daily returns is a same-day phenomenon that doesn't show up as extra daily variance going forward. A same-day check against AskAmerica's own EIA stocks and WTI price data (2023-2025, n=155 report weeks) is consistent with this: release-day volatility is barely above an ordinary day's (1.60% vs. 1.53% average absolute daily move), the reaction is asymmetric (builds push price down noticeably more than draws push it up), and there is no meaningful reversal over the following two weeks -- if anything the move very mildly extends. Retail gasoline prices, by contrast, respond with a lag of weeks, not minutes, since they track wholesale/crude costs with a documented pass-through delay.

What moves, and by how much

Futures (WTI/Brent), intraday: The market trades the surprise vs. consensus, not the level. A bigger-than-expected build is bearish, a bigger-than-expected draw is bullish, and an in-line print often moves little. Reported magnitudes for a meaningful surprise run about 30 cents to $1 per barrel in the opening minutes, with trading volume spiking 300-500% in that window (OilDon; Damn Prop Firms trading-desk accounts corroborate the academic timing). Ederington, Fernando, Lin, Linn and colleagues, in a series of papers on EIA/API storage announcements, find the market pre-positions ahead of the Wednesday release using analyst forecasts and API's Tuesday-evening number, so much of the expected content is already in the price by 10:30am -- only the residual surprise moves it further.

EIA vs. API: Ye & Karali (2016, Energy Economics 59) directly compare the two weekly reports intraday and find unexpected inventory changes in both move returns inversely and volatility positively on impact, but "the duration and magnitude of EIA inventory shocks are longer and larger" than the API pre-report, with the biggest moves when API and the Reuters consensus both erred the same direction (i.e., the EIA number surprises everyone at once).

Asymmetry and regime dependence: Bu (2014, Energy Economics 46) finds the inventory-information-shock effect on returns is a next-to-immediate, same-day phenomenon, is stronger for negative shocks (builds), and "is weakened in rapid growth periods and disappears in steep fall markets" -- i.e. the effect is not a stable constant, it depends on the prevailing price regime.

How long the move lasts

Distinguish three windows the trading and academic literature both point to:

  • 0-60 seconds: algorithmic/headline-driven initial spike.
  • 5-30 minutes: "smart money" repricing completes; this is where the academic literature places essentially all of the measurable volatility and volume response.
  • Rest of the day and beyond: the size of the move that survives to the close is set mostly by whether the surprise was genuinely informative (a real change in the supply/demand balance) versus noise, and by what else happens that day -- OPEC headlines, macro data, geopolitical news routinely swamp a single Wednesday print.

None of the papers surveyed here find a systematic multi-day fade back to the pre-report price -- the report either reflects new information that sticks, or it didn't move much to begin with. That is exactly what the AskAmerica check below finds: a slightly negative but small correlation between the release-day move and the move over the following 1 to 10 trading days (-0.08 to -0.19), i.e. no strong reversal, and if anything on build weeks the price kept drifting in the same direction over the following week.

AskAmerica daily-close check (2023-2025, n=155 report weeks)

Using energy.eia_petroleum_stocks (series WCESTUS1, U.S. commercial crude stocks ex-SPR) and econ.global_conditions (WTI daily close), matched to each Wednesday release date (report_date + 5 calendar days):

  • Average absolute close-to-close price move on a release Wednesday: 1.60%, barely above the average absolute move on any trading day in the sample (1.53%, n=745 days). This is expected -- daily closes wash out an intraday burst that the literature places in the first 20-30 minutes, so a same-day close-to-close comparison understates the true event effect.
  • Sign concordance (build coinciding with a price decline, or draw with a price rise) was only 53% of weeks -- close to a coin flip. This is consistent with, not contrary to, the literature: it is the surprise vs. analyst consensus that moves price, not the realized change itself, and this connector has no analyst-consensus series to net that out against.
  • The relationship is asymmetric: build weeks (n=76) averaged a -$0.30/bbl move on the release day, widening to -$0.69/bbl relative to the pre-report price five trading days later. Draw weeks (n=79) averaged essentially zero effect (+$0.01, then +$0.04) at the close. This lines up with Bu (2014)'s finding that the effect is stronger for negative (build) shocks.
  • No mean-reversion: correlating the release-day move against the subsequent move (day+1 through day+10) gives small negative correlations (-0.08 to -0.19) -- a mild continuation/no-reversal pattern, not the sharp round-trip a pure noise-trading spike would produce.

Caveat: this is a realized-change proxy computed from close-to-close daily prices, not the intraday, consensus-surprise event study the cited papers run. It is offered as a same-day sanity check on direction and persistence, not as a replacement for the higher-frequency academic estimates of the 30 cent-to-$1 opening-minutes move.

Retail gasoline: a much slower, separate channel

The weekly WPSR also carries retail gasoline and diesel prices (surveyed Mondays, published with the report), but retail pump prices do not react to the Wednesday inventory print the way futures do. Pump prices track crude with a lag of roughly two to three weeks plus a seasonal refining-margin adjustment, reflecting the time it takes wholesale/rack price changes to work through distribution and station-level repricing. A single week's inventory surprise is a minor input to that slower-moving series, not a Wednesday-morning event for consumers.
