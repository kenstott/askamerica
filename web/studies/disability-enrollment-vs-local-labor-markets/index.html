How much do disability rolls rise when the jobs in an area disappear?

Report: http://127.0.0.1:57210/a/a4e3fbccbcc3288a9a76a0badf5f9836.html (local AskAmerica-published page; link is for a human reader on this machine — do not fetch it)

Dashboard image: dashboard.png

Answer

In the classic literature, yes, but only for permanent local job losses, and the effect is moderate, not dramatic:

  • Black, Daniel & Sanders (2002, AER) — natural experiment on the 1970s Appalachian coal boom and 1980s coal bust. Elasticity of DI payments with respect to local earnings: -0.3 to -0.4; SSI: -0.4 to -0.7. Critically, the relationship is much stronger for permanent than transitory shocks — people only apply for disability when the local decline looks lasting, because qualifying is costly.
  • Autor & Duggan (2003, QJE) — after the 1984 liberalization of DI medical-eligibility screening, DI application/recipiency became 2-3x as responsive to a given local labor-demand shock as it had been before. The mechanism is real, but its strength is policy-regime-dependent, not a fixed constant.
  • Later work (manufacturing-decline and trade-shock literature; an Amazon-fulfillment-center natural experiment) confirms the sign in both directions: local job loss raises SSDI applications, and one recent study found a local job-market IMPROVEMENT (an FC opening) cut SSDI applications by about 1.4% over 3 years.

What I added by re-running this on this corpus's own data: I recomputed the same design — county employment growth vs. change in disabled-worker beneficiary rate — on the most recent complete county-level data available here (SSA disabled_workers beneficiaries 2019-2023 joined to BLS QCEW county employment and ACS population, counties >20k population, n≈1,879-2,500 depending on spec). It did not replicate the classic negative relationship:

  • Two-way fixed-effects panel (county + year FE, contemporaneous or 2-year-lagged employment growth): coefficient ≈ 0 and not significant (p=0.13 and p=0.93). Year FE absorbs the one huge shock in this window (COVID), which hit almost every county at once, so little independent local variation is left to identify an effect.
  • Cross-sectional long-difference 2019→2023, controlling for each county's 2019 baseline DI rate and log population: coefficient on employment growth = +0.011 per 1,000 population per 1pp of growth (p=0.022, R²=0.63, n=1,879) — wrong-signed relative to the hypothesis, though economically tiny. Quantile-binning confirms a genuine (if weak) monotonic trend: the worst-job-loss quintile of counties (avg -5.7% employment) saw disabled-worker rates fall by 4.0/1,000, while the best-job-growth quintile (avg +12.1%) fell by only 3.1/1,000. Leave-one-state-out sensitivity: no sign flip across 50 refits — this isn't one outlier state.

Why the sign flips in this window: national SSDI disabled-worker rolls fell every single year from 2017 to 2023 (8.67M → 7.34M, -15%), straight through the COVID employment shock — an aging-into-retirement wave, tighter screening, and a very tight 2021-2023 labor market pulling in marginal workers. Counties that started 2019 with the highest baseline DI rates (typically older, slower-growing, more rural) saw the fastest declines regardless of their job trend (baseline-rate coefficient -0.146, p<0.001) — a mean-reversion/composition effect that swamps the local-shock signal this design is built to detect.

Bottom line: the mechanism in the question is real and well-established for permanent, geographically concentrated local demand shocks (coal-region decline, multi-year trade shocks) — elasticities around -0.3 to -0.7. But 2019-2023 — the only county-grain SSA window this corpus carries — is dominated by one transitory, nationwide shock plus a preexisting secular DI decline, and is a poor natural experiment for the question as classically posed. This corpus's SSA table only reaches back to 2017, so the 1980s coal-bust or 1999-2011 China-shock eras that produced the strong published estimates cannot be replicated here; that would need an older SSA county series or a manufacturing-specific (not total) employment shock at commuting-zone grain.
