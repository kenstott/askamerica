Disability rolls rise roughly 0.2-0.6 percent for every 1 percent drop in local earnings when an area's jobs disappear

Elasticities from quasi-experimental local-labor-demand studies (coal boom/bust, oil price shocks, trade shock, Amazon openings); AskAmerica corpus has no direct SSA disability-caseload table, so a warehouse extension used a proxy and is reported separately

Summary

The academic literature that isolates local job-disappearance shocks (rather than the national business cycle) puts the effect at roughly a 0.16 to 0.55 percent rise in disability-program participation for every 1 percent drop in local earnings — i.e., an elasticity of about -0.16 to -0.55, varying by program (SSDI vs. the means-tested SSI) and by the type of shock. A 10 percent area-wide earnings decline (a plausible size for a plant closure or a coal-bust-scale shock) therefore predicts roughly a 2-6 percent rise in the disability rolls in that area. The effect is real, causal (not just correlated with the business cycle), and strongest where the job loss is permanent rather than a temporary downturn. Separately, in the most trade-exposed U.S. commuting zones during the 1990-2007 China-import shock, the resulting per-capita rise in SSDI payments was more than 30 times the per-capita rise in Trade Adjustment Assistance — disability insurance, not the program built for displaced trade workers, absorbed the bulk of the transfer response. AskAmerica's own data warehouse has no direct SSA/SSDI administrative caseload table; an extension using the closest available proxy (ACS self-reported disability prevalence against county-level BLS employment) returned a correctly-signed but statistically insignificant result, which reflects the proxy's limits rather than contradicting the literature (detail below).

1. The core estimates, and why they are credible

Black, Daniel & Sanders (2002, American Economic Review) use the 1970s Appalachian coal boom and 1980s coal bust as a natural experiment in local labor demand for low-skill men. They find the elasticity of DI participation to local earnings is -0.3 to -0.4, and for SSI (the means-tested program, which responds to household income more directly) -0.4 to -0.7. Critically, they show the relationship is much stronger for permanent shocks than transitory ones — a short-lived layoff does not move the disability rolls the way a structural collapse of an area's main industry does.

Charles, Li & Stephens (2018, Review of Economics and Statistics) use county-level swings in world oil and gas prices (a shock to local earnings that is plausibly exogenous to local health or disability-screening policy) and find a more contemporary, somewhat smaller elasticity: -0.29 for SSDI and -0.16 for SSI. The smaller magnitude relative to Black-Daniel-Sanders may reflect tighter disability screening standards adopted since the 1980s, or that oil/gas price swings, while sharp, are less obviously permanent than a multi-decade industry collapse.

Autor & Duggan (2003, Quarterly Journal of Economics) is the foundational paper connecting declining labor demand for less-skilled workers to the national rise in the DI rolls (which grew 60 percent among nonelderly adults from 1984 to 2001, to 5.3 million beneficiaries). They estimate that declining demand for less-skilled labor, combined with loosened DI screening and a rising earnings-replacement rate, roughly doubled the propensity of displaced high-school dropouts to exit the labor force onto disability after 1984 — large enough to have lowered the measured national unemployment rate by about half a percentage point, because DI recipients are not counted as unemployed.

Autor, Dorn & Hanson (2013, American Economic Review, “The China Syndrome”) study commuting zones exposed to rising Chinese import competition 1990-2007, using an instrument (Chinese import growth in other high-income countries) to isolate the causal, exogenous component of the shock. Exposed zones saw higher unemployment, lower labor-force participation, and lower wages; transfer payments for unemployment, disability, retirement and healthcare all rose sharply. The single most striking number: the per-capita increase in SSDI payments was more than 30 times the per-capita increase in Trade Adjustment Assistance payments in the same places — disability insurance, a program with no formal connection to trade policy, did far more of the safety-net work than the program Congress built specifically for displaced trade workers.

A reverse-direction corroboration: a PLOS ONE study of Amazon fulfillment-center openings (a positive local labor-demand shock) found SSDI application rates fell about 1.4 percent in the three years after a center opened nearby — consistent with the same mechanism running in the opposite direction when jobs appear rather than disappear.

2. What this corpus could and could not verify directly

AskAmerica's search_catalog returned no table for “SSDI” or “OASDI” — there is no SSA administrative disability-caseload table (applications, awards, or beneficiaries by county) in this warehouse. That is a real coverage gap, not an oversight in the search.

As the closest available proxy, I built a county-year panel joining census.acs_disability (ACS 5-year self-reported disability prevalence, 2019-2023) to econ.county_qcew (BLS county total employment, same years), and ran a two-way fixed-effects panel regression of the disability rate on year-over-year employment growth, clustering standard errors by county. The joined panel held 16,083 county-year rows across 3,221 counties; computing a year-over-year employment-growth rate requires a prior year, so each county's first available year (2019) has no growth figure and was dropped — 3,221 rows excluded, exactly one per county, leaving n = 12,862 county-years actually used in the regression. This exclusion is mechanical (there is no earlier QCEW year in the join to compute growth from) rather than a modelling choice that could bias the result, so no alternate specification is reported.

The coefficient was correctly signed (-0.012: faster job growth is associated with a lower disability rate) but not statistically significant (p = 0.23), and the magnitude is far smaller than the literature's elasticities.

This null result should not be read as contradicting Black-Daniel-Sanders or Charles-Li-Stephens. Two things blunt this proxy: first, the ACS disability question measures self-reported health-based disability status in the population at large, not SSDI/SSI program enrollment — most people with a disability never apply for or receive disability benefits, and the causal channel in the literature runs through the decision to apply for or be awarded benefits, not through population health itself. Second, ACS 5-year estimates are rolling averages that share four of five underlying survey years with the prior year's estimate, which mechanically smooths out most of the year-to-year variation this design needs to detect a labor-demand shock. The honest conclusion is that this corpus can describe the disability-prevalence side of the story but cannot independently replicate the SSDI-caseload elasticity the literature reports; the quantitative answer above rests on the published quasi-experimental estimates, not on a warehouse computation.

3. Bottom line

When the jobs in an area disappear permanently — a coal-industry collapse, a sustained oil-price bust, a factory closure driven by import competition — local disability-program rolls rise by roughly 0.2 to 0.6 percent for every 1 percent drop in local earnings, with SSI (means-tested) generally more responsive than SSDI (earnings-based), and permanent shocks producing much larger responses than temporary downturns. In the hardest-hit trade-shock regions, disability insurance became the single largest channel of federal transfer response to the job loss, far outweighing programs designed explicitly for that purpose.
